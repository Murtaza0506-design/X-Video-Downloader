# Chapter 10 — Winter

The first winter took four people out of this valley. The second took nineteen. There was no attack in the second winter, the harvest had been better, and the weather was milder by any measure you like.

That is the fact that organises this chapter, and it took me most of a year to understand it. Winter does not kill people in December. It kills them in February, and it does it to households that came through the autumn in good order, and the mechanism is not cold. It is accumulation. Everybody is a little wetter than they should be, has been for eleven weeks. Everybody is sleeping badly in a cold room. Nobody has eaten fat since November. The wood is green because it was cut in October by people who were busy. Small jobs get postponed because it is raining, and then it is always raining. By the second week of February a household has fifty postponed jobs and no capacity. One ordinary problem arrives and there is nothing left to meet it with.

The people who died in the second winter did not die of the winter. They died of the accumulated cost of getting through it badly, and every one of those costs was set in September by somebody with time on their hands.

So this chapter is mostly about autumn. That is the trick of it. Almost nothing in here can be done in January, and almost all of it is easy in October, when the danger feels remote and the days are still long enough to work in. Read it in September. If you are reading it in January, read the last two and let the rest go until spring.

---

> **Wet kills. Cold only helps it.** — Deniz Aksoy

**What it buys you.** The correct enemy. A dry person in bad clothes at freezing will be uncomfortable for a long time and will live. A wet person in good clothes at eight degrees is on a clock, and eight degrees and raining is the ordinary weather here for five months. Everything about a winter routine should be built around getting dry and staying dry: a drying place, a spare set, and a hard rule about changing.

**What ignoring it costs.** People will work all day in the wet because they are not cold while they are working, and the bill arrives when they stop. Deniz put the drying rack in the kitchen at Bracewell and made it the most valuable square yard in the building. He made changing out of wet clothes a rule and not a choice. The person who most needs to change is always the one who says they are fine.

---

> **Heat the person, then the bed, then the room. Never the house.** — Samuel Oyelaran

**What it buys you.** A winter's fuel that lasts a winter. Warm clothes and a hat indoors, a hot stone or a bottle in the bed an hour before it is used, one room with the door shut, and everything else left to the weather. Sam reckoned the difference between heating a room and heating a farmhouse was a factor of six in wood, which is the difference between cutting in October and cutting in January.

**What ignoring it costs.** You burn the whole store by the middle of February trying to make a stone building comfortable, which it will not become. Then you are cold in the worst month with nothing left and a green woodpile. The households that were warm in the second winter were not the ones with the most wood. They were the ones living in one room and wearing coats in it.

---

> **Cut your wood a year before you burn it.** — Tom Shackleton

**What it buys you.** Twice the heat and a quarter of the smoke, which is chapter three's problem solved as a side effect. Green wood has half its weight in water and burns cold, dirty and slow, and it lines a chimney with the thing that later burns the house down. A year under cover, split, off the ground. Tom worked two winters ahead and always had.

**What ignoring it costs.** A cold February with a full woodpile, which is the most demoralising thing I have watched a household go through. They did the work. They cut, hauled, split and stacked four tons, and it will not burn properly, and there is no fixing it now. This is the purest example in the book of a rule about time rather than effort. You cannot work harder in January to undo not having cut in the previous January.

---

> **Two pairs of dry socks are a survival stock.** — Aiden Kerr

**What it buys you.** Feet you can still walk on in March. Wool, changed daily, dried by the fire overnight, with the wet pair going inside your coat during the day if there is no fire. Aiden had four pairs and treated them the way other people treated the medicine box, and he walked more miles than anybody in this valley and never had a foot problem.

**What ignoring it costs.** Trench foot, which nobody expects and which took three people out of action for weeks in the second winter, and one of them permanently. It comes from days of damp rather than from cold, and it arrives without pain until it is well established. Feet, hands and head. Everything else about winter clothing is negotiable and those three are not.

---

> **Sleep small and sleep together.** — Ruth Cadogan

**What it buys you.** The eight hours a day when you are producing no heat and losing it fastest. A small room, a low ceiling or a canopy over the bed, bodies together, straw or bracken under the mattress rather than a cold floor beneath it. Ruth put nine people in two rooms at Bracewell and there was some grumbling about it in the first November and none at all by January.

**What ignoring it costs.** A household that is cold all night for five months does not recover during the day. It shows up as illness, accidents, short tempers and a general slowness that everybody blames on the diet. This is also the cheapest of all the winter rules. It costs nothing at all except the idea that adults are entitled to their own rooms, which is a thing worth having and not worth a winter.

---

> **Do the outside jobs in November.** — the Gisburn stock book

**What it buys you.** A January you can survive on the inside. Roof, gutters, drains, gate, wall gaps, the path to the store, the woodshed door, and the state of the boots, all done while there is still light and the ground is workable. The stock book has a November list four pages long and a January list with six items on it, and the discipline is in the difference.

**What ignoring it costs.** Every one of those jobs done in the wet, in the dark, badly, by somebody who is already cold, at the exact moment when the failure of any of them is most expensive. A blocked drain in November is a morning's work. In February it is a flooded store, a fortnight of damp and a household eating wet flour.

---

> **Nobody travels alone between December and March.** — Elin Pryce

**What it buys you.** Somebody to fetch help. Winter travel here is not dangerous because of what is on the road. It is dangerous because a turned ankle four miles out at three in the afternoon is a night outside. A night outside in January is not survivable in the clothes people actually wear. Elin lost a colleague that way in the first year on a journey of five miles that she had made forty times.

**What ignoring it costs.** The whole margin. A pair can carry an injured person, keep them warm, or leave one and go, and any of the three is a survivable afternoon. Alone, all of it is the same afternoon. The rule has one exception and it is not the one people reach for: a short familiar journey in daylight with somebody at both ends who knows your timing and will come looking. Being expected is the actual protection.

---

> **Watch for whoever has gone quiet.** — Hanna Volk

**What it buys you.** The person you are about to lose to something that is not cold. February in a dark house with the same eleven people is when the grief that everybody has parked since the first spring comes back for its answer. Hanna's method was simple and I have never seen it fail: notice who has stopped talking, and go and sit next to them and do a job together without asking them anything.

**What ignoring it costs.** Two of the nineteen we lost in the second winter were not lost to the winter. Both had been quiet for a fortnight in a full house. Everyone said afterwards that they had noticed and had not wanted to intrude, which is the most understandable sentence in this book and the most expensive.

---

> **You are allowed to spend the winter doing nothing.** — Joyce Attwell

**What it buys you.** Spring, with people left in it. There is no useful outside work for a great deal of January and February in this country. A household that treats the whole winter as a productivity problem will grind through it and arrive at March exhausted, in the month when the year's actual work begins. Joyce's winter timetable had three hours of jobs in it and the rest was reading, teaching, sleeping, cards and talking.

**What ignoring it costs.** Everything that matters and nothing you can point at. Rest is not idleness and it is not a reward for having earned it. It is the thing that puts a household on its feet in the spring. The seasons in this country have been arranged so that there is a stretch in the middle where the only sensible thing to do is very little. Take it. The list will still be there.
