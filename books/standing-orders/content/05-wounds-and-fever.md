# Chapter 5 — Wounds and Fever

A cut on a hand. That is how most of them start.

Not the dramatic injuries. Those are rarer than you would think and they tend to resolve one way or the other within a day. What fills the ground behind the chapel at Whitewell is small wounds that were cleaned badly on a Tuesday and were hot by Friday, in people who were otherwise strong, in households that were otherwise careful. Marta Ellison used to say that the whole of modern medicine, stripped down to what a person in a farmhouse can actually do, comes to about four actions, and that the difference between doing them and not doing them is the difference between a scratch and a grave.

The four are these. Get it clean, with a lot of clean water and some force behind it. Cover it. Look at it twice a day and mark what you see so you are comparing against a record rather than a memory. And keep the whole person fed, watered and warm, because a body that is fighting something needs fuel and a body that is cold and hungry will lose an argument it would otherwise have won.

Everything else in this chapter is detail hung on those four, apart from one entry that is not about medicine at all. The sixth rule concerns what a household does with somebody who has been bitten, and it is here because more people have been killed by the answer to that question than by the question itself. I have put it in the middle of the chapter on purpose. It belongs with the other injuries.

Bibi Farooq is the second voice here. She kept the only drug stock in the valley, rationed it for two years, and she is the reason we still have anything at all.

---

> **Every wound is dirty. Treat it as dirty.** — Marta Ellison

**What it buys you.** The right starting assumption, which does more than any technique. Clean water, a lot of it, poured with height and force rather than dabbed. Get out the grit, the wool, the thread, the fragment of gate. Marta's line was that irrigation is ninety per cent of wound care and that everybody wants to skip to the interesting part, which is the dressing, because the dressing feels like treatment and washing feels like tidying.

**What ignoring it costs.** A closed wound with dirt under it, which is a warm dark container for the thing that will kill the person. It will look fine for two days. That is the cruel part and the reason the rule has to be an assumption rather than a judgement, because on the day you decide a wound looks clean enough you will be wrong at a rate of about one in twenty, and one in twenty over three years is everybody you know.

---

> **Clean water and pressure beat every ointment in the house.** — Bibi Farooq

**What it buys you.** A correct order of operations when your instinct is reaching for a bottle. Bleeding stops with firm direct pressure held for ten minutes by a clock and not by feel, and almost nobody holds it long enough. After that, water. The tube of antiseptic cream in the drawer is close to irrelevant, and Bibi was blunt about honey, spirits, iodine and every other favourite: they are arguments about the last five per cent.

**What ignoring it costs.** Time spent on the wrong thing at the only moment when the right thing works. She watched a man lose a hand over a fortnight that started with a farm injury and a cupboard full of remedies applied one after another to a wound nobody had washed. Hold pressure. Then wash it properly, with more water than seems reasonable. Then dress it and leave it alone.

---

> **Draw a line round the redness and write the time on the skin.** — Marta Ellison

**What it buys you.** Evidence instead of impression. A pen line at the edge of the red, with the hour beside it, turns a vague worry into a measurement, and it means the person who looks at four in the afternoon is comparing against a fact rather than against what they remember from breakfast. Spreading beyond the line, red tracking up the limb, heat, swelling, or a fever coming on: those are the signals, and the line is what makes them visible.

**What ignoring it costs.** Two days. Infection is a race and every treatment available to us works better early, so the entire value of watching is in noticing sooner. Households without the line always report the same thing afterwards, which is that it seemed to happen overnight. It never happens overnight. It happens over four days in front of people who have no way to compare Tuesday with Thursday.

---

> **Antibiotics are a course or they are nothing.** — Bibi Farooq

**What it buys you.** A stock that still works in a year. Whatever you have, use it when it is needed and then use all of it, at the intervals, to the end, even after the person feels better on day three. Bibi kept hers behind a locked door and made every request go through a conversation, and she refused far more often than she agreed.

**What ignoring it costs.** Half a course clears the easy part of an infection and educates the rest, and the person is ill again in a fortnight with something that no longer answers to the only drug in the valley. That is not a private decision. It spends a resource that belongs to everybody, and there is no more of it coming. The other cost is hoarding: tablets kept in a damp drawer for the day they are needed are usually past use by the time somebody is dying in front of you.

---

> **Teeth will take more of you than anything with teeth.** — Elin Pryce

**What it buys you.** Attention to the thing that is coming for everybody and is entirely preventable. Brush with a stick and ash or salt if there is no paste, and keep doing it every day of the winter when nobody can see you and it feels absurd. A tooth abscess is a serious infection nine inches from the brain, in a place where nobody can treat it, and it arrives on a schedule dictated by two years of neglect.

**What ignoring it costs.** Elin had pulled teeth from cattle and ended up pulling them from people, without anaesthetic, on a kitchen table, four times in the second year. She described it as the worst thing she has done and said that every one of the four was avoidable eighteen months earlier by a person spending ninety seconds a day on something they could not see the point of. Sugar and neglect. That is the whole mechanism.

---

> **A bite is not a diagnosis, and it is never yours to pass sentence on.** — the Whitewell list

**What it buys you.** A household that does not turn on itself in its worst hour. The rule as written has three parts: the bitten person is nursed and not restrained; they are asked what they want while they are still able to say it; and no decision about them is made by one person alone. The Whitewell list is the only document in this book that was written by a committee, and it took them nine hours.

**What ignoring it costs.** I could fill the chapter. Two brothers on the Bolton road, one of whom was bitten by a dog. A woman locked in an outbuilding for six days over an injury nobody could identify who died of dehydration in there while the household argued about her. Fear does the sentencing, and fear is always in a hurry, and it is always certain. Whatever you believe about what a bite means, the household that decides in an hour, without the person's voice in it, will get it wrong more often than the household that waits and watches. And getting it wrong here is not a mistake you can put down afterwards.

---

> **Fever is a clock. Start it at the first shiver.** — Bibi Farooq

**What it buys you.** A record, and a threshold you have agreed on in advance. Write the time of the first rigour, then the state every four hours on paper: hot or cold, drinking or not, talking sense or not, passing water or not. That last one is the most useful sign available without equipment, and it is the one everybody forgets to ask about.

**What ignoring it costs.** Decisions made from panic at three in the morning about a person who has actually been improving since midnight, or the reverse, which is worse. Bibi would not release anything from the store without the paper, and she was thought hard for it. The paper meant that the household asking her had already looked properly, and it meant she could tell the difference between a fever that was going somewhere and one that was not.

---

> **Whoever is nursing eats and sleeps first.** — Ruth Cadogan

**What it buys you.** A second week of care. Nursing is a job of days and it is done at night, and the person doing it stops eating without noticing because there is always something else to do. Ruth ran it as a rota with hard reliefs and sent people to bed when their shift ended whether or not they wanted to go. It sounds cold. It is the reason the sick at Bracewell were still being turned at the end of a fortnight.

**What ignoring it costs.** The nurse goes down, and now you have two patients and nobody who knows the history. The households that lost the most people in the second winter were not the ones with the most illness. They were the ones where one person, almost always the same person, tried to hold all of it alone and was on the floor by the ninth day.

---

> **You are allowed to stop treating and start comforting.** — Hanna Volk

**What it buys you.** A better death, which is a real thing to be able to give somebody and which most of us have forgotten is a choice at all. Warmth, water, clean bedding, light, company, and somebody holding a hand: those are all available to us. Hanna sat with eleven people and said afterwards that she had never once regretted stopping and had regretted continuing three times.

**What ignoring it costs.** Days of pain spent on interventions that were not going to work, and a family that spends its last hours with somebody arguing about a treatment instead of talking to them. There is a moment when the honest answer is that this person is dying, and everything after it is about how. Recognising the moment is not giving up and it is not something to be ashamed of. This is the exit from this chapter, and it is the one I have needed most.
