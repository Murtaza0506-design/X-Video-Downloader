# Introduction

This is a list of ninety-nine rules. I invented none of them. Every one was paid for by somebody, and where I know who paid, I have put the name underneath.

It began as a sheet of paper nailed to the door of the milking parlour at Hillfoot, in the first spring, with seven rules on it in a hand that was not mine. Boil it. Don't shout. Two doors. I do not know who wrote them and I have never met anybody who did. The sheet is gone now, along with the parlour and most of the people who walked past it every morning without reading it, but the seven are all still in here, three of them word for word, and not one of them has needed correcting in three years.

Each entry is built the same way. The rule, in as few words as it can be carried in. Underneath it, whoever it came from. Then two paragraphs: what keeping it buys you, and what ignoring it costs.

The second paragraph is the reason for the book. A rule with only the first half is a slogan, and slogans get you through about a fortnight. Anybody can tell you to boil your water. What makes a person actually do it, at two in the morning, in the rain, when the stove is outside and they are already ill, is knowing precisely what the alternative looks like on the fourth day, in a room with no plumbing, in front of their children. I have written those paragraphs plainly and without softening them. Skip them if you like. They are what the rest of the book is standing on.

You will find that the rules contradict each other. Rule eighteen tells you that staying is a decision, and that a man of seventy with bad hips should never take the road. Rule sixty-three tells you that the place is worth less than the people in it, and that you must be ready to walk out of three years of work in ten minutes. Both are true. They were written by different people, in different country, in different years of this, and each of them was right about the ground they were standing on. Where two rules disagree I have left them disagreeing and said so underneath. A list that never argues with itself has been tidied until it is useless, and I would rather hand you the argument than the tidy version.

The names are real to me and will mean nothing to you. Marta Ellison was a district nurse out of Settle who taught half the valley to clean a wound properly and then died of something a hospital would have shrugged off. The Bracewell standing orders were nine lines of gloss paint on the side of a grain store, and whoever painted them understood people better than they understood the dead. The Whitewell list came off a school noticeboard with the drawing pins still in it. Some of the sources in the index are camps, some are documents, and two of them are single sentences shouted across a river by somebody I never saw again. The index at the back tells you which is which.

There is nothing in here about how it started. I do not know how it started, and neither does anybody I have met whose word I would take on the weather.

The chapters are arranged by the trouble you are in, not by the order things happened. Nobody comes to a book like this at leisure. They come to it because there is a noise downstairs, or a wound that has gone hot, or a stranger at the gate with a child, and they need the nine pages that bear on that and none of the others. Chapter one is the first hour. Chapter eleven is three winters in. If you are somewhere in the middle, start at the chapter that names your problem and read the rest later, if there is a later.

One warning, and then I will get out of the way. A list like this turns dangerous at the point where people stop reading it as advice and start reciting it as law. I have watched that happen. It ends with somebody quoting rule forty at a woman who is asking for the last of the tablets for her husband, and the terrible thing is that the rule was correct and the room was still wrong. Chapter eleven is about when to break these, and it is the reason for the other ten.

Nine of these rules will not apply to your ground. I cannot tell you which nine. Learn the other ninety and you will work it out.
