# Chapter 9 — Living in a Group

Not one household in this valley has failed because of what was outside it. I have thought about that sentence for a long time and I cannot find an exception.

They failed over the rota. They failed because two people wanted the same thing and there was no way of settling it that both of them had agreed to in advance. They failed because a man was counting the stores alone and everybody knew it, and nobody said anything for four months, and then somebody said it badly at the wrong moment in front of the children. Bracewell held sixteen people for three years and it did that on the strength of nine lines of gloss paint, and the lines are not about the dead at all. They are about who decides, who counts, who cooks and what happens when somebody is impossible to live with.

That is the real work and nobody prepares for it. Everybody has thought about the fence. Nobody has thought about what to do with a competent, hard-working, frightened man who is quietly certain that he should be running the place, and there is one of him in every group of more than six people, and if you do not have a way of handling him then the way you handle him will be a shouting match in February.

Ruth Cadogan ran Bracewell without ever holding a title. Her view was that a group needs three things written down: who has the final word in an emergency, how food is decided, and how somebody leaves. She reckoned that groups which had settled all three could survive nearly anything and groups that had settled none of them would come apart in the first hard winter without any help from outside at all.

She was right. I have watched it seven times.

---

> **Write the rules down before you need them, and put them where they can be read.** — the Bracewell standing orders

**What it buys you.** Arguments about a document instead of arguments about a person. Once it is on a wall, disagreement is directed at nine lines of paint rather than at whoever is standing in the kitchen, and the thing can be amended in daylight by people who have eaten. It also means that everybody who joins the group learns the rules in the first hour rather than in their first row.

**What ignoring it costs.** Every dispute becomes personal and every precedent has to be argued from scratch. Unwritten rules always exist, they are just held differently by each person, and the discovery that they differ happens in the middle of a crisis. The nine lines took Bracewell an evening. Nothing else they did bought so much for so little.

---

> **Somebody has the final word in an emergency, and everybody agrees who before there is one.** — Deniz Aksoy

**What it buys you.** Ninety seconds, which is the whole of an emergency. It does not need to be the same person for everything, and Bracewell had three: one for anything outside the wall, one for anything medical, one for fire. Named, agreed, written up, and holding only until the thing is over.

**What ignoring it costs.** A committee in a yard at night with something coming up the lane. Deniz had seen it and described it as the worst thing he had ever watched: eleven capable adults, all of them right, none of them able to move, losing four minutes. The rule is not about hierarchy and it collapses if it becomes hierarchy. It is about a temporary, bounded authority that ends when the noise stops, and about everyone knowing which voice to obey without checking whose it is.

---

> **The person who holds the food does not also hold the rota.** — Ruth Cadogan

**What it buys you.** Trust that does not depend on anybody's character. Split the jobs that carry power: stores, work allocation, the medicine, the decision to trade. Different people, rotated on a known cycle, each of them counted in front of somebody else. Ruth's design assumed that everybody was decent and that being decent would not be enough.

**What ignoring it costs.** Suspicion, which is slower and more destructive than theft and does not need any theft to happen. A single person who decides who works and who eats will be believed to be favouring somebody within about two months, and they may well be, because they are a human being deciding those things about people they like unevenly. Do not ask anybody to be that good. Split it.

---

> **Everybody works, including the ones who cannot.** — Joyce Attwell

**What it buys you.** People who are still part of the group. The old, the injured, the ill and the very young all have work available to them: watching, sorting, mending, teaching, minding, peeling, keeping the log. Joyce had a woman of eighty-one who could not walk to the gate and who ran the entire seed inventory and taught four children to read.

**What ignoring it costs.** Two things at once. The group loses hours it needs. And the person loses their place in it, which happens fast, and after a fortnight of being carried they will start apologising for eating, and then they will start refusing food, and I have seen where that goes twice. Give everybody a job that genuinely matters and let them do it badly if that is how it gets done.

---

> **Say the bad news at breakfast.** — Hanna Volk

**What it buys you.** A group that can absorb it. Bad news delivered in the morning, to everybody at once, in plain words, gets a day of daylight and work to be metabolised in. The same news at nine at night sits in the dark with people and comes out as something else entirely by morning.

**What ignoring it costs.** Rumour, which is always worse than the fact and always arrives anyway. Hanna's rule came out of the second winter when a decision about the stores was taken quietly by three people who were trying to avoid frightening anybody, and it was known in an inaccurate form within a day and a half, and the version that circulated was so much worse than the truth that two families left. Tell them at breakfast. Tell them the actual number.

---

> **A grievance kept for a fortnight is no longer about the thing.** — the Settle school log

**What it buys you.** Small problems that stay small. There is an entry in the log, in a teacher's handwriting, listing three arguments and the number of days each had been running before it was raised, and the conclusion at the bottom is that nothing raised in the first two days had ever needed more than a conversation. Have a way of raising things that does not require courage: a weekly hour, a person whose job it is, anything.

**What ignoring it costs.** In a group of nine people who cannot leave, a fortnight is long enough for an irritation about washing up to become a settled belief about somebody's character, and once it is about character it cannot be resolved by fixing the washing up. Everything I have watched come apart came apart at that stage and not before.

---

> **Count the stores in front of two people, on a fixed day.** — Samuel Oyelaran

**What it buys you.** A number everybody believes, which is the foundation of every hard decision you are going to have to make together. First Sunday of the month, two people, written in the book, read out at breakfast. Sam argued for it not because he expected theft but because he expected disbelief, and disbelief is the thing that actually breaks a group.

**What ignoring it costs.** A household where everybody is privately estimating, and where the estimates diverge, and where the people who think there is less are hoarding and the people who think there is more are resentful of the rationing. Bracewell's count took twenty minutes a month. Sam's phrase for it was that the count is not an audit, it is a conversation everybody is allowed to be in.

---

> **Nobody watches alone for more than two hours.** — Tom Shackleton

**What it buys you.** A watch that is actually being kept. Attention collapses after about two hours in the cold and dark, and the person on watch does not notice it collapsing, which is the whole difficulty. Short shifts, a proper handover with something said out loud, and a warm place to go afterwards.

**What ignoring it costs.** Four hours of theatre. A household that runs long watches has people sitting in the dark achieving nothing, sleeping worse, working worse the next day, and providing a sense of security that is not connected to anything. Tom would rather have had passive warning on the approaches and everybody asleep than a man out for six hours, and after the second winter that is what he did.

---

> **You are allowed to leave.** — Aiden Kerr

**What it buys you.** The thing that makes all of the above voluntary. A group people cannot leave is a different kind of place, and it becomes that kind of place by degrees without anybody choosing it. Bracewell wrote down what a person takes with them when they go, which was their own gear, a fortnight of food and a share of the seed, and agreed it in a good month so that nobody had to negotiate it in a bad one.

**What ignoring it costs.** People stay in groups that are going wrong, and they stay for the reason that leaving means starving, and a group that knows its members cannot leave stops having to be worth staying in. Aiden left two places in his first year and both of them tried to keep him and one of them took his boots. Write down the terms of leaving. Then hope nobody uses them.
