# Chapter 11 — When to Break a Rule

Colm Reidy wrote three of the rules in this book. He was right about all three. He was right in a way that saved people who are still alive. He has been dead for fourteen months.

He died four miles from here keeping two rules that are printed in this book, in a barn, in October, with a fire he would not light because of chapter three. There was a house with people in it eight hundred yards away and he knew that. His leg had been broken since the afternoon. He had spent two years being right about things, in front of people. By the end he could not be the man who arrived somewhere at night, cold, needing help, having broken his own instruction. I do not think he decided to die. I think he could not find the version of himself that was allowed to.

So this chapter exists, and it is the shortest one, and I have thought about it more than the other ten put together.

A rule is a bet that the ground has not changed. Every line in this book was written by somebody standing in a particular field in a particular year with particular people, and it is right there. Carried far enough from where it was made, any of them turns into a way of dying with your principles intact. But the reverse is worse, and it is much more common. A person who breaks rules whenever the situation feels exceptional has no rules at all, and every situation feels exceptional at the time. That is what makes this hard. Both failures are real, and there is no formula.

What follows is the nearest thing I have to one. It is a set of conditions for breaking a rule rather than a list of the ones to break, and the last of them is the last thing in the book.

---

> **Know which rule you are breaking, and say it out loud first.** — Ruth Cadogan

**What it buys you.** The difference between a decision and a drift. Naming it, aloud, to another person, in the moment, takes four seconds and converts an impulse into a choice somebody else has heard. We are lighting a fire in the open. We are going out after dark. Ruth's household did it dozens of times and the saying of it was the whole discipline.

**What ignoring it costs.** Rules do not get broken. They get eroded, one reasonable exception at a time, until the household is doing something it would never have agreed to do and cannot remember deciding. Nobody at the mill ever voted to stop keeping watch.

---

> **Break a rule for a person. Never for a thing.** — Marta Ellison

**What it buys you.** A test you can apply in twenty seconds under pressure, and the only one in this chapter that has never let me down. Going out at night for somebody who is hurt is a rule worth breaking. Going out at night for a store of flour is not, and it will feel identical while you are pulling your boots on.

**What ignoring it costs.** Almost everybody who has died breaking a rule in this valley died going back for an object. Fuel, tools, a generator, a box of tins. Every one of those journeys was justified at the time by arithmetic that was correct. The flour is still there. So is the man who went for it.

---

> **A rule you learned in the first winter is about the first winter.** — the Gisburn stock book

**What it buys you.** Rules that keep up with the world. The valley of the first year had cars in it, open shops, a functioning road east and eleven times as many people. Half the instructions that were correct then are now costing somebody a morning's work for nothing. The stock book has whole pages crossed out with a date and a note beside them.

**What ignoring it costs.** Effort spent defending against a year that has finished. Review the list once a year, in the quiet part of the winter. Ask of each rule what it is protecting you from, and whether that thing is still out there.

---

> **Two of you have to agree. One of you can always keep it.** — Deniz Aksoy

**What it buys you.** A brake that does not depend on being the calmest person in the room. Any rule in this book can be broken by a household that agrees to break it, and any single member can refuse and the thing does not happen. It sounds slow. It takes about a minute and it has stopped more bad journeys here than any other arrangement.

**What ignoring it costs.** The person who is most frightened, most tired or most certain makes the decision, and those three are usually the same person, and they are always the one moving first. Deniz's addition was that the one who refuses does not have to explain, because the requirement to give reasons is how a refusal gets talked out of.

---

> **Distrust anybody who has never been wrong.** — the Bracewell standing orders

**What it buys you.** Protection from the most dangerous person in a group, who is not the reckless one. It is the competent one with a good record, whose judgement everybody has learned to defer to, and who has therefore stopped receiving any correction at all. Colm was that man. He earned it honestly.

**What ignoring it costs.** A group with one voice in it, which is a group with one point of failure. It is also unkind to the person, who ends up unable to be uncertain in front of anybody and then unable to arrive at a door at night asking for help. If you are the one being deferred to, be wrong out loud about something small, on purpose, in front of everybody, twice a year.

---

> **When the rule and the room disagree, believe the room.** — Aiden Kerr

**What it buys you.** Your own eyes back. The rules in this book are averages taken across three years and a whole district. You are standing in one place, looking at one particular thing that none of us has seen. Where the ground in front of you plainly contradicts the page, the ground is the better evidence. It knows about today.

**What ignoring it costs.** Aiden broke chapter eight's rules to bring two children in through the back field at dusk without meeting anybody at the meeting place first, and by every line of that chapter he was wrong. They are both still here. He also, in the same month, trusted a room over a rule and lost a coat, a bag and a fortnight of food. The room is better evidence. It is not good evidence.

---

> **Write down what you broke and what happened.** — Joyce Attwell

**What it buys you.** A book that improves. Every rule in here came from somebody keeping a record, and the exceptions are more informative than the rules. A rule that gets broken four times a year with no consequence is not a rule. It is a habit with a uniform on. Joyce kept the log at Whitewell in a school exercise book.

**What ignoring it costs.** The same argument every year and nothing learned from any of it. Half the entries in this book are in the shape they are because somebody broke them and wrote down why. Three were removed when the log made it clear they had never once been right.

---

> **Four of these are not for breaking.** — the Hillfoot parlour door

**What it buys you.** A floor. Everything in this book is negotiable except four things, and I have chosen them by looking at the ground behind the chapel and not by reasoning about it. Boil the water. Keep the second door. Talk from a distance before anybody moves. And no decision about a human being gets made by one person in a hurry.

**What ignoring it costs.** These are the four where the cost of being wrong lands on somebody who did not make the decision, and that is the whole basis for the list. Break any of the other ninety-five when the room tells you to. Break these when you have run out of ways of being a person, and not before.

---

> **Put this down and go and live.** — Nell Shackleton

**What it buys you.** The point of the exercise. Nell is nineteen now. She has read this list more carefully than anybody and she is scathing about it, and her objection is the correct one. It is a book about not dying, and there is nothing in it about anything else. She has a garden that is not a food garden. She is teaching two children to swim in a river that four of these rules would keep them out of.

**What ignoring it costs.** Ninety-eight rules will get a household through a winter. They will not give anybody a reason to want the spring. I have watched people learn all of this and keep every line of it, and become careful, and quiet, and unbeatable. By the third year there was nothing left inside the wall except the arrangements for staying alive. That is not survival. It is a slower version of the other thing. Learn the ninety-eight. Then close the book, because it is a list of ways not to die and you were not put here to spend your life reading one.
