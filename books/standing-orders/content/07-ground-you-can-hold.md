# Chapter 7 — Ground You Can Hold

Everybody's first instinct about a building is wrong, and it is wrong in the same direction, which is at least useful.

Asked to pick somewhere to live now, almost anybody chooses the strongest thing they can see. Thick walls, small windows, a heavy door, high up. They are picking a castle, because that is the shape the mind reaches for, and every one of those features answers a question nobody is asking. Nothing outside your walls is going to besiege you. What will happen instead, over three years, is this. You will carry water eight hundred times. You will get in and out at night. You will see somebody coming while they are still four fields away. And you will leave in a hurry once.

So the good ground in this valley is not the fortified ground. It is Hillfoot. A farm halfway up a slope, with a spring above it. One lane in, visible for half a mile. A back field that goes to open fell. Stone walls five feet high, because that is how walls are built here. A set of buildings arranged round a yard by people who were thinking about cattle. It has been holding for three years and it has never once been defended.

Deniz Aksoy's contribution is the phrase I keep coming back to, and he was talking about lorries and depots when he first said it. You are not building a fort. You are building a place that is boring to approach, easy to see out of, and quick to leave.

The last two rules in this chapter argue with each other on purpose. That is not sloppiness. It is the actual state of the question.

---

> **Two doors, always.** — the Hillfoot parlour door

**What it buys you.** The difference between a problem and an ending. Two ways out of every building you sleep in, two ways off every piece of ground, and both of them checked, in the dark, by everybody who lives there. Not a window you could theoretically get through. A route you have walked at night, in boots, carrying something.

**What ignoring it costs.** One door means one bad afternoon finishes you. This is the rule that appears in every survivor's account I have ever collected, and the one that costs least to keep. A back door unbolted. A ladder left up. A gap in a wall kept clear. Whatever else you do to a building, do not reduce it to a single exit in the name of making it safe. Every improvement that seals a place is also sealing you inside it.

---

> **Choose ground for its water first and its walls second.** — Samuel Oyelaran

**What it buys you.** A place that works for years rather than a place that survives a night. A spring or a beck above the buildings, so it comes to you and does not have to be carried up. Sam's ranking was water, then approach, then exits, then shelter, then walls, and he would not move on the first one. A perfect house that is four hundred yards from water is a house where somebody makes eight hundred trips a year in the open with both hands full.

**What ignoring it costs.** The slow bleed. Every trip is exposure, every trip is time, and the household adapts by fetching less, which means washing less and drinking less, and you are back in chapter four. Two families in the second year abandoned good stone buildings for worse ones with better water. Both said the same thing afterwards: they should have done it in the first month instead of the fourteenth.

---

> **A wall you cannot watch is a wall you do not have.** — Deniz Aksoy

**What it buys you.** An honest picture of your own perimeter. Walk it at three in the morning and ask, from the kitchen window, what you can see and what you would hear. A hundred yards of good stone wall behind a barn where nobody ever looks is not protecting you; it is telling you a story about being protected while somebody stands behind it.

**What ignoring it costs.** Effort spent in the wrong place. People build. It is satisfying, visible work that feels like progress, and households will spend a fortnight raising a barrier they cannot observe rather than an afternoon cutting the hedge that blinds them. Deniz's version was blunter than mine: you do not need a wall, you need to know. Watch first. Build only where watching has told you to.

---

> **Cut the sightlines before you build anything.** — the Bracewell standing orders

**What it buys you.** Distance, which is the only resource that matters when somebody arrives. Take out the hedge that hides the bend, the shed that blocks the view of the lane, the elder in the corner of the yard. Four hundred yards of warning is enough for a household to be inside, quiet and counting. Forty yards is enough to be surprised at.

**What ignoring it costs.** Every problem arrives at arm's length. There is a caution attached, and Bracewell learned it the hard way. Land you have cleared is also land you are visible on, and a farm stripped of every hedge announces itself as a farm somebody is holding. Cut what blinds you on your own approaches. Leave the screen that hides your roof from the ridge. It is a trade, not a principle, and the orders are wrong to state it as flatly as they do.

---

> **Upstairs is a trap with a view.** — Aiden Kerr

**What it buys you.** A correct use for the first floor, which is watching, and a correct place to sleep, which is not up there. Height is the best observation you have and the worst position you can be caught in. Aiden scavenged houses for two years and had a rule about never going above ground level in a building he had not first walked the outside of.

**What ignoring it costs.** A staircase is a corridor with one end. Everybody who has been trapped in a building in this valley was trapped upstairs, and in three of those cases the ground floor was clear and stayed clear the whole time. Use the height in the day. Sleep near a door. If you must sleep upstairs, put a rope on the window sill and leave it there, and check the knot in daylight now and again.

---

> **Make every route in noisy on purpose.** — Tom Shackleton

**What it buys you.** Warning that works while you are asleep, for the price of an afternoon. Gravel on the two paths people would actually use. A gate that is wired so it rattles. Tins on a line across the gap in the wall. Tom did it in the second month, and he did the thing most people miss. He left one silent route for his own household, so they were not setting off their own alarms four times a night.

**What ignoring it costs.** A watch rota that has to be kept by human beings, all night, in winter, for years. That is the alternative and it is not sustainable; watches degrade, people doze, and the third winter is when it happens. Passive warning does not get tired. It is also the only thing that protects you on the night everybody is ill.

---

> **Fire ends places. Plan for it before it is on you.** — the Settle school log

**What it buys you.** The one disaster that can finish a well-run household in twenty minutes. Water in the yard where it can be reached without going through the building. A break between the store and the house. Nothing burning left unattended, ever, and the stove out at night. The school log has eleven pages about a chimney and then stops.

**What ignoring it costs.** Everything you have built and everybody asleep in it. We are all burning wood indoors in buildings wired for a different century, with candles and lamps and no water above the ground floor, and we are doing it while exhausted. Fire is more likely to kill you now than it was before, by a long way. It receives the least attention in every household I have visited, including my own until the school burned.

---

> **Do not fortify what you cannot leave.** — Deniz Aksoy

**What it buys you.** A clear head about a thing people become sentimental about. Three years of work makes a place into an argument you cannot lose. Deniz tested every improvement against one question. Does this make it harder for us to walk out of here in ten minutes. Barred windows fail that test. A dug well passes it. Stored food in one cellar fails it, which is chapter six's third cache.

**What ignoring it costs.** You will stay when you should go, and you will not experience it as a mistake. You will experience it as loyalty to the place. Everybody who died at the mill died because the mill was worth defending, and it was, and they were right about that, and it did not matter.

---

> **The place is worth less than the people in it, and you will not believe that when it matters.** — Ruth Cadogan

**What it buys you.** Permission, decided in advance, on a calm day, in front of witnesses. Ruth made her household say it out loud once a year: this is a building, we are not going to die in it, and if we go the seed and the medicine and the children go first and the rest stays. Say it while nothing is happening. It will not be sayable later.

**What ignoring it costs.** This rule contradicts the one before it and the whole of chapter two, and I have left all three of them standing. Some ground is worth holding and Tom was right to hold it. But every household that lost people to a place lost them the same way, which is by making a decision to leave four hours after the decision was available. The building will still be there in a month. You can come back. Nobody has ever come back to a person.
