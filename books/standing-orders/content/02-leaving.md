# Chapter 2 — Leaving

The hardest decision in this book is the first one, and it is not a decision about danger. It is a decision about a house.

People do not stay too long because they are stupid or because they cannot see what is coming. They stay because leaving means admitting that the kitchen they painted is now a room in a country that no longer exists, and the mind will do a great deal of work to avoid admitting that. I watched a man wait four days past the point of sense so he could keep believing he might be back by the weekend. He was a careful man. He had thought about all of this in the abstract and had opinions about it. When it arrived he stood in his hallway and could not pick up the bag.

So the rules in this chapter are mostly not about routes and fuel. They are about deciding in advance, in writing, in cold blood, what fact will make you go, so that the going is a matter of following an instruction rather than making a choice while frightened. Ruth Cadogan wrote hers on the inside of a cupboard door in the first week: when the water stops, or when the second neighbour goes, whichever comes first. She left on a Thursday and did not have an argument with herself about it, because the argument had happened three weeks earlier with a pen.

The other half is weight. Everything you own will be carried, and the distance you will carry it is always further than the plan. Aiden Kerr was seventeen and made a habit of picking up other people's abandoned bags on the Slaidburn road and going through them. His account of what people take is the most useful survey I have. Kettles. Toolboxes. A microwave, once. All of it left in a hedge within four miles, because the leaving was done at the speed of panic and the walking was done at the speed of a person.

And then, at the end, the rule that says none of the above. Some people should stay. The chapter ends there because it has to.

---

> **Decide your trigger before you need it, and write it down.** — Ruth Cadogan

**What it buys you.** A decision made by the version of you that has slept. The trigger has to be a fact you can check and not a feeling: the mains go off, the second house on the row empties, you hear one in daylight. When the fact arrives you do not deliberate, you execute, and the whole terrible internal argument has already been settled weeks ago by somebody calmer wearing your clothes.

**What ignoring it costs.** You will make the biggest decision of your life while your hands are shaking, in a house full of things you love, with somebody asking you a question. Nobody does that well. The consistent pattern in the first month is not people leaving too early. It is people leaving four days late, on foot, having missed the window when the vehicle would still have got them there.

---

> **Leave in daylight or not at all.** — Deniz Aksoy

**What it buys you.** Sight, which is the entire game. In daylight you can see a blocked road from four hundred yards, read a junction, spot the group at the bridge before it spots you, and turn around with room to do it. Deniz drove lorries for nineteen years and his whole philosophy came down to one line: the dark does not hide you from them, it only hides them from you.

**What ignoring it costs.** Night driving after the first week means arriving at every problem at the moment you are closest to it. The road out of Bracewell was cut by a fallen tree at head height across a bend. In daylight it is a nuisance and you reverse. Four families hit it in the dark over two nights. If your trigger fires at nine at night, you lock the door and you go at first light. Waiting eight hours is not the same as waiting four days.

---

> **Everything you carry has to go six miles uphill.** — Aiden Kerr

**What it buys you.** An honest packing list. Load the bag, put it on, walk to the end of the lane and back with it, and it will tell you the truth in about ten minutes. What survives that test is water, food, a blade, cordage, a means of fire, a means of boiling, dry socks, a coat and a bag of medicine. Aiden had a rule for anybody hesitating over an object: if you would not carry it up the fell for somebody else, leave it.

**What ignoring it costs.** You will decide what to abandon while exhausted, on a road, in front of people who are watching you do it. That is how good equipment ends up in hedges and how people arrive somewhere with a full pack of the wrong things. The other cost is speed. A man carrying forty kilos walks at two miles an hour and cannot run at all, and there are afternoons when the ability to move quickly for ninety seconds is worth more than everything in the bag.

---

> **Take the map, not the phone.** — Joyce Attwell

**What it buys you.** Every lane, ford, footpath, farm track and contour line in the district, in your hands, in the rain, for years, without power. A paper sheet lets you plan around the roads everybody else is on. The ability to move by tracks that do not appear on a road atlas is the largest tactical advantage available to a person on foot in this country.

**What ignoring it costs.** Roads. You will move on roads because roads are what you can remember, and roads are where everybody else is. By the second week roads have people on them who are waiting for people like you. Joyce took eleven Ordnance Survey sheets out of the school cupboard on the first Friday and they have routed more journeys than anything else we own. Nobody has ever asked me for a phone.

---

> **Siphon on the way out, not on the way back.** — Tom Shackleton

**What it buys you.** Range while range still matters. In the first fortnight there is fuel in every driveway in the county and it is nobody's, and the tanks are full because people topped up in the panic and then never went anywhere. Take it as you pass. A jerry can filled on Tuesday is forty miles you can spend later on a decision you have not had to make yet.

**What ignoring it costs.** Petrol goes off. It is stale in six months and useless in a year, and the water in the bottom of an abandoned tank arrives at the same time. The people who thought of fuel as a thing they could come back for came back to varnish. There is a window of about three months when the whole county is a fuel depot, and it closes without any announcement.

---

> **A vehicle is a loud room with windows.** — Elin Pryce

**What it buys you.** The right relationship with the thing that feels safest. A van gets you eighty miles. Then it is a metal box that announces you for half a mile in every direction, cannot leave the road, and cannot be defended by the people inside it. Use it hard and early for distance, and stop thinking of it as shelter the moment you arrive.

**What ignoring it costs.** People sleep in cars because a car has doors and doors feel like a house. Elin found a family who had done it in a lay-by above Wigglesworth, and they had done everything else right. The windows are the problem. You cannot see out at night and everything can see in, and a car park is a place other people also think of as safe. Sleep in a building with two exits, or in a hedge, and leave the vehicle where it can be reached without walking into the open.

---

> **Have a second destination, and tell nobody outside the house what it is.** — Colm Reidy

**What it buys you.** Somewhere to go when the first place is burnt, occupied, or full. Half of all journeys in the first month failed on arrival, and the families who had a second name in their heads turned around inside an hour. The ones who did not stood in a yard arguing until dark, which is how people end up sleeping somewhere they have not chosen.

**What ignoring it costs.** Improvisation at the worst moment. The second half of the rule is harder and it is the half people break. A destination that four groups know about is a destination four groups will walk to when their own plan fails, and none of them will be arriving in a good mood. Tell your household. Tell whoever you would want to find you. Beyond that, say you are heading north.

---

> **Go back for the animals once.** — Elin Pryce

**What it buys you.** A decision you can live with, and a limit on it. Elin was a large animal vet and she understood better than anybody what it costs a person to walk away from stock in a shed. So the rule is not do not go back. It is go back once, in daylight, with somebody watching the road, and open every gate you can reach before you leave. Opened gates are the difference between a slow death and a chance.

**What ignoring it costs.** The second trip is where people die, and the third is where they die having achieved nothing. The pull increases rather than fades. Elin went back four times to a practice in Settle for drugs that were already gone by the second visit. She said afterwards that she had not been going for the drugs since the first time. Know what the journey is actually for. Then go once.

---

> **Staying is a decision too, and for some people it is the right one.** — Tom Shackleton

**What it buys you.** Ground you know in the dark. Tom never left Hillfoot and is the reason a good deal of this book exists. A farm with its own water, walls, stock and one approach is worth more than any journey. A man of seventy with bad hips will not survive a fortnight on the road. The road is not safety. It is a different set of risks that happen to be moving.

**What ignoring it costs.** Nothing, if you have chosen it. This rule exists to stop the rest of the chapter from becoming a command to run, because running is what people do when frightened and it is not always right. Weigh what you actually have: water, walls, exits, neighbours, and legs. If the answer is that your ground is better than the road, then say so out loud to the people in your house, and stop relitigating it every evening. Deciding to stay and then half staying is the worst of the three.
