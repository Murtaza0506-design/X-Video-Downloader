# Chapter 8 — Strangers

I have been on both sides of a gate and I am going to say the uncomfortable thing first, because every book of this kind gets it wrong in the same direction.

The people at the gate are almost never the danger. In three years I can count the genuinely predatory groups in this district on one hand, and four of the five were formed out of households that had been turned away from somewhere in the first winter. Nearly everybody who has ever stood at the end of our lane was what they appeared to be: a family, walking, with the wrong shoes on, asking whether we knew anything about the road east. The arithmetic is not close, and the reason it feels close is that the one bad group is the only one anybody tells stories about.

But the caution in this chapter is real, and it is not about the arithmetic. It is about the fact that being wrong once is different in kind from being wrong ninety times. The households that were destroyed by other people were not destroyed by strangers arriving. They were destroyed by strangers arriving in a way that gave the household no time and no room, at the gate, in the dark, with everything visible behind them.

So the rules here are almost all about geometry and sequence rather than judgement. Where you stand. How far away. Who speaks. What can be seen from where they are. What happens before a decision has to be made. Get the geometry right and you can afford to be generous, which is the actual aim, and the only households in this valley that are still worth living in are the generous ones.

The last rule is the reason for the chapter and it is Marta's.

---

> **Talk first, from a distance, with something solid between you.** — Deniz Aksoy

**What it buys you.** Time, and the ability to change your mind. Sixty yards, a wall, a raised voice, and a conversation that begins before anybody is committed. Almost every situation that goes wrong between two groups goes wrong inside fifteen feet, in the first ninety seconds, because both sides were surprised and both were frightened and somebody moved a hand.

**What ignoring it costs.** You will make a decision about eight people in the time it takes them to cross a yard. Deniz's whole method was to slow the encounter down: talk across a distance for five minutes about nothing, the weather, the road, where they have come from, and let both sides get bored. Boredom is the thing you are trying to manufacture. Nobody has ever been killed by a group they had already been bored with.

---

> **Never meet anybody at your own gate.** — the Bracewell standing orders

**What it buys you.** A meeting place of your choosing, which is worth more than any weapon. Bracewell used a bend in the lane three hundred yards out with a wall on one side, and every conversation with an outsider happened there. It shows nothing, commits nothing, and gives you a walk home during which you can talk it over.

**What ignoring it costs.** Everything you own becomes part of the negotiation. At your own gate they can see the roof, the stock, the store, the children, the number of you, and whether the men are older than they look, and all of that is now in the head of somebody you may never see again, or may see in four months with company. Meet away from the place. Always. Even for people you like.

---

> **Feed them outside the wall.** — Ruth Cadogan

**What it buys you.** The ability to be decent without being opened. Bread, hot water, a bag of potatoes, and directions, handed over at the meeting place, is a real kindness and costs you almost nothing, and it is what the great majority of people at your gate actually need. Ruth did it more than a hundred times and never once regretted it.

**What ignoring it costs.** The two failure modes are opposite and both are expensive. A household that gives nothing accumulates people who have nothing and know where you are, which is exactly how the group at the mill formed. A household that brings everybody in has lost the only thing that lets it keep giving. Between them there is a wide, workable middle, and Ruth lived in it for three years.

---

> **Believe the children.** — Joyce Attwell

**What it buys you.** The most reliable information available at a meeting. Adults arrive with a prepared account because they have learned they must. Children answer what you ask. How many nights have you been walking. Was there a house. Did the men come with you from there. Joyce would talk to the children while somebody else talked to the parents, and she said she had never had a false picture from doing it.

**What ignoring it costs.** You will take an adult's story at face value and it will be a story built to get them in, which is what a frightened parent should be doing and not a sign of bad faith. The rule has a limit that Joyce insisted on: this is for building a picture, not for cross-examining a nine year old about their family in front of them. Ask two gentle questions. That is usually the whole of it.

---

> **A group that will not give you a name will not give you anything else.** — Aiden Kerr

**What it buys you.** The single most useful signal at a distance. Names, where you have come from, where you are going, who is hurt. The great majority of people answer these, and answer them at once, because they have nothing to hide and a great deal to gain from being known. Aiden's line was that honest people over-explain and frightened people over-explain more.

**What ignoring it costs.** You will miss the small number of encounters that are actually dangerous, and they announce themselves early if you are listening for it: no names, questions in both directions but no answers, a count of you being taken, a story that adjusts to what you seem to want. That is a different thing from what a nervous family does, and once you have met it you will not confuse the two.

---

> **Trade at distance, in the open, in daylight, or not at all.** — the Whitewell list

**What it buys you.** Trade, which is worth having, and which almost stopped in the second winter because nobody had a safe shape for it. Neutral ground both parties can see across. Goods set down and stepped back from. Two people, not eight. And an agreement made the meeting before about what is coming, so that nobody is improvising.

**What ignoring it costs.** The trade that goes wrong is almost always the one arranged quickly, at dusk, at somebody's buildings, with more people present than were expected. Whitewell lost salt and a man in one afternoon by breaking three of the four conditions at once, and the list was written the following week by people who were arguing about whose fault it had been.

---

> **Nobody tells the same story twice unless they have rehearsed it.** — Hanna Volk

**What it buys you.** A quiet check that costs nothing. Ask about the route again an hour later, in a different order, and a true account will come back with different details and the same shape, because that is how memory works. A prepared one comes back word for word, or comes back different in the shape and the same in the words.

**What ignoring it costs.** Hanna was careful about this and I want to repeat her warning rather than her method. People lie for good reasons. A family that has been turned away from four places will have learned a story that works, and the fact that it is polished does not make them dangerous, it makes them experienced. The signal is worth having and it is not a verdict. Use it to ask a better question, not to close the gate.

---

> **Never let anybody see the whole of what you have.** — Tom Shackleton

**What it buys you.** A margin that survives your own generosity. Whatever is visible from the meeting place is what you are known to have, so the store is out of sight, the stock is in the back field on the days you are expecting anybody, and the third cache is a thing nobody outside the household has ever heard mentioned. Tom went further and kept a small obvious store that could be given away.

**What ignoring it costs.** You become a number in other people's plans. This is the rule that sounds least generous and is actually what makes generosity survivable, because a household that is known to be thin can afford to give a stranger bread, while a household that has been seen to be rich will be visited by the fifth group in a way the first four did not consider.

---

> **Take somebody in, or admit what you are becoming.** — Marta Ellison

**What it buys you.** A household you would want to survive into. Everyone in this valley who is still here is here because at some point somebody who owed them nothing opened a gate. Marta's position, and she argued it hard at Whitewell against people with better reasons than hers, was that a household which has decided in principle never to take anybody in has already made its decision about the rest of it too, and will keep making that decision until there is nothing left inside the wall worth the walls.

**What ignoring it costs.** She was not naive and she was not talking about taking in everybody, which is not possible and gets people killed. She was talking about the third winter, and about what a household becomes after a hundred consecutive refusals: quiet, efficient, sealed, and unable to remember why it is doing any of this. We took in four people that Marta argued for. Two of them are the reason Hillfoot came through the second winter. One of them wrote a third of the chapter you have just read.
