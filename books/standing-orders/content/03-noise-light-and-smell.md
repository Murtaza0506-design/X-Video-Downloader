# Chapter 3 — Noise, Light and Smell

Everything in this chapter follows from one observation that took the valley about six weeks to make, and it is this: they do not find you. They arrive at you, from wherever they were, along a line drawn by something you did.

That distinction sounds academic and it is not. If you believe you are being hunted, you behave as though the danger is intelligent, and you spend your energy on hiding from something that is looking. If you understand that you are being arrived at, you spend it instead on the four or five things that draw a line, and you can count those on one hand. Noise. Light after dark. Smoke. Smell. Movement in the open.

The Bracewell standing orders got there first and put it in nine lines of gloss paint on a grain store wall. The whole of that document is an audit of a farm's emissions. Where does sound leave this place. Where does light. What can be smelled from the lane. Ruth Cadogan used to walk the perimeter at dusk once a week and listen to her own household from the outside. I have not seen the discipline bettered. Almost nobody keeps it up past the first winter.

The second thing to understand is that the standard is not silence. It is consistency. A valley makes noise. Water, wind, stock, rooks, a gate. What draws attention is the sound that does not belong to the landscape: the metallic, the rhythmic, the human voice raised. A generator running at nine at night is audible to anything within a mile and is the single most expensive noise anybody in this valley has ever made.

And the last rule in the chapter is about the cost of keeping all of the others. After a year that cost is considerable, and it is paid in a currency people do not notice they are spending.

---

> **Assume every sound you make travels twice as far as you think.** — Colm Reidy

**What it buys you.** A working margin. Sound carries further at night, further in cold air, further along water and much further down a valley than daytime intuition allows for. Every person who has tested this has been shocked by the result. Send somebody half a mile down the lane at ten at night and have them tell you what they can hear of your house. The answer is the rule.

**What ignoring it costs.** You will draw things towards you while believing you are being careful, which is worse than being careless, because careless people stay alert. The families that were overrun in the first winter were not loud households. They were households that had decided they were quiet ones, on no evidence, and had stopped checking. Test it from outside. Everything else in this chapter is guesswork until you have.

---

> **Cook before dusk.** — Ruth Cadogan

**What it buys you.** The smell and smoke of your kitchen are released into the one part of the day when the air is still moving and light hides nothing that dark would hide anyway. Cooking in daylight, eating at dusk and having a cold, dark, quiet house from nightfall onwards is the single largest change a household can make, and it costs nothing but habit.

**What ignoring it costs.** Cooking smells at night travel down a valley on the cold air with nothing to compete with them. They can be picked up at a distance that will not seem credible. Stand in a lane and smell somebody else's meal from a quarter of a mile away. Ruth's household ate at four in the afternoon for three years. The people who kept eating at seven kept being visited, and never worked out why.

---

> **One lamp, one room, and cover the window before you strike the match.** — the Whitewell list

**What it buys you.** Light, which people give up too readily and then live worse for. You do not have to sit in the dark. You have to make a single room lightproof, do it properly with battens and blankets rather than a curtain, and check it from the yard once. Then that room can have a lamp in it every night of the winter and the house stays black from the lane.

**What ignoring it costs.** A candle behind an ordinary curtain is visible for a mile and a half across open country and it is the only light in the landscape. Nothing else advertises occupancy like it, and it advertises to people as much as to anything else. The order of the rule matters and is the part people get wrong: cover first, then light. Doing it the other way round means you have already broadcast for the ninety seconds it takes to hang the blanket.

---

> **A dog is a decision, not a companion.** — Elin Pryce

**What it buys you.** Honesty about a trade that people make without noticing they have made it. A dog is the best early warning system available and is worth more than any watch rota. A dog is also a thing that barks at the wrong moment, needs feeding, and cannot be told to be silent when it matters. Elin's position was that a working dog that has been trained to stop on command is an asset and an untrained one is a liability with a face you love.

**What ignoring it costs.** Two households I know of were emptied because of a dog, and both of them had been told and had made the reasonable human choice. If you keep the dog, train it in the first month while you still have food to train with, and understand what you are keeping. If you cannot train it, you are choosing company over warning, and that is a real choice that people are allowed to make. Make it with your eyes open.

---

> **Never run where you can walk, and never walk where you can wait.** — Deniz Aksoy

**What it buys you.** Running is the loudest thing a person does, and it turns one problem into three. A running human is visible at distance and is understood by everything that sees it. Walking is quiet, is thinking speed, and can be stopped. Waiting is free. Deniz reckoned that four out of every five situations he had been in would have resolved on their own if the person had stood still for a hundred seconds.

**What ignoring it costs.** A hundred yards of panic and you are somewhere you have not chosen, breathing hard, with no idea what is behind you. The rule is not about courage. It is about the fact that the first movement is almost always a reaction, and reactions in this world are expensive. Stop. Count to a hundred. Then move at a walk.

---

> **Metal on stone carries further than a voice.** — Aiden Kerr

**What it buys you.** The specific knowledge of which noises are the dangerous ones, which is not intuitive. Human speech at conversational volume is absorbed by hedges and walls and is a poor traveller. A dropped pan, a bolt thrown on a gate, a spade struck on a flag, a car door: these are metallic, sharp, and belong to nothing in a landscape. Wrap what you carry. Tape the buckles. Aiden lined his pack with a jumper for two years.

**What ignoring it costs.** You will be careful about the wrong thing. Households whisper indoors and then hang a metal bucket on a gatepost. Walk your own yard and find every hard surface that can be struck by accident in the dark, and deal with all of them in an afternoon. It is the cheapest hour of work in this book.

---

> **Fix the hinge before it needs fixing.** — Tom Shackleton

**What it buys you.** A place that does not announce its own use. Every gate you open twenty times a day, every door, every latch, every board on a stair. Tom greased everything at Hillfoot on a schedule and it sounded like nothing at all, and I did not understand why he bothered until I stayed somewhere that had not.

**What ignoring it costs.** You will start avoiding your own infrastructure. That is the real cost and it takes months to show. People stop using the near gate because it squeals, so they walk the long way round in the open. They stop going to the store after dark because of the door, so they carry a week of food at a time and lose the ability to move at night. A place that is noisy in small ways slowly bends everybody's behaviour into worse shapes, and nobody ever names the hinge.

---

> **Smoke is a letter with your address on it.** — Samuel Oyelaran

**What it buys you.** Warmth, if you are clever about it, and the whole of the second winter for the households that were. Burn dry wood and only dry wood. Burn it hot and fast rather than smouldering it all day. Burn in the middle of the day, when the sky is white and there is weather about. Never on a still, clear, cold morning, when a column will stand up three hundred feet and be visible from the far side of the valley.

**What ignoring it costs.** The most reliable way to find people in this country is to stand somewhere high at seven in the morning and look. Sam did it for the Bracewell people as a survey and mapped eleven occupied houses in a single hour, none of which knew they were visible. Green wood and a banked fire produce a white smoke that hangs and does not disperse. That is the signature everybody looks for, and it is the one produced by a household trying to make its fuel last.

---

> **Keep something loud that you are allowed to do.** — Joyce Attwell

**What it buys you.** A household that can bear its own discipline. Joyce ran a singing hour on Sunday afternoons in a stone cellar with two doors shut. She took a great deal of quiet mockery for it in the first year and none at all in the second. Every rule in this chapter is a small tax on being a person, and a year of paying it without relief produces households that are silent, careful, obedient and coming apart at the seams.

**What ignoring it costs.** Children who whisper. Adults who flinch at a dropped spoon. Arguments about nothing at ten at night, which is what unspent fear turns into. Two of the households that failed in the second winter did not fail to anything outside their walls. Pick your hour, pick your room, do it properly with the doors shut and somebody on the lane, and then be as loud as you like inside it. The point of surviving is not to become something that cannot sing.
