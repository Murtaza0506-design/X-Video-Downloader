# A Note on This Book

This book is fiction, and it is worth stepping out from behind the narrator to say so plainly.

There was no outbreak. Hillfoot, Bracewell and Whitewell are invented, along with everybody named in the index and every rule they are credited with. The valley is put together out of real country in the north of England and then bent wherever the story needed it bent. Nobody in these pages ever lived.

That matters more here than it would in an ordinary novel, because the book is shaped like a manual, and a manual invites you to use it. So: do not. The rules in here were written to be true inside their own world and to be repeated by people under pressure. They were not checked against medicine, hydrology, structural engineering or cold-weather physiology, and in several places I have kept a rule that is wrong in our world because it is the kind of thing survivors would believe, and believing it is part of the story. A reader who cannot tell which ones those are should assume it is all of them.

If you want the real version, it exists and it is better than this. A first aid course from any recognised provider will teach you more about a wound in one afternoon than the whole of chapter five. Your national flood, fire and civil emergency guidance is written by people who have studied actual disasters, and it is free. Water treatment, hypothermia and food storage are all well documented by public health bodies whose interest is in you not dying. None of them will entertain you. All of them are correct, which this is not trying to be.

On the shape of the thing. The entries argue with each other on purpose. Advice that never contradicts itself has been simplified into decoration, because real situations differ and the honest form of guidance is a rule plus the conditions it holds under. Every chapter ends with a rule that lets you off: permission to stay put, to keep the grief, to leave the wound alone, to break the list. That was the hardest part to write and the part readers of the drafts kept naming as the reason they believed the rest.

On the sources. Attributing invented rules to invented people is a device, and it does one job: it keeps the book from sounding like an authority. Every rule in here is somebody's opinion, formed in bad light, on incomplete information, by a person who was frightened. The index of sources is at the back so you can see how thin the evidence is. Three of the most confident rules in the book come from the same man, and chapter eleven tells you what happened to him.
