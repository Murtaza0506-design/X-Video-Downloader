# Chapter 4 — Water and Waste

More people in this valley have died of their own drinking water than of anything else. Not close. Not arguable.

I want that sentence to sit on its own. The shape of this whole thing in most people's heads is wrong, and it stays wrong until they have nursed somebody through four days of it. You picture the danger as a thing coming up a lane. The danger is a jug on a table. It is quiet, it arrived by a route nobody watched, and it takes out a household from the inside while every rule in chapter three is being kept.

The mechanism is dull, which is why nobody guards against it. A latrine gets sited in the wrong place in the first week by people who are thinking about other things. Water is drawn from a beck below it. Somebody skips the boil once because the fire is out and they are tired and it looked clean, and clean is a judgement the eye is not qualified to make. Then a child is ill. Then the person nursing the child is ill. By the fourth day the household cannot fetch its own water. That is the point at which this stops being a stomach complaint and becomes a way of dying.

Samuel Oyelaran worked on water for a living before, and he said the thing that changed how I think about all of it. Sanitation is not a health measure. It is a defensive structure, no different from a wall, and it fails the same way: not dramatically, but at the one point where somebody stopped maintaining it.

Nine rules. Most of them are boring. The boring ones are the ones that work.

---

> **Boil it.** — the Hillfoot parlour door

**What it buys you.** Nearly everything. A rolling boil kills what is in the water in this country. It needs no filter, no chemical, no fuel you do not have, and no judgement you are not qualified to make. It was the first of the seven rules on the parlour door, and it is two words long. The man who wrote it understood that a rule you have to remember correctly under pressure has already failed.

**What ignoring it costs.** Four days on a floor, and the household around you cutting its own throat trying to nurse you with the same water. The lie the eye tells is the whole problem: it looked clean, it was moving, it came off the fell. All true, and none of it relevant. Boil the water that came off the fell. Boil the water from the tap that still runs. Boil it when you are tired. That is the rule.

---

> **Two containers, and they never touch.** — Marta Ellison

**What it buys you.** A system that cannot be broken by tiredness. One vessel for raw water, one for boiled, marked differently, kept in different places, never swapped and never nested inside each other for storage. Marta painted the raw ones red with fence paint. A household that has done this can be exhausted, ill and working in the dark and still not poison itself, because the correct action is the only available action.

**What ignoring it costs.** You will do everything right for eleven weeks and then pour boiled water into the can that carried the dirty, at night, and undo all of it. This is the most common single failure I know of and it is never committed by careless people. It is committed by tired ones, at the end of a long day, in a kitchen with one clean surface. Design for the tired version of yourself. She is the one who will be doing this.

---

> **Site the latrine downhill of the water and twice as far away as feels necessary.** — Samuel Oyelaran

**What it buys you.** Permanence. Thirty metres from any well, spring, beck or standing water, downhill, downwind, and dug deep with the spoil kept beside it to cover each use. Do it in the first week while it is a matter of choosing, and you have solved it for years. Sam's figure was thirty metres and he was scathing about people who negotiated with him about the last ten of them in the rain.

**What ignoring it costs.** A hillside is not a diagram. Water moves under it, along the rock, in directions you cannot see from the surface, and it will arrive in your spring six weeks after you made a decision you have since forgotten making. That gap is why nobody ever connects the two. Two households on the Slaidburn road spent a winter ill and blamed the flour. It was not the flour.

---

> **Wash your hands before you eat, every time, with no exceptions and no soap.** — Marta Ellison

**What it buys you.** The largest single reduction in illness available to a household, for the cost of a jug and thirty seconds. Soap helps and its absence is not an excuse: running water over hands with vigorous rubbing removes most of what matters, and the mechanical part is doing most of the work. Ash and water is a serviceable soap and you have ash.

**What ignoring it costs.** The whole route from latrine to hand to bread to family, which is the route that empties houses. Marta's argument at Whitewell was that no other intervention gives so much for so little. She pushed for a jug at the door of every eating room, not a rule people had to remember. Make it a fixture and not a virtue. Virtue is not there on the bad days.

---

> **Rain is the cheapest water you will ever have.** — Colm Reidy

**What it buys you.** Volume, off any roof, from any weather, without a journey. A shed roof and two butts will keep a household in washing and stock water through most of the year in this country, which means your drinking water and your carrying trips are for drinking alone. Boil what you drink anyway. Birds use the roof too.

**What ignoring it costs.** Every litre you did not catch is a litre somebody walks for, and walking for water is the most dangerous routine activity in this book. It is predictable. It happens at the same time each day. It goes to a place other people also need, and it is done by somebody carrying weight with both hands full. Cut the number of trips and you cut the exposure, and the roof does it while you sleep.

---

> **Never drink from still water, whatever it looks like.** — Elin Pryce

**What it buys you.** A simple rule for choosing a source when you have no way of testing one. Moving water in the hills, above habitation, above stock, taken from the middle rather than the edge, is the best you will find. Elin's veterinary eye was on the ground above the source: what grazes there, what is dead in it, what drains into it after rain.

**What ignoring it costs.** Still water concentrates everything that has entered it and adds its own. A tarn, a trough, a flooded ditch, the pool below a ford where cattle stand. All of them look calmer and cleaner than a peaty beck, and the peaty beck is the safer drink by a distance that is not close. Colour is not contamination. Stillness is.

---

> **Put the water on a rota and never let one person own it.** — Ruth Cadogan

**What it buys you.** A household that keeps working when somebody is ill. That is precisely when the water job matters most, and when the person who has always done it is on the floor. Everybody fetches, everybody boils, everybody knows where the site is and how the containers are marked. Ruth ran hers on a fortnightly rotation with a written list.

**What ignoring it costs.** Water goes unfetched on the exact day it is most needed, and somebody improvises, and the improvisation is what kills. There is a second cost that is slower and worse. A household where one person carries every load builds a quiet resentment. It comes out in the second winter as something else, and it will look like an argument about cooking.

---

> **Filter for grit, boil for life, and do not confuse the two.** — Samuel Oyelaran

**What it buys you.** Clarity about what your equipment does. Cloth, sand and charcoal take out silt, leaf and much of the taste, and they make water pleasant to drink and easier to boil. They do not make it safe. Sam built beautiful slow sand filters at Bracewell and put a sign on every one of them saying this water is not yet drinkable.

**What ignoring it costs.** People trust apparatus. Give a household a filter that produces clear water out of brown, and within a month somebody will be drinking straight from the tap on it. It looks like a machine, and machines feel like a warranty. That sign is the whole reason nobody at Bracewell ever did. Filter first if you like. Then boil, every time, without an argument.

---

> **A dirty person who is drinking beats a clean one who is not.** — Bibi Farooq

**What it buys you.** Priorities, on the day the whole system fails. If you have one pan of water and a choice between washing and drinking, drink. If you have a source you distrust and no fire, drink it and be ill later. The person who refuses to drink for two days in August will be past making decisions by the evening of the second. Bibi was a pharmacist and she had watched people become fastidious about exactly the wrong thing.

**What ignoring it costs.** This chapter, taken as scripture, has killed people. There was a man on the Ribble who would not drink from a beck he could not boil. He walked eleven miles in July looking for a better one. He was found four fields from a spring. The rules above are for the ordinary weeks, which is almost all of them. On the day that is not ordinary, drink the water.
