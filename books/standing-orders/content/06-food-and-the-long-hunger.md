# Chapter 6 — Food and the Long Hunger

Nobody starved in the first year. Almost everybody was hungry in the second, and the difference between those two sentences is a single decision made in about the fourth month by people who were not thinking about food at all.

The first year is easy, and its easiness is the trap. There is an enormous quantity of food in an ordinary district: shops, houses, sheds, stores, freezers that stay cold for a week, and stock in the fields that nobody is milking. A household with legs and a bag eats well for months and comes to believe it has solved the problem, when what it has actually done is spend a stock that does not renew. The tins in this valley were finished by the end of the second summer. All of them. There is no more of that anywhere within reach, and the people who understood that in month four are the ones who put seed in the ground in March, and the people who understood it in month fourteen were too late by a full growing season.

That is the thing to take from this chapter if you take one thing. Food is not a supply problem, it is a calendar problem, and the calendar is unforgiving in a way that nothing else in this book is. You cannot make up for a missed sowing with courage or work or luck. There is no version of August that fixes April.

The Gisburn stock book is the source I lean on most here. It is a ledger from a farm supplier, and somebody, and I have never found out who, kept using it after: sowings, yields, weights, what went wrong. Four years of somebody else's careful arithmetic. It is the most valuable object I own.

The last rule in the chapter is Nell's and it is not about calories.

---

> **Eat the perishable first, in order, and write the order on the wall.** — Ruth Cadogan

**What it buys you.** Weeks of food that would otherwise rot in front of you. Freezer, then fridge, then fresh, then root and dry store, then tins, in that sequence, with the list written up where everybody can see it so that nobody is deciding at the cupboard door. Ruth's board had three columns and a date beside each item and it fed nine people through a summer.

**What ignoring it costs.** Households eat what they fancy, which means they eat the tins because tins feel like the emergency ration, and the fresh food spoils while they are protecting the food that would have kept for four years. This is the most common error of the first fortnight and it is committed out of prudence. Eat in the order that things go bad. Save what does not.

---

> **Salt is worth more than ammunition.** — the Gisburn stock book

**What it buys you.** Preservation, which is the entire difference between a good autumn and a hungry March. Meat, fish, beans, cabbage, hide. Salt is heavy, cheap while there is any, and impossible to make in this country in any quantity, and there is a note in the stock book from the first winter that reads simply: get salt, all of it, now, in a hand that is noticeably less steady than the rest.

**What ignoring it costs.** A glut you cannot hold. The second autumn was extraordinary. There was more meat in this valley in October than we could eat, and by December there was very little, because it had been eaten fresh or lost, and the households with salt went into the spring with a barrel and the others went into it with nothing. Trade for salt. Take it from every kitchen you pass. It does not spoil and it never gets cheaper.

---

> **Never eat the seed.** — Tom Shackleton

**What it buys you.** Next year, and the year after. Seed potatoes, grain, beans held back for sowing, and the two ewes you do not kill. Tom kept his separate, in a locked stone store, labelled, and treated the store as though it were not food at all, which is the only way anybody manages it. It is a small quantity that stands between a household and permanent dependence.

**What ignoring it costs.** In February the seed store is the best food in the house and there is a hungry child in it, and I am not going to pretend that this is a straightforward rule to keep. But a household that eats its seed has converted a farm into three good meals, and there is nothing after that except asking somebody else for seed on their terms, and their terms in March are not the terms they would have offered in October. Tom's answer was to make the decision structural. Lock it. Give the key to somebody who will not be in the kitchen at midnight.

---

> **Hunger makes bad plans look reasonable. Eat before you decide.** — Deniz Aksoy

**What it buys you.** Judgement at the moment it is worth most. A hungry group will talk itself into a raid, a river crossing, a trade with people it does not trust, or a walk to a rumour, and will experience all of that as clear thinking. Deniz would not hold a planning conversation before a meal, and the rule at his table was that anybody who suggested something ambitious got a bowl before an answer.

**What ignoring it costs.** The worst decisions of the second winter were all made at the end of the day, by people who had eaten once, about food. Three of them killed somebody. Hunger does not feel like impairment from inside; it feels like urgency and clarity. That is what makes it more dangerous than tiredness, which at least announces itself.

---

> **Learn three wild foods properly instead of thirty badly.** — Elin Pryce

**What it buys you.** Real calories from things you cannot misidentify. Nettle, which is everywhere and is unmistakable and holds up boiled. Hawthorn leaf in spring. Fish, if you have water. Elin's whole argument was about confidence under pressure: three plants you know absolutely, in quantity, in all their stages, are worth more than a book of thirty you half know, because you will be foraging tired and in poor light.

**What ignoring it costs.** The foraging books make it look like a larder and it is not. Wild food in this country is a supplement, not a diet, and the calories per hour of searching are terrible compared with a garden or a snare. The households that leaned on it went into the winter light. And there is the other cost, which is rarer and absolute: two deaths in the valley from something eaten by somebody who was fairly sure.

---

> **Cache in three places and never all of it anywhere.** — Aiden Kerr

**What it buys you.** Survival of a disaster rather than an inconvenience. Split the store: some in the house, some buried or walled up at a distance, some at a third site nobody discusses. Fire, theft, rot and flood are all single-location events, and a household that loses one third of its food in November is having a bad month, while a household that loses all of it is finished.

**What ignoring it costs.** Everything, at once, on somebody else's schedule. Aiden had watched three households cleared out in an afternoon in the second summer and every one of them had kept a good store in one dry room, which is exactly what a careful person would do. Keep the third cache genuinely secret and check it twice a year, because the failure mode of a buried cache is that it is wet and nobody looked.

---

> **Fat is the thing you run out of.** — Bibi Farooq

**What it buys you.** The winter. A diet of potatoes, roots and greens looks adequate on the plate and leaves people cold, slow and always hungry, and the thing missing is fat. Render everything. Keep the drippings, the marrow, the skin, the tallow. Butter, lard, oil and suet are the highest-value foods you will handle and they are the first things people stop noticing when they disappear.

**What ignoring it costs.** A household that is eating what looks like enough and is thin by March. Bibi identified it before anybody else because she was watching people rather than stores, and the pattern was always the same: plenty of bulk, no fat, and a slow decline that everybody blamed on the cold. Cold is worse when you have not eaten fat. So is thinking.

---

> **A garden sown in March feeds you in August, and not one day sooner.** — the Gisburn stock book

**What it buys you.** An accurate calendar, which is the only thing that lets you plan a hungry period rather than discover one. Potatoes go in when the soil warms and come out in late summer. Brassicas sown in June carry you through the winter and have to be started while you are still busy with everything else. The stock book has it all laid out by week, in somebody's handwriting, with what went wrong each year in the margin.

**What ignoring it costs.** The gap. Between the end of the stored food and the first harvest there is a period of about eight weeks that nobody plans for because it happens in early summer when the countryside looks generous, and it is the hungriest part of the year in every account I have. Count backwards from your harvest. Then look at what you have and decide now, in comfort, how thin June is going to be.

---

> **Keep one thing back that is only for pleasure.** — Nell Shackleton

**What it buys you.** People who are still willing to do the work. A jar of something sweet, a bottle, a tin of peaches, tea. Nell kept a box under her bed and produced it four times a year without warning, on bad days, and the effect on a farmhouse in February is out of all proportion to the calories. Nobody has ever worked hard for a life of pure sufficiency.

**What ignoring it costs.** A household that is provisioned correctly, following every rule in this chapter, and slowly stops caring. That sounds soft next to the rest of the chapter and it is not. The failures I have seen in the third winter were not failures of supply. They were people who could not find a reason to go out in the rain and check the snares, and there is no store in the world that survives a household in that state.
