# Canon

The world bible. Everything a spinoff has to agree with, in one place, so that
the second book cannot quietly contradict the first. Where the manuscript and
this file disagree, the manuscript wins and this file gets corrected.

## The thing itself

Nobody agreed on a name. Officially *the affected*. Commonly *them*. In the
north, *the quiet ones*. The generation that grew up in it says *sleepers*.

1. **Transmission is saliva into blood.** A bite. A scratch only if the hand
   had been in a mouth. Not airborne, not waterborne, not by touch. Blood on
   unbroken skin is nothing; blood in a cut, an eye or a mouth is not.
2. **They do not eat.** They bite once and walk on. This is the load-bearing
   fact of the whole setting: a predator that fed would have left few carriers,
   and this leaves nearly all of them. It is vector behaviour, not hunting.
3. **Turning takes four hours to nine days**, tracking body mass and how much
   saliva got in. Small children turn fast. Heavy adults turn slow. Nobody has
   recovered. The six-day protocol in entry 64 is a compromise with available
   labour, not a finding, and it has failed twice.
4. **Slow and tireless.** Just under four miles an hour, held indefinitely.
   You walk three, and you stop, and you sleep. Over a day they cover ninety
   miles and you cover twenty. You cannot outwalk them, ever. You can only
   stop being perceptible, at which point they drift again within an hour or
   two.
5. **Sound, then movement, then smell.** Hearing about human, a little better
   low down. Eyesight poor and worsening. Smell is how they tell each other
   apart, which is why smelling enough like them works.
6. **They run on air temperature.** Below about four degrees they are slow and
   stupid. In a hard frost they stand still and become street furniture. In
   August they are quick and they rot. **Winter is the working season. Summer
   is the season you survive.** This is the engine of the setting: it gives the
   year a rhythm and it makes every plan seasonal.
7. **The brain stops them**, or the spine above the shoulders, or enough broken
   frame that the legs will not carry them. They do not bleed out and they do
   not tire.
8. **They rot, slowly.** By the fourth year the first wave is failing at the
   hips and ankles. What survives is selected: the ones that turned in the cold
   and have spent four years in shade.
9. **They gather.** No coordination, but they follow each other's noise, so a
   sound in a town becomes a crowd of thousands over days. A **drift**, or in
   the north a **walk**. A drift cannot be lost by breaking line of sight,
   because the back follows the front. Against a drift, only distance works.
10. **They do not drown.** No breathing, no panic, no floating. One that goes
    into a canal walks along the bottom and comes out the far bank six or seven
    minutes later.

## When and where

Began the third week of March, on a Tuesday morning, between nine and half past
eleven. Britain; this book is Yorkshire, mostly Leeds and the valley above it:
Beeston, Armley, Hunslet, Kirkstall, Otley, Guiseley, Skipton, Wetherby. The
present of the book is **the fourth winter**. Day counts in attributions run
from day 1 (that Tuesday) to about day 1,400.

Timeline anchors used in the text:
- First fortnight: the checking machinery goes. The radio is still talking.
- Days 11–22: the Kendal camp broadcast. Four hundred people walk north. There
  is no camp and there never was.
- First February: the cold kills more than anything with teeth.
- Second summer: the worst season in the book. Flies, rot, quick ones.
- Third year: settlements begin. Trade, rotas, the first weddings.
- Fourth year: the count of walkers falls for the first time.

## Rules of the telling

- Every entry is a rule, a signature with a day count, why it works, and how it
  goes wrong. The failure paragraph is never optional and never softened.
- Entries contradict each other on purpose and the compiler refuses to
  arbitrate. Named pairs so far: 39 and 44 (down versus height), 70 and 77 (the
  door), 53 and 55 (silence).
- Every chapter's last entry gives the reader permission not to follow the
  chapter.
- British English. No em dashes in prose. The compiler is precise about
  numbers, and every number in the book adds up; breaking that breaks her.
- Nobody in the book is a fool, and any entry that reads as though somebody was
  has failed.
