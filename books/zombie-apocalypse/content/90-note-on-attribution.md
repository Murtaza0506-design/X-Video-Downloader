# A Note on the Sources

Eleven hundred notebooks came into the Long Room between the second autumn and this one. Most arrived in bin bags, brought by people who had cleared a house and could not bring themselves to burn the paper in it. Ninety-nine rules came out. It is worth saying plainly what that number is made of, because a manual that will not show its working is asking you to bet your life on a stranger's memory.

**Fifty-one are verbatim.** The rule is on the page in the hand of the person named, and I have the page. Where the wording is odd I have left it odd. Bea Winslow wrote in the imperative because she had spent forty years writing Post Office notices, and her rules read like Post Office notices, and I am not going to improve them.

**Twenty-two are verbatim but undated.** The rule is in the notebook and the day count is my estimate, taken from what is around it: a weather note, a death, a mention of the frost. Where I have guessed I have guessed low, because people write rules down after the thing that taught them, not before.

**Twenty-five are reconstructed.** Somebody told me a rule that somebody else had kept, and the second person was dead, and there is no page. I have written those in the words I was given and named the person they belong to. Two of them I have from three separate mouths that never met, which is the strongest evidence in the collection and also, I am aware, exactly what a well travelled invention looks like.

**One is mine.** It is the last entry in the book and it is signed with this building, because leaving a gap in a manual is worse than filling it in your own name. Fifty-one and twenty-two and twenty-five and one is ninety-nine, and if that arithmetic ever stops working then somebody has edited this without telling me.

**On Idris Pyle.** Four rules in this book are attributed to a man who by his own account was a confidence trickster in Sheffield before all this, and who by the account of four other people was a driving instructor. His rules are the best writing in the collection. They are also, two of them, wrong in ways I can demonstrate, and one of those is a rule for taking food off a group of strangers dressed up as a rule for joining one. I have kept all four and marked the two. If you want a single reason to distrust every attribution in this book, it is that the most quotable man in it is the one I am least sure existed. Two of the notebooks in his hand are in different hands.

**On the neat ones.** Four rules arrived already famous, passed mouth to mouth across three counties, attached to no notebook and no body. They are the four best sentences in the manual. That is the tell. A rule that has been polished by three hundred retellings is a rule that has been optimised for being repeated, which is not the same property as being true, and the entries say so where I could establish it and admit it where I could not.

**On the dead.** Thirty-six people have rules in this book, and the index gives each of them with the entries they contributed. Two hundred and six are named somewhere in these pages once you count the ones who appear inside an entry rather than at the head of one, and of those, a hundred and seventy-one are dead, twenty-nine are alive as of the first frost, and six I have no word of either way. I have been asked more than once to take the day counts out, on the grounds that they make the book frightening to read. They stay in. A rule is worth what it cost, and the day count is the price on the tag.

**On what is missing.** There is nothing in here about children under four, because the two people who knew most about that both died in the second summer and I will not reconstruct what I do not understand. There is nothing about the coast, which is a different apocalypse with different rules, and Hana Sørensen has a notebook of her own and is welcome to it. There is nothing about London.

If you have a notebook, the Long Room is on the corner where the bank was. Bring it. If the rule in it is better than the rule in here, the rule in here comes out.
