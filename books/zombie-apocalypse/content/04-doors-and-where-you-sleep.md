# Chapter 4 — Doors, and Where You Sleep

Almost everything people built in the first year was built against the wrong thing.

The instinct is to build against them, and the instinct is understandable, and it is a poor use of the two weeks of work you have in you. They are not a siege engine. One of them at a door is a nuisance that a wedge and some patience will see off. A hundred of them at a door is a situation that no wall a family can build in a fortnight was ever going to hold. So the entire middle ground that people spent their labour on turns out to be the part that never comes up. What actually comes through a wall is fire, water, cold, and people, in that order by frequency and in a different order by how much it hurts.

Here is the correction, and every rule in this chapter is a version of it. A shelter is not a fortress. It is a machine with four jobs. It keeps the weather off you. It keeps you warm on the fuel you have. It tells you something is coming before the thing arrives. And it lets you leave. That last job is the one everybody deletes. It is deleted because deleting it feels like commitment and looks like competence. It is the single commonest fatal design error in these eleven hundred notebooks. I have put it at the front of the chapter and again at the back, in the hope that saying it twice does something.

There is a second correction and it is quieter. The winter kills more than the door does. In the first February, in this county, more people in these papers died of the cold in a building than died of anything with teeth, and most of them died in large rooms in fine houses. They were heating a volume of air that nothing they owned could ever have heated. The rule about the small room is the least dramatic entry in this chapter and it is the one that has saved the most people. You will not want to follow it in November. You will be glad of it in March.

---

> "Count the doors before you count the exits." — Terrance Bello, locksmith, day 9

**Why it works.** Bello's point is that people survey a building for how things get in, which is a list of doors, and then believe they have surveyed it. The useful list is a different list with different members. An exit is a door you can go through, in the dark, in a hurry, carrying somebody, with whatever tool is in your pocket. A door you have screwed, barred, filled or bricked is not on that list any more. He walked buildings with two counts in two columns and made people say both numbers out loud. In a leisure centre, a chapel and two schools those numbers came out very far apart.

**How it goes wrong.** He counted eleven doors in the leisure centre at Cottingley and made all eleven fast. When the fire took the east stand there were eleven doors and no exits, because he had welded the difference out of the building. That is the whole of it, and he was the best man at that trade in this half of the city, and he had been reasoning correctly the entire way. What he lacked was a rule about which of the two numbers is allowed to reach zero. Make the doors hard. Leave two of them, on different walls, that a frightened person can open with their hands, and tell everybody in the building which two they are.

---

> "Wedge. Do not lock." — Marcus Okon, police sergeant, day 55

**Why it works.** A wedge under a door that opens towards you is close to unbeatable by anything without a shoulder and a plan, and it has four properties a lock does not. It is silent going in. It needs no key, so nobody is ever the wrong side of it. It works on any door in any building you walk into, including the one you have been in for eleven minutes. And it comes out with a kick when you have decided to go, which no bar, screw or barricade does. Okon carried six of them cut from a fence post and got through the whole of the second year on nothing else.

**How it goes wrong.** Wedges want a floor. On carpet they ride. On a worn stone flag they skate. On a door with a quarter inch under it they do nothing at all, and you find this out at two in the morning. They are also a fine defence against a push and a poor one against a sustained lean. That is what a crowd is. A crowd does not push. It arrives and continues to be there. Okon's own note is that a wedge buys you between forty seconds and four minutes, and that the number is not the point. The point is that the forty seconds are yours to use, and if you have not decided in advance what you are going to do with them then you have bought nothing.

> "Glass is not a wall. Glass is a curtain." — Oz Prentice, scaffolder, day 22

**Why it works.** Go and look at a house that was lost in the first year and the door is nearly always intact. People fortify what they think of as the way in. The way in was the bay window in the front room, four feet of nine hundred millimetre float glass that a wheelie bin goes through by falling on it. Prentice had spent twenty years working outside buildings and had a glazier's view of what a house actually is, which is a light frame with big holes in it and some plaster. His rule reorders the work: the ground floor openings first, all of them, before anybody touches a door.

**How it goes wrong.** He boarded a whole ground floor in Armley in three days and then had to live in it. A house with every opening blinded is a house where nobody can see out. Not being able to see out is worse than most of what you were afraid of seeing. Every decision about going outside is now made blind. The people in that house began to go out to find out what was happening, which is the exact behaviour the boards were meant to prevent. Board the glass. Then cut a slot at eye height in two boards on two different walls, and accept the small risk that comes with them. The alternative is a group that has to open the front door to learn anything.

---

> "Sleep where you have to be lifted to." — Lila Semenova, climber, day 24

**Why it works.** They do not climb. They manage stairs, badly, because stairs are a floor at an angle and their legs still work. A ladder is not a floor at an angle. Anything that needs a hand on a rung and a decision about where the next foot goes is outside what they can do. It always has been. It is the most reliable single fact about them after the cold. So: a hayloft with the ladder pulled up. A flat roof reached by a scaffold plank you draw in. A first floor with the stairs taken out and a rope ladder in the hole. Any of those turns eight hours of unconsciousness from the most dangerous part of your day into the safest.

**How it goes wrong.** People. A rope ladder is a wonderful defence against a thing that cannot think, and a signpost to a thing that can. Semenova was robbed twice in a loft she could not get out of, by men who stood at the bottom and waited. It took them no effort at all. There is also the ordinary failure. She broke a wrist coming down in the dark at speed in the second November, in a hurry, for a reason that turned out to be a fox. Every night for a year she had climbed up and been safe. It took one descent to cost her four months of work.

> "Two ways out of any room you sleep in." — Rosa Kaminski, schoolteacher, day 70

**Why it works.** She ran fire drills for twenty-two years and she brought the finding with her. What people do in the first eleven seconds of being woken by an emergency is go back the way they came in, all of them, together. If that way is blocked they stop and stand in it. A second exit does not merely double your chances. It halves the crowd at the first one, which is the thing that kills. Kaminski made every group she was ever part of walk both exits, in the dark, twice, on the night they arrived, and no group of hers has ever lost anybody in a building.

**How it goes wrong.** Two ways out is two ways in, and the whole of chapter four is an argument about that trade. Her old school had fourteen exits and was the most indefensible building in the district, and she is clear that she would not sleep in it. There is a second failure that is subtler. A group that has drilled its exits will use them. Using an exit means going outside at night with no information. Twice in these notebooks a group has gone out of the back door into something worse, because a bin fell over at the front. The drill is right. Add one word to it: everybody goes to the second exit, and nobody goes through it until somebody has looked.

---

> "The smallest room you can stand." — Solomon Quaye, barber, day 200

**Why it works.** This is the entry that has saved the most lives in this chapter and it will not feel like it in November. A body puts out about the heat of a bulb. Three bodies and one candle will hold a box room above freezing all night with the door shut. Nothing you own will do that for a Victorian front room with a nine foot ceiling and a chimney in it. In the first February people froze in beautiful houses with fuel still in the yard. They were heating a volume instead of a space. Quaye moved five people into a back scullery eight feet by seven and hung blankets over the door and the window. They came through the worst month any of us have seen without a single case of anything.

**How it goes wrong.** Five people in a room eight feet by seven, in the dark, for four months, is a thing that happens to your mind. Two of his five stopped speaking to each other in that scullery over something neither of them can now reconstruct, and one of them left in the spring and did not come back. Small rooms preserve the body and grind the group, and the grinding does not show up until March, and by March it is done. Take the room. Then build the day around getting people out of it in ones and twos for an hour, in daylight, doing something with their hands, whatever the weather is doing. Treat that hour as fuel and not as leisure.

> "Bells." — Isaac Bramall, beekeeper, day 40

**Why it works.** You cannot watch. That is the fact underneath every perimeter anybody has ever built. A group of six cannot keep a pair of eyes awake and useful on three approaches for six months. The ones that tried burned out in a fortnight and then slept through everything. So the ground watches instead. Bramall ran fine wire at shin height through the hedges at thirty yards out, with tins and two brass bells off a harness. He could sleep. He had not slept properly since March. The distance matters more than the noise: thirty yards is far enough that the sound reaches you before the thing does and near enough that you know it is your problem.

**How it goes wrong.** Alarm fatigue, and it is merciless. Foxes, cats, wind, a badger every night for a week. By the fortieth false alarm nobody is getting up, and the wire is now a thing that ruins everybody's sleep without protecting anything. Bramall's group had stopped rising to it well before the night that mattered. The fix he arrived at in the second year is the one worth copying: two lines, at different distances, with different sounds. The far one is information and you note it and stay in bed. The near one has never once been an animal, and when it goes everybody moves.

---

> "Leave the bodies in the street." — Fen Doherty, poacher, day 88

**Why it works.** It is the most unpleasant rule in this book and it is sound. A street with bodies in it reads, at a hundred yards, as a street that has already been done. The thing you most want in the second year is to be uninteresting to people. It does more than that. The smell covers yours, which matters, because smell is how they sort each other out. Doherty lived a whole winter on a terrace that four separate groups walked past, and one of them stopped at the end of it, and looked, and went the other way.

**How it goes wrong.** Rats came, and then everything rats bring, and by the second August the flies were a serious medical problem and two of his household had something they could not shake. There is also what it does to people to live in it, which he does not write about and which the people who were there do. And once, on his own doorstep in the second March, one of them was not a body. It had been lying in the same position for eleven days in the cold, doing nothing, because in the cold that is what they do, and it stood up when the thaw came. Cold ones are not dead ones. If you are going to use this rule, you put a spike through every head on your street. You do it in the frost, when it is safe to. And you write down how many there are.

---

> "The smoke, not the flame." — Duncan Ferrier, gamekeeper, day 60

**Why it works.** Everybody worries about the fire being seen and hardly anybody does the arithmetic on the smoke. At night a flame in a window is visible for two or three miles and you can cover a window. By day a column of smoke off a wet fire is visible for eight or nine, and you can cover nothing. In the first winter you could stand on the ridge above here at ten in the morning. And count the households. Ferrier's method is the one the estates used for a century. Burn dry hardwood. Burn it hot and small. Burn in the middle of the day, when the sky is already grey and the air is turbulent. Put it under a long cold flue that breaks the column up.

**How it goes wrong.** It gives you one hot meal a day, at a time chosen by the weather. It is very hard to run a household of children on a fire that is only lit under cloud. The second failure is the one that got him. His flue trick works because moving air pulls the plume apart. On the still bright morning of the fourth of December there was no moving air at all. The smoke went up out of that chimney in a rope you could see from Otley. He had used the same fire for a hundred days. Weather is not a background condition. It is a member of the household, and it gets a vote every morning.

---

> "The best wall is a bad approach." — Maggie Lunt, hill farmer, day 120

**Why it works.** You cannot build a wall. Be honest about the labour. A family, in a fortnight, with hand tools, cannot build anything that will hold against a serious push. Every hour spent on it is an hour not spent on the store and the crop. What you can do is choose ground where the approach does the work for free. A single track with a drop on one side. A spur with water on two. A slope steep enough that anything coming up it is slow and in the open and visible for four minutes. Lunt's place has one way in, uphill, in view of the house for three hundred yards, and she has never had to defend it because nothing has ever got near it unseen.

**How it goes wrong.** A bad approach is bad in both directions. Everything she owns has come up that track on her back or on a pony. In the second winter the snow filled the cut, and nothing came up or down it for five weeks. There was a woman in that house who needed a doctor. And good ground is legible to anybody who reads ground. Twice in these notebooks a settlement has been found by people with rifles, because the terrain around it was the terrain anybody would choose. That is a strange and specific way to be caught. It has happened more than once.

---

> "It is a house. Leave it." — Bea Winslow, retired postmistress, day 31

**Why it works.** She spent thirty days making a house in Norfolk safe, and it was good work. On the thirty-first she put four things in a bag, walked out of it and never went back. The water had gone bad and she could count. That is the whole content of the rule and it is the hardest sentence in this chapter to actually perform. A shelter you have worked on becomes a thing you are unwilling to lose, and the unwillingness arrives quietly and dressed as sense. By the time it is obvious, you are a person arguing that the water is probably fine. Everything you built was to keep you alive. When it stops doing that, it is timber.

**How it goes wrong.** Leaving is a real cost and not a free move. There is a kind of person who reads this rule and becomes unable to commit to anything, and drifts for three years accumulating nothing, and is thin. Winslow left one house in four years and settled the second time. The rule is not walk away easily. It is that the walking away must remain possible. The bag stays packed. The two exits stay clear. And the decision gets made on the evidence rather than on the labour already spent. She keeps the key to the Norfolk house on a string. She has never suggested going back for it.
