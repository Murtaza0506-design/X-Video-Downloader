# Chapter 9 — The Long Middle

Nobody wrote a rule for this part, because nobody expected to be in it.

Everything in the first eight chapters was written by people in an emergency, for people in an emergency. An emergency has a shape everybody understands without being told. It is short. It is survived rather than lived in, and the whole of your behaviour inside it is permitted by the fact that it is going to end. What none of us had any preparation for is the discovery that it was not going to end. The notebooks fall silent at almost exactly the eighteen month mark. This was not a period of difficulty between the world and the world coming back. It was simply the world now. And the habits that had kept us alive through a bad winter were, carried into a fourth year, going to hollow us out and leave a county full of thin capable people who could do everything except want anything.

I have read four hundred notebooks that stop for that reason. They do not stop at a death. They get shorter, the entries get further apart, the handwriting stays perfectly steady, and then there is a run of blank pages and then nothing, and the person is alive, and I have met eleven of them. That is what the long middle does. It is the only chapter in this book where the failure mode is not a body.

So these eleven rules are different from the rest and I want to be honest that some of them will look like luxuries. Planting a tree, teaching a child to read, holding a wedding, walking three weeks to look at the sea. None of that keeps anybody alive this Tuesday. Every one of them costs labour that could go into the wall or the store. The argument for them is that they are the only things anybody has found that keep a person interested in being alive next Tuesday. Interest in that is not a decoration on survival. It is the load-bearing part. The four hundred silent notebooks are the evidence.

This is the chapter people ask me for. When somebody comes to the Long Room and wants the book, they have almost always heard about one of these eleven and not about any of the other eighty-eight. I have thought about why. I think it is this. The rest of the manual tells you how not to die. This part is the only part that says anything at all about what the not dying was supposed to be for.

---

> "Count the ones still walking. Then count them again next year." — Yusuf Errai, veterinary surgeon, day 1,250

**Why it works.** This is the first piece of good news in the book and it has taken four years to be able to write it. They rot. Slowly, and much more slowly than anything else does, and not at all in the way a body rots. But they do. The fourth year is when it began to show. Hips going, tendons at the ankle failing, the ones that come down the lane now doing so with a gait that would not have got them across a field in the first summer. Errai has counted the same three miles of road every March for four years and the number has fallen each time, and the fall between the third year and the fourth was the largest. This is measurable, it is repeatable, and anybody can do it.

**How it goes wrong.** The number is falling and the danger is not. What is being removed from the population is the weak. What is left is a selected remainder: the ones that turned in the cold, that have spent four years in shade and damp, that are intact. Errai is careful about this and everybody who repeats him is not. Then the second thing, which he thinks is the more important and which nobody wants to hear. Any unburied cluster of a few hundred is still a few hundred. A hard winter preserves them. The arithmetic of a second wave has never once required anything new to happen. Count them. Publish the count. Do not build anything on the trend that you could not defend if the trend stopped.

---

> "Teach them to read." — Rosa Kaminski, schoolteacher, day 1,090

**Why it works.** It goes in one generation. That is the fact that ought to frighten people more than it does. A skill that took this country four hundred years to establish is gone in about fifteen if nobody teaches it. The children who were four when it started are eight now. There is a window and it closes. Everything in this book is a written thing. The whole method of the Long Room, and of Skipton, and of every settlement that has learned anything from another settlement's dead, runs on paper, and paper without readers is insulation. Kaminski runs two hours a day, six days, for nine children, and she has been doing it since the second winter and she has never missed a day for weather.

**How it goes wrong.** Two hours a day for nine children is two hours a day for one adult and nine sets of hands. In a settlement of thirty that is a real subtraction from the harvest. It is paid by everybody and it benefits the parents. There are settlements that have had this argument badly. The other cost is quieter and Kaminski raises it herself. Whoever teaches chooses what is taught, and there is nobody to check her. She has been the only source of what nine children think happened to the world. She has started having Quaye take one hour a week for exactly this reason, and she says he tells it differently, and that this was the point.

---

> "Plant a tree." — Maggie Lunt, hill farmer, day 800

**Why it works.** An apple takes seven years to be worth anything and that is the entire argument. Everything else a settlement does is inside one season. You plant in April to eat in September. You cut wood in January to burn in February. A life made only of those has a twelve month horizon, which is a survivable way to live and not a way to build anything. A tree is a wager on being here in seven years, made in public, with your hands. Lunt put in eleven in the second spring and says the change in the household was immediate and had nothing to do with fruit.

**How it goes wrong.** It is a commitment to a place and it is the strongest one available. This is the chapter four problem in its most seductive form. The household with the orchard is the household that will argue for staying when it should be arguing about the water. Her own first eleven went into the flat ground by the beck because it was the easiest digging. It is ground that floods. She lost nine of them in the second winter and did it again on the slope, where the work is four times as hard. Plant the tree. Plant it where you would build, not where it is easy, and plant it in the third year rather than the first, when you know what the ground does in February.

---

> "Marry people." — Sister Agnes Moray, day 1,000

**Why it works.** Her position is that a public promise, witnessed, is a piece of machinery and not a sentiment. A settlement is built out of them. They turn private arrangements into things the group has a stake in. They give everybody a role at somebody else's important day. And they are the only mechanism anybody has found for making a commitment that is harder to walk away from than the walking away is easy. She has done eleven. She will tell you, and the notebooks bear her out, that the settlements with weddings in them are the settlements with children in them, and that this is not the obvious causation but the other way round.

**How it goes wrong.** It builds households inside the settlement. Households have interests, and interests become factions. By the third year there was an argument at Moray's place about whose child got the schooling hours, and it had nothing to do with schooling. There is also the question of what happens when one of the eleven ends, which two of them have, and there is no machinery for that at all and she is making it up. She has refused two weddings in four years, both times because she judged one of the pair was asking for shelter rather than for a marriage. Both times she was told she had no right. She thinks now that she was correct once.

---

> "Learn a trade that is not survival." — Marta Kowal, day 900

**Why it works.** For three years everybody in this county did the same nine jobs and the nine jobs were all versions of not dying. What has changed in the fourth year is that there are now a handful of people who make one thing well. A man at Horsforth who does boots. A woman at Bramhope who is a potter. Kowal herself, who started as a hairdresser, pulled teeth, and has spent two years learning to do it properly from a book and from Errai. A settlement that contains a cobbler is a different kind of place from one that contains eleven interchangeable survivors, and the difference shows up in what people will walk twelve miles for, which is the beginning of everything else.

**How it goes wrong.** A specialist is a person who cannot be sent to the harvest, and in a settlement of twenty that is felt. There was a bad fortnight at Bramhope in the third September over precisely this and the potter went back on the rota and has done less good work since. The deeper fragility is that a trade takes years and the person carrying it is one person, which is Errai's chapter eight entry again, and Kowal has one apprentice and needs three. Her own note is worth quoting. She says the hardest part was not learning the teeth. It was being allowed to spend the time, and she was allowed it because the alternative was watching people die of it.

---

> "Go and look at the sea." — Hana Sørensen, marine engineer, day 1,200

**Why it works.** Sørensen's argument is that four years of errands is not a life, and that a journey with no cargo in it does something no amount of rest does. She is talking about a specific thing she watched happen to people. The horizon shrinks to the length of the water run and then does not come back. There is a practical half as well. Nobody in this valley knew anything about the coast until people started walking to it. What came back was worth the walking. There are working boats out of the Humber. The fishing is extraordinary. A whole different apocalypse happened out there and it has produced a whole different set of rules. Three weeks. Two people. Nothing carried but food.

**How it goes wrong.** It is three weeks of exposure for nothing material, in a world where three weeks of exposure is how most of the names in this index ended, and the honest version of the rule has to say that. And Sørensen went, in the fourth May, with a man called Bell who came back alone in June with a letter. She is not dead. She is on a boat out of Paull with eleven other people and she is not coming back. The letter says why. It is the best thing in the Long Room and I have read it more often than I should. Go and look at the sea. Understand before you go that some people who look at it do not return. That this is not always a tragedy. And that whoever is waiting for you will not be able to tell the difference for a long time.

> "Keep a bad map of the whole county." — Tomas Vrba, lorry driver, day 1,150

**Why it works.** What matters in the fourth year is not terrain, it is who is where. Vrba keeps a sheet with about forty marks on it: this settlement, that many people, they have salt, they need seed, this one has a smith, this one had a bad winter and will not answer. It is wrong in a dozen places at any moment and it does not need to be right, it needs to exist, because it turns forty isolated households into something that can move a surplus to a shortage. Three settlements got through the third February on grain that Vrba knew was sitting in a barn eleven miles from people who had none.

**How it goes wrong.** It is precisely the document you would want if you were planning to take things from people, and Vrba carries it on his person and has thought hard about what happens if he is stopped. He has been, once, and talked his way out of it. The second failure is subtler and it is the one Doherty warned about from the other direction. A map like this makes its keeper important. There is now one man who knows the shape of this county and nobody else does. If he goes into a ditch in February, forty households go back to knowing nothing. He is teaching it to two people. He has been teaching it to two people for a year and he is not very good at letting go of it.

> "Let the children be bored." — Ivy Okonjo, midwife, day 1,300

**Why it works.** The generation that is growing up in this is frighteningly competent. Reith is the obvious case and he is not unusual. They are quiet, they read ground, they are calm about things that reduce adults to uselessness. Every one of them has been useful since they were six, because there was no version of the last four years in which a child could be spared. Okonjo's finding, and she has had more of these children through her hands than anybody, is that the competence has come out of something, and what it has come out of is play. She has written a rule that says a child gets a portion of the day in which nothing is required of them and nothing is taught to them, and she defends it against everybody, including the children.

**How it goes wrong.** It is bought with adult labour and everybody can see the price, and in a settlement of fifteen with a wall to finish there is a version of this argument that Okonjo loses and should lose. And there is a harder objection which comes from the children themselves. Reith's generation is competent because nobody was ever bored. Several of them have said, in various ways, that they would not trade it. Being needed at eight was the thing that made the first winter survivable for them, rather than the thing that damaged them. Okonjo's answer is that she is not arguing about the first winter. She is arguing about the eighth, and about what happens to people who have never once been permitted to be no use.

---

> "Say their names on the day." — Wes Ordish, undertaker, day 1,000

**Why it works.** Grief that has nowhere scheduled to go turns up on its own account, in February, in the middle of a task, and it takes people out for a fortnight at a time and they cannot say why. Ordish's whole trade was built on the observation that the marking has to happen and that the only question is whether it happens on a day you chose. So the fourth of April, every year, everything stops. Every name this settlement has lost is read out, in order, by somebody different each time. There are a hundred and six of them. It takes most of an hour. Nobody has ever suggested shortening it.

**How it goes wrong.** It is a bad day. It is the worst day of the year in every settlement that does it. It costs a working day at exactly the point in April when there is work. Two people at Moray's place leave for the week every year and have done since the second one. Ordish does not pretend otherwise and does not think the cost is avoidable. He has made one refinement in three years. The reading happens in the morning and the afternoon is given over to a meal. He found that a day which ends in the list ends badly, and a day that begins with it does not.

---

> "Stop calling it the apocalypse." — Solomon Quaye, barber, day 1,320

**Why it works.** Language of emergency licenses behaviour of emergency, for as long as you let it. Quaye's point is that a settlement which still describes its situation as a catastrophe in progress will keep making catastrophe decisions. No long work. No repairs beyond the winter. No trees. Nothing that assumes a year after next. He started calling it the fourth year rather than the fourth year of anything, and made a small stubborn point of it in every conversation. He says it took about eight months to spread. The visible effect was people mending things properly instead of patching them.

**How it goes wrong.** It normalises what should not be normal. There is a real objection here that Moray makes and that I have not been able to dismiss. A hundred and six people are dead on that list. The thing that killed them is still walking about outside the wall. A language in which all of that is the weather is a language that has given up on something. There is also the chapter two problem. Some people are running on the belief that this is temporary, and it is load-bearing for them, and Quaye's sentence takes it out from under them in public. He now says it only to people who have been here four years. He has never once said it to anybody who arrived in the last twelve months, and that is the rule underneath the rule.

---

> "Put the book down." — the Long Room, the fourth winter

**Why it works.** I have spent two years on this and it is the last entry and it ought to be honest about what the other ninety-eight are worth. Nobody who survived the first year had this book. They had judgement, and luck, and mostly luck. What I have assembled here is ninety-eight rules written by people who were making it up, and the index will tell you how many of them are still here. I have no way of knowing whether the rules are why the others are alive, or whether the alive ones had more time to write. A manual is a substitute for judgement and it is a poor one. The purpose of reading this is to stop needing to. If you have got to the end of it and you are still looking things up, then I have made you worse rather than better. Shut it. Go and look at the ground in front of you.

**How it goes wrong.** The people who most need to put it down are the people who will not. I know this because they come to the Long Room and I can see it. What they want is not the rule. It is somebody else to have made the decision. I cannot make it. Every entry in here has a failure printed underneath it for that reason. I put them there so that nobody could read this book as a set of instructions. A great many people are going to read it as a set of instructions anyway. So the last thing, and then I will stop. Everything above is what worked in one valley, for one set of people, against one winter after another, and the sample is made entirely of survivors. Take what is useful. Argue with the rest of it. Write down what you learn, put a name and a date on it, and bring it here. If it is better than what is in this book, then this book will be shorter next year. That is the only outcome I have ever wanted from it.
