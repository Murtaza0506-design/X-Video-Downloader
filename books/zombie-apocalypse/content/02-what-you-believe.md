# Chapter 2 — What You Believe

More people in this index were killed by something they had been told than by anything that ever laid a hand on them.

That is not a figure of speech and it is not quite a statistic either, because I cannot audit a cause of death from a notebook, but I have counted what I can and it is not close. The bad information came in three kinds and they are worth separating, because they need three different defences. There was the rumour that moved people, which is the deadliest: a camp at Kendal, a working hospital at Harrogate, a ferry. There was the rumour about the biology, which killed slower and in larger numbers: that it was airborne, that fire frightened them, that running water stopped them, that a fever meant you were turning. And there was the rumour about other people, which is chapter seven's problem and which killed the fewest bodies and the most communities.

The thing that made all three so effective was that the ordinary machinery for checking a claim went in the first fortnight, and nobody noticed it going. For a few weeks the radio was still talking and it sounded like the machinery. It was not. It was three men in Skipton with a generator, reading out things that people had shouted at them. They were doing their honest best. They were wrong about the Kendal camp for eleven days, and about four hundred people walked north on the strength of it.

So the rules in this chapter are all, underneath, the same rule in different clothes. Find out who saw it. Everything else here is a technique for getting to that question faster, or for surviving the fact that you often cannot get to it at all.

One warning before you start. This is the chapter where the entries argue with each other hardest, and I have not smoothed that out. Entry 20 tells you to discount anything you cannot source. Entry 21 tells you that the best trick anybody found was discovered by nobody and has never been sourced by anyone. Both of those are in the book because both of them are true, and living inside that contradiction is most of what thinking is for now.

---

> "Write the date before you write anything else." — Nell Okafor, librarian, day 5

**Why it works.** Time stops behaving. Without a written date the weeks fold up, and the folding costs you three things that matter. You lose the count on a bite, which is the difference between watching somebody and burying them. You lose the season, and this is a book about seasons. And you lose the ability to put two accounts side by side and see which came first. Okafor started the Long Room ledger on the fifth day with the date and the weather and nothing else, because that is what a librarian does when the world ends. Everything in this book has a day count on it because of her.

**How it goes wrong.** She kept the record and did not keep herself. There is no rule in her hand about food and she was seven stone in the second November. Beyond that, dates become a fetish. A group at Otley ran a calendar that had drifted nine days by the second spring. They planted on a written date instead of on a hedge coming into leaf, and lost most of a crop to a frost that the ledger said was over. The date is a tool for comparing accounts. The hedge is the authority on the hedge.

---

> "A rumour that tells you where to go is a rumour somebody wants you to be." — Idris Pyle, day 61

**Why it works.** Ask what a piece of information does when it enters a hundred heads. Most true things do nothing much. But a rumour that moves people moves them all in one direction, at one time, along one road, carrying everything they own, and there has never been a shortage of men who find that arrangement convenient. Pyle knew because he had spent his working life in the trade. Three of the four big movements in this county in the first year had somebody standing at the far end of them. The Kendal camp is the one everybody knows. There was no camp. There had never been a camp.

**How it goes wrong.** He was right and he used it. Two of the four movements were started by Pyle. Then there is the cost to everyone who believed the rule too well. A family on Dewsbury Road turned down three true offers of shelter in one winter, because each of them told them where to go. Two of the four died a hundred yards from a house with a fire in it. Suspicion of good news is expensive and it is not free of charge just because it is clever. Ask who benefits, then go and look at the place from a distance, then decide. Do not stop at the first step and call it wisdom.

---

> "Believe the weather. Nothing else." — Jonah Kite, radio operator, day 12

**Why it works.** It is a rule about verifiability, and it has the merit of being usable by somebody with no training and no time. You can check a forecast by walking outside. You cannot check a report about Harrogate without going to Harrogate. Kite ran a set on car batteries from a bungalow at Guiseley and listened to everything that came over for eleven months. This is what he had to show for it. The weather was right about four times in five. Nothing else on the air was better than a coin.

**How it goes wrong.** There was one broadcast in the first year that was worth acting on, and it was not the weather. On the ninth of April a woman read out, four times, that the Aire valley was going to be opened up by a dam release, and Kite did not act, because it was not the weather. He lost his set and his bungalow and one of his two neighbours. A rule for filtering noise will filter the signal too, and it will do it on the one day of the year when that matters. Keep the rule and add a second sentence: anything that repeats itself four times gets a look out of the window.

---

> "Nothing you hear about them is true unless you can name the person who watched it happen." — Yusuf Errai, veterinary surgeon, day 30

**Why it works.** Look at what this rule killed. That it travels on the air. That fire frightens them, which cost a barn and nine people at Collingham. That running water stops them, which is a folk belief about something else entirely and which put two men in the Wharfe in February. That a fever means you are turning, which meant that in the first winter people were being put out of doors for having flu. Not one of those had a witness attached to it. Every one had a confident man attached to it. Errai's rule is the cheapest test there is, and it takes one question, and the question is who saw that.

**How it goes wrong.** The best thing anybody discovered has no author. Nobody knows who first worked out that they leave you alone if you smell enough like them. There is no witness, no notebook, no name. It works. It has saved more people than any other single fact in this manual. Errai's rule would have thrown it away. The rule is right about claims and wrong about techniques, and the difference is that a technique can be tested behind a wall by one person in an afternoon. Test what you can test. Doubt what you cannot.

---

> "Two sources or it does not go on the board." — Rob Hatherleigh, logistics corporal, day 44

**Why it works.** He ran a board in the Wetherby depot with three columns: heard, confirmed, acted on. Nothing crossed from the first column to the second without a second mouth that had not spoken to the first. It is the discipline of an operations room and it is the reason his group never once walked into an empty camp, in a year when four hundred people did. When a group is deciding whether to move eleven miles on a stranger's word, a board on a wall is worth more than any amount of good judgement in one man's head.

**How it goes wrong.** Two people repeating one rumour is one source wearing a coat, and telling the difference takes exactly the skill the board was built to replace. Worse is what it does to speed. On day 202 a boy came in and said the Aire had come over at Kirkstall, and there was nobody to confirm him. It sat in the first column for two days. By then eleven people were in the water. Hatherleigh added a line to the board that night, in different ink, and it is still there: a warning goes up on one source and comes down on two.

---

> "Ask them what they were doing when they saw it." — Marcus Okon, police sergeant, day 18

**Why it works.** It is the oldest question in the trade and it works because of how memory is built. A person who saw a thing was in the middle of something else at the time: carrying a box, arguing, looking for a dog. That detail comes back with the event attached to it and it comes back cheap and specific. A person who is repeating a story has no such detail, because there was no such afternoon. Okon separated true accounts from passed ones at the Hunslet gate for eight months, and it took him one question and about ten seconds.

**How it goes wrong.** Anybody good enough to be worth catching has thought about this. Pyle's accounts are full of the dog and the box, and they are full of them because he understood what the question is for. And the test punishes a particular kind of honest person: the shocked one, who has no detail at all, and whose account is flat and thin because it happened three hours ago to them and they have not slept. Twice in this book somebody was turned away at a gate for telling the truth badly. Use the question to find people to trust. Do not use it to find people to refuse.

---

> "The map is four years old. So are you." — Callum Reith, day 1,340

**Why it works.** Reith is the only person in this book who has spent more of his life in this than before it, and it makes him worth listening to on a subject none of the rest of us are honest about. A map is a claim about a place, made at a date, by somebody who is not there now. The Kirkstall terraces are a field. Two viaducts are down. There is a lake where the retail park was, and it is on no map and it is nine feet deep. He walks with a map and treats it as a rumour with contours.

**How it goes wrong.** He went the other way with it and stopped believing written things at all, which is a young man's error and an expensive one. In the third summer he walked into the works at Knostrop, which is marked, clearly, on the four year old map, as a sewage treatment plant. He came out with something in a cut on his shin that nearly took the leg, and he was carried nine miles by two people who could ill afford the day. A map is out of date about buildings. It is not out of date about the ground, or the water, or where the poison was.

---

> "Say that you do not know." — Priya Raghunathan, A&E nurse, day 96

**Why it works.** She came out of a trade where the words are said out loud and where saying them is a mark of competence, and she brought the habit with her into a world running on men who were certain. Here is the mechanism. A group can absorb the news that its leader does not know something. What it cannot absorb is discovering, later, that the leader did not know and said otherwise, because after that nothing that person says can be spent. Uncertainty said out loud is cheap. Uncertainty found out is the end of your authority in one afternoon.

**How it goes wrong.** There is a moment when a group needs a decision more than it needs an accurate account of your confidence. She hit it at Wetherby in the second January, with fourteen people standing in sleet waiting to be told which way. She told them what she did not know, at length, and she was completely honest, and they stood in it for forty minutes and two of them lost fingers. Say you do not know, then say what you are going to do anyway. The second half is not optional and it is the half she left out.

---

> "Anybody certain about the biology has never watched one turn." — Nadia Rahman, pharmacist, day 40

**Why it works.** In the first spring every third man was an expert. The confidence was inverse to the exposure, exactly and reliably, and it is the cleanest example of the effect I have ever seen. The people who had sat with somebody through the six days would not tell you the shape of it, and would say the thing about how it varies, and were hedging because the world was hedging. Rahman had counted out other people's medicines for nineteen years and had a professional's ear for a man who has read one thing.

**How it goes wrong.** It disarms the person who did the work. Errai had the transmission route correct on day three, from evidence, and was dismissed for months by people quoting Rahman's rule at him, because he was certain and certainty was the tell. She has said since that it is the thing she regrets. Doubt a man's certainty, then ask him what it is built on, and if the answer is a pattern he counted himself then he is not the problem. The rule catches the confident. It does not distinguish between the confident and the correct, and you have to do that part yourself.

---

> "Keep a book of what you were wrong about." — the Skipton radio log, day 150

**Why it works.** Three men with a generator broadcast the Kendal camp for eleven days and about four hundred people walked north on it. What they did afterwards is the reason they are in this manual. They kept a second book, read out every Sunday, of everything they had said that had turned out to be false. By the second year they were the only voice on the air that anybody in three counties trusted. You cannot get better at judging a claim without a record of your own misses, because memory quietly deletes them. Write them down and the deleting stops.

**How it goes wrong.** A list of a man's errors, in his own hand, is a weapon lying in the open. Skipton's book was used in the second winter to break the youngest of the three operators in front of a room of forty people, by somebody who wanted his position, and it worked, and he left. If you keep the book, keep it for one person. A group's book of its own errors is a fine idea and I have never once seen it survive a disagreement about food.

---

> "You are allowed to believe something because you need to." — Sister Agnes Moray, day 300

**Why it works.** Four years is a long time to run on evidence alone and I have not met anybody who managed it. Moray's position, and she is careful about it, is that a belief has two properties and they are separate: whether it is true, and what it does to the person holding it. The man on Tong Road who is sure his daughter reached Ireland gets up in the morning. The one who worked out the odds does not. She is not telling you the odds are wrong. She is telling you that you are a piece of equipment that has to keep working, and that the maintenance of it is a legitimate thing to spend belief on.

**How it goes wrong.** The moment a belief starts costing anybody anything, it has stopped being maintenance and become a plan. Eleven people walked to Holyhead in the second autumn on a belief of this kind and four came back. And the sentence is the exact sentence that a certain sort of man says at the beginning of the thing he builds around himself. There have been two of those in this county and one of them is still going. Moray's own guard against it is worth copying. She holds one belief this way, she knows which one it is, she can name it, and she has never once asked anybody else to hold it with her.
