# Chapter 7 — Other People

I have put off writing this chapter for two winters and I want to say why, because the reason is the argument.

Every set of rules anybody has ever pinned to a wall about strangers is a rule for getting the answer wrong in a chosen direction. That is all a rule of this kind can be. You are being asked to decide, in about four seconds, on almost no evidence, whether the person at the end of the lane is a danger. There is no method that gets that right. Anybody who tells you there is has either been lucky or has stopped counting the people they turned away. So what you are choosing is not accuracy. You are choosing which mistake you would rather make, over and over, for the rest of your life. Make that choice in daylight. The alternative is having it made for you at nine at night by whoever is most frightened.

Here is the count, for what it is worth, and it is worth something. In these eleven hundred notebooks I can identify four hundred and six encounters with strangers described in enough detail to classify. Eleven of them ended in somebody being robbed or killed by the stranger. Twenty-nine ended in the stranger being killed or driven off by people who turned out to have been in no danger at all. The rest were nothing: a wave, a trade, a night in a barn, a piece of information, a person who walked on. If you are going to be wrong, and you are, the numbers say which way. You are far more likely to harm somebody who came to you for help than to be harmed by them.

That is not an argument for opening the door. Eleven is not nothing, and two of the eleven took whole households. It is an argument for knowing what you are trading, and for not letting the fear do the arithmetic. The fear has been wrong about the size of the risk for four years running, and in this county the wrongness has been fatal.

The entries here disagree more sharply than anywhere else in the book. Rule 70 says the door does not open, and the man who wrote it has buried people who opened theirs. Rule 77 was written by a woman who has opened hers every time she has been asked, for four years, and who is alive, and who is not naive, and whose settlement is the largest in this valley. Both of them are here in full and I am not going to arbitrate between them.

---

> "They have decided about you before either of you has spoken." — Idris Pyle, day 200

**Why it works.** Pyle spent his working life on the first four seconds and he is right about what is in them. Before a word, the person opposite has read five things. Your condition, which is how well you have been eating and sleeping. Your posture, which is whether you expect trouble. What is in your hands. Whether you are looking at theirs. Whether you are alone. All of that is legible at forty yards and none of it can be talked away afterwards. His practical point is that you may as well choose what it says. Stand square, hands visible and empty, look at the face and not the hands, and stop walking before you are asked to.

**How it goes wrong.** He is describing a performance, and a performance costs something to run and can be read as a performance by anybody who has met one. There is a particular flatness that very careful people acquire, and in the third year it has become its own signal in this county, and it is not a reassuring one. Pyle also had four years of this and ended up unable to meet anybody without working, which is a way of being profoundly alone in a crowd. Do the hands and the stopping, which are courtesies and cost nothing. Leave the rest of it. Most people are not reading you nearly as closely as he was.

---

> "Meet them at a distance you can both walk away from." — Marcus Okon, police sergeant, day 210

**Why it works.** Forty yards of open ground with a clear line out behind each of you is a geometry that lets a meeting end in nothing, and most meetings should end in nothing. Close the distance and you have taken that option off the table for both of you: now something has to be resolved, and it has to be resolved by two frightened parties standing within reach of each other. Okon ran gate meetings on this principle for two years without an incident. Space is not coldness. Space is what makes it possible for the other person to change their mind and leave without losing face, and losing face is what most of the eleven bad outcomes in these notebooks are actually made of.

**How it goes wrong.** Forty yards of open ground reads as hostility to a family that has walked eleven miles with a child, and it reads that way because it is, a little. There is a woman in Farsley who will tell you about being held at that distance in the rain for twenty minutes while a good, careful man decided about her. It ended her willingness to ask anybody for anything, and it has not come back. And the distance that is safe from a person is the distance a rifle prefers. Okon's own amendment is that the geometry is for the first minute and not the twentieth, and that somebody has to be willing to close it, and that it should be you.

---

> "Trade first. Talk after." — Tomas Vrba, lorry driver, day 150

**Why it works.** An exchange of two small things establishes more in ninety seconds than an hour of conversation, and it does it in a way nobody can fake, because a trade is a claim that gets tested immediately. It says: I have a surplus, I would rather have a relationship than your possessions, and I expect to see you again. Vrba traded salt in half-ounces up and down this valley for two years, and built out of it a network of eleven households that fed information to each other. He did it without once having a conversation about trust. It is a word that does no work at all.

**How it goes wrong.** A trade shows your hand. Salt in half-ounces says you have a lot of salt, and by the second summer everybody between here and Otley knew which man to rob if you wanted salt, and two of them tried. It also sets a price on your household, and a household with a known surplus is a fixed asset in the sense of chapter six. Vrba's fix is the one the old markets used: trade at a third place, on a day, in small quantities, and never at either party's home. He has not been robbed since he started doing that, and he says the network works better because everybody has to walk.

---

> "The door does not open. There is no version where the door opens." — Rob Hatherleigh, logistics corporal, day 240

**Why it works.** He has the eleven cases and he is the only person who has written them all up. The pattern in them is consistent and it is worth having in front of you. In nine of the eleven, the door was opened on purpose, to somebody who was asking for help, by somebody who had decided this one was different. In seven of those nine the person asking was in real distress, was alone, and had walked a long way. They were also being followed by four people who had sent them. Hatherleigh's argument is that a rule with an exception in it is not a rule, because the exception is the only door anybody has ever come through. Food goes out. People do not come in. He has run it for three years and lost nobody.

**How it goes wrong.** Twenty-nine against eleven. That is the number from the top of this chapter and it is the whole case against him. He has read it and does not dispute the arithmetic. He says the twenty-nine are not his responsibility and the eleven are. What his household has become in three years is the part he does not write about and other people do. They are fed. They are safe. They are eight people who have not admitted anybody since the second spring. Three have left. The ones who left all left for the same reason and said so. There is a cost to being the household that everybody in the valley knows will not open. It does not show up as a death. It shows up as nobody coming when it is your turn.

---

> "The dangerous ones are not hungry." — Callum Reith, day 1,100

**Why it works.** Read the condition and not the manner. A desperate person is frightened, unpredictable and mostly harmless. Desperation is loud and slow, and it wants food rather than a fight. Almost every person who has frightened a household in these notebooks by shouting at a gate turned out to be exactly what they looked like. The ones who took whole settlements were fed, rested, unhurried and polite, and they were fed and rested because they had been doing it for a while. Reith's rule inverts the instinct, which is to fear the wild-looking man, and the instinct has been wrong nearly every time.

**How it goes wrong.** It is a heuristic and he used it as a licence. A group that is well fed and organised is, more often than not, a working settlement that has planted something. Reith spent his seventeenth year in a crew that used exactly this reasoning to choose who to take from. He has written about it. I have printed the entry with the rule that came out of it. That is the failure of this rule: it identifies the strong, and there are two things you can do with that identification, and one of them is what he did. He is twenty now and he grows things. He asked me to leave this in.

---

> "Children are not a trick." — Ivy Okonjo, midwife, day 190

**Why it works.** The rumour that children were being used as bait went through three counties in the first autumn, and it did more harm than almost anything else on the air. Okonjo went looking for the cases. She found two, in eleven hundred notebooks, in four years. Set that against what the rumour cost. Households that would not answer a child at a gate. A boy at Elland Road who is in chapter five. And a lasting willingness to treat the most vulnerable person in front of you as the most likely threat. The base rate is the argument. It is not sentiment, it is counting, and she did the counting.

**How it goes wrong.** It happened twice. Both cases are documented, both were in the first eighteen months, and in one of them a household of six was finished by it. Okonjo does not hide either case and her position is that a rule which is right ninety-nine times in a hundred is still a rule that gets somebody killed, and that anybody adopting it should know they are accepting that. What she does in practice is the thing to copy. The child comes in. The child is fed. One person stands where they can see the road for the next two hours. Nobody makes the child answer questions about who is with them.

---

> "Lie about the number. Never lie about the food." — Idris Pyle, day 260

**Why it works.** His argument is that a household's apparent size is the main thing deterring anybody, and that it cannot be verified from outside in a short observation. The food can be. So you inflate the one, tell the truth about the other, and keep your credibility where it matters. He ran it for a year. It sounds right and it is beautifully put. It is one of the two rules of his that I have marked in the note at the back. The evidence runs the other way, and I want it in the book with the correction attached rather than left out.

**How it goes wrong.** Anybody serious watches for two days, and two days of watching gives them your real number, your rota and your water run, and now they know you lie. Worse is the thing Pyle did not model. A large household implies a large store, and in the second winter the two households in these papers that had most successfully inflated their numbers were both attacked, and both were attacked because they looked big enough to be worth it. The rule optimises for deterring one man walking past and it advertises to the four men who are planning. Tell the truth about the number and let it be small. Small and known to be defended is a much better position than large and interesting.

---

> "Ask them for something small." — Ruth Vellacott, day 220

**Why it works.** Vellacott's people have been doing versions of this for three hundred years, and it works on something old in us. A person who has done you a small favour is better disposed towards you afterwards. The effect is larger than the favour. So you do not open with an offer, which puts them in debt and makes them wary, and you do not open with a large request, which is a demand. You ask for water, or the road, or the time of the tide. It is nearly free for them, it gives them the position of the person with something to give, and it turns a confrontation into a transaction that has already gone well once.

**How it goes wrong.** It marks you as somebody who asks, and in a bad winter that is a category people are watching for. There is also a real cost hidden by the word small. A household that has nothing is being asked to demonstrate that it has nothing, in front of a stranger. Vellacott has been on the receiving end of that and describes it as one of the worse things that has happened to her. Ask for something they obviously have. Directions, water in a place with water, a light. Never ask a thin household for food and call it small.

---

> "Never show them the way home." — Fen Doherty, poacher, day 175

**Why it works.** Everything about your household that matters is its location, and location is the one thing that cannot be undone once it is given. Doherty meets people on a lane half a mile from where he lives, walks doglegs, comes back after dark by a different way, and has never in four years let anybody follow him in. It costs him an hour a week. In exchange, the eleven encounters in this chapter that ended badly all had one thing in common. Somebody knew where the house was. No defence, no wall and no rota competes with not being findable.

**How it goes wrong.** He is alive and his household is four people and it was nine. Nobody has ever come for them and nobody has ever come to them either. When the roof went in the second February there was no one to ask. When his sister was ill there was no one to ask. The settlements that grew in this valley grew because people knew where they were. Doherty's method is the correct method for a household that intends to stay four people forever. It is not a method for building anything, and he is the first to say so, and he has said it to me twice in a way that I have thought about since.

---

> "Give the first stranger of the winter something." — Maggie Lunt, hill farmer, day 300

**Why it works.** Reputation travels faster than anybody expects and it is the cheapest defence there is. What Lunt wants said about her place, up and down this valley, is two sentences: they will give you a meal at the gate, and it is not worth trying anything. Both halves are necessary and the first is the one people neglect. A place known to give a little is a place nobody needs to raid, because raiding is dangerous and asking is not. In four years she has fed perhaps sixty people at that gate. She has been approached with any other intention exactly once.

**How it goes wrong.** It produces a queue, and a queue is a fixed asset with a number on it. In the second winter she had forty people at that gate over eleven weeks, and she could not feed forty people. The ones she turned away went off with a much worse story than if she had never fed anybody at all. Generosity that runs out is remembered as a refusal. Her fix is to set the amount for the season in advance, publish it, and hold it. A meal at the gate and a night in the byre. That is what it is and what it has always been. Nobody who arrives in February is told something different from the man who arrived in October.

---

> "Open the door." — Sister Agnes Moray, day 400

**Why it works.** She has opened it every time she has been asked, for four years, and her settlement is the largest in this valley and it is not an accident. The reasoning is not what people assume. It is not that everybody is good. It is that a settlement is made of people who arrived. Every single person now feeding her, mending her roof and standing her watches was at some point a stranger at the gate. A place which does not open cannot grow. A place that cannot grow is on a slow clock, and it runs out somewhere around the fifth or sixth winter, when the people in it get old. The twenty-nine against eleven is her arithmetic. She got there before I did.

**How it goes wrong.** It has cost her. Twice. The second time, in the third autumn, it cost her four people and most of a store. She opened the door again eleven days later. There are people in that settlement who have never forgiven her and are still there. What makes it work is not the opening, which is the part that gets quoted. It is the four things underneath it, and she lists them every time and nobody ever writes them down. The door opens into a yard and not into the house. Somebody is always standing where they can see the yard. Nobody sleeps inside the walls on their first night. And the decision to open is hers alone, so that it can never become a thing the household argues about at the gate with a person listening. Open the door. Build the yard first.
