# How to Use This Book

This is a manual of ninety-nine rules for staying alive, assembled from the papers of people who for the most part did not.

Each entry has four parts. The rule, in the words somebody wrote it down in. Who kept it, what they did before, and what day they were on when they wrote it. Then two paragraphs that are mine: why the rule works, which is the reasoning, and how it goes wrong, which is the body.

**The last paragraph is the point of the book.** Anybody can tell you to keep quiet and stay off the roads. You know that. What nobody tells you is the precise circumstance in which keeping quiet gets your whole group killed, and there is one, and it is in chapter five. A rule with no stated failure is not a rule. It is a slogan. Slogans are most of why the first winter went the way it did.

**The rules disagree with each other, and I have left them disagreeing.** Rule 70 says the door does not open, ever, and the man who wrote it has buried people who opened theirs. Rule 77 was written by a woman who has opened hers every time she has been asked, for four years, and who is alive, and whose settlement is the largest in this valley. Both are in here because both are true, in different weeks, to different people, and a manual that never contradicts itself has been tidied into uselessness. When two entries pull against each other I say so and I name the conditions. Deciding between them is yours. That is not me avoiding the question. It is the question.

**Almost nobody in this book was a fool.** The genre before all this was full of collections that assumed otherwise, and they were worthless then and they would get you killed now. The man who welded the fire doors was a locksmith of thirty years and he was reasoning correctly about intrusion. The group that shot first had buried four people who had not. If your reaction to an entry is that the person was an idiot, the entry has failed and you should read the second paragraph again.

**What you need to know about them, in seven lines.** It passes in saliva, into blood, by a bite. Not by air, not by water, not on your hands. They do not eat, which is the fact everything else hangs from: they bite once and walk on, so almost everyone bitten lives long enough to turn, which is why there are so many of them and so few of us. Turning takes between four hours and nine days. They are slow, about four miles an hour, and they never stop, and the difference between those two facts is the whole of chapter three. They hunt by sound, then by movement, then by smell, and they use smell to tell each other apart. They run on the air temperature: below four degrees they are slow, in a hard frost they stand still, and in August they are quick and they rot. The brain stops them, or the spine above the shoulders, or enough broken frame that the legs will not carry them.

**Learn the seasons before you learn the weapons.** Everyone wants the chapter on killing them. There is no chapter on killing them, because killing them is a thing you do when the rest of the manual has already failed. Winter is the working season. Summer is the season you get through. The single most useful sentence I can give you is that a plan made in February will kill you in July, and half the graves I have dug were dug for people who made one good plan and kept it.

**How it is arranged.** Three parts, by the problem in front of you. The First Week covers the first hour, what to believe, and moving. The Body and the Building covers doors, noise, and the unglamorous business of water, food and teeth. Everyone Else covers other people, your own group, and the long middle, which is what we are in now and what nobody wrote a rule for, because nobody expected to get here.

**Chapter nine is the reason for the other eight.** It is about the fourth year. It has the fewest deaths in it and it is the chapter people ask me for. Read it last, or read it first if this week has been bad.

**On me.** I run the Long Room, which was a subscription library in Beeston and is now a store of paper, seed and about eleven hundred notebooks. I did not write these rules. I collected them, checked them where checking was possible, and wrote the two paragraphs underneath. Where an entry is my own I have signed it. Where I have reconstructed a rule from somebody who could not write it down themselves, the note at the back says so.

The Long Room, Beeston. The fourth winter.
