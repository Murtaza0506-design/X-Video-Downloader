# Chapter 6 — Water, Food, and Teeth

Nobody wants this chapter. It is the one people skip and it is the one that would have saved the most of them.

Go through eleven hundred notebooks and count what is written on the last page that anybody wrote, and a picture comes out that nobody in the first year would have believed. After the first winter, the things killing people in this county are these, in order. Cold, dirty water, teeth and jaws, wounds that were not cleaned, other people. And then, a long way down, them. That order has held for three years. There is a whole literature in these papers about how to put a spike through a skull. There are four pages in total about how to keep your mouth from killing you. The skulls are not what is in the graves.

The reason is simple enough. Every one of us came out of a world where water arrived clean, food arrived safe, waste went away and a bad tooth was an appointment. None of those were skills. They were services. They went in a fortnight. What replaced them was a set of jobs that are dull, daily, unforgiving and completely unglamorous. Nobody has ever once been thanked for doing them properly. They will not tolerate being done four days out of five. A well dug in the wrong place does not fail dramatically. It fails in August, to everybody, at once.

The last four entries in this chapter are about wounds and about bites, and I have put them here rather than in a chapter of their own for a reason. A bite is a medical event. It is not a moral one, it is not a supernatural one, and it is not the beginning of a story. It is a wound with a known course, a known timescale and a known outcome. Treating it as anything else is how families end up doing things in barns that they cannot afterwards live with. Read those four cold. They were written cold, by people who had done it more than once, and two of them are the most careful writing in this book.

---

> "Boil it. If you did not watch it boil, boil it." — Gwen Prydderch, water engineer, day 70

**Why it works.** A minute at a rolling boil finishes everything biological that this country has to offer, and biological is what kills you. All the dysentery in these notebooks. The thing that went through the Kirkstall camp in the second July and took nineteen people in nine days. Prydderch's second sentence is the one that matters. Water that somebody says is boiled is not boiled. The failures are all social rather than technical. The person who let it off at a simmer was tired and it was late and it looked like it was going. She ran one pot, one fire, one named person a day, and that household has not had a case in three years.

**How it goes wrong.** Boiling is expensive and it fixes only one of the two problems. It costs wood, which costs cutting, which is noise and exposure, and a household of six needs eighteen litres a day and that is a real fire every day forever. It does nothing to what came out of the works at Knostrop, or off the fields, or out of a tank with a dead pigeon and four years of lead in it. And boiled water in a warm store is fine for two days and is not sterile after that. It goes back to being ordinary water the moment it is standing in a bucket in a kitchen. Boil it, cover it, and use it inside two days.

---

> "The latrine goes downhill of the water and further away than you want it." — Ivy Okonjo, midwife, day 45

**Why it works.** Thirty yards and downhill of anything you drink, and that number is not a superstition, it is roughly how far it takes ordinary ground to do the filtering. Everything Prydderch's entry is about gets into the water at this one point. The camps that failed in the first two years failed here more than anywhere else. They failed within about six weeks of setting up, every time, in the same way. Okonjo had delivered babies in places with no plumbing. She knew that the whole of public health, when you take the pipes away, is one hole in the right place and a rule about hands.

**How it goes wrong.** A latrine thirty yards away is not used at three in the morning in February by a woman with dysentery. That is not a failure of character. It is arithmetic about a person who is ill in the dark and cold. What happens instead is behind the shed, ten feet from the door, which is worse than having no rule at all because nobody is admitting to it. Okonjo's own correction took her a year to write down. The rule has two halves and everybody publishes the first. The hole goes thirty yards downhill, and a covered bucket goes by the door. The bucket is emptied into the hole by whoever is up first, and nobody is ever asked about it.

---

> "Perishables first. The tins are for February." — Rob Hatherleigh, logistics corporal, day 20

**Why it works.** It is the order every quartermaster has used and it is right for the reason that is easy to miss. You are not choosing what to eat. You are choosing what to throw away. Anything with a short life is either eaten now or lost entirely, and a tin loses nothing by waiting a year. So the fresh goes first, then the dry, then the tinned, and the shape of the store you end up with in January is the shape you would have designed. Hatherleigh's depot came out of the first winter with four months in hand when three groups within five miles were eating candles.

**How it goes wrong.** It assumes there is a February and that you are in it. Two households in these papers ate to a plan for a spring they did not reach, and left stores behind them that other people are eating now. That is not the worst outcome in the book. It is not what they were planning. The subtler failure is what it does to the autumn. Heavy work happens in September and October. The plan has you eating the thin end of the perishables then, and the good tinned fat in February when you are sitting still. That is backwards for the body doing the work. Feed the season you are in. Move about a fortnight of the good food forwards into the digging.

---

> "Salt, fat, and something green." — Ade Lawal, chef, day 60

**Why it works.** These are the three things a scavenged diet lacks and each one kills differently. Without fat you can eat rabbit until your jaw aches and starve doing it, which happened to at least two people in these notebooks and which reads like nonsense until you understand it. Without salt you cannot work in July and cannot keep anything through October. Without something green you have about three months before your gums go and your old wounds open. There were people in the second March who thought they had been bitten and had scurvy. Lawal knew every one of these from the trade and he wrote the rule so that a person who could not cook could still eat correctly.

**How it goes wrong.** Something green in February costs more than it returns. Going out for four hours in the cold for a handful of nettles that are not there yet is a bad trade in calories and a worse one in exposure. The answer is not the winter forage. It is the autumn one, dried and hung. His own household's failure was different and it is a professional's failure. He had them cooking, beautifully, every day, and not preserving. They came out of the second autumn with the best food anybody had eaten in a year, and eleven weeks of store where they should have had twenty.

---

> "Plant more than you can eat and less than you can weed." — Maggie Lunt, hill farmer, day 200

**Why it works.** Everybody who has never grown anything believes the constraint is seed and it is not, it is labour in June. A plot that is twice what you can keep on top of does not give you twice the food. It gives you a field of weeds with some potatoes in it, and it takes the same ground and the same water. Lunt's rule sets the size by the bottleneck rather than by the ambition. The households up this valley that took it seriously are the ones with a store. The ones that dug up half a hillside in the first optimistic April fed the rabbits.

**How it goes wrong.** A crop is a fixed asset and a beacon. It is visible from a distance, it cannot be moved, it announces the number of people it is meant to feed, and it ripens on a date that anybody watching can also read. Two settlements in these notebooks were robbed at exactly the right week by people who had done nothing all year but wait. Then there is the ordinary agricultural disaster. She went into the second year on potatoes, because potatoes are the calories, and the blight came through in August. She has planted four things every year since and expects to lose one of them.

---

> "Learn one animal properly." — Isaac Bramall, beekeeper, day 40

**Why it works.** A person who half knows chickens, rabbits, goats and bees has four things that will die on him and no idea which. A person who knows one, in detail, through a full year, has a thing that produces food without being hunted for. It turns grass or scraps or flowers into calories while he is asleep. Bramall had kept bees for thirty years and got honey, wax for light, and a swarm to trade, out of hives he did not have to feed at all. The knowledge is the asset. Everybody can acquire the animal.

**How it goes wrong.** One animal is one bad winter away from nothing, and his was the second one. He lost every colony he had between December and February, to cold and to a mite he had no treatment for. A man who has spent two years being the person with the bees turns out to have no second thing. And no time to learn one at fifty-eight. He is alive because his neighbours fed him through a spring. Know one animal properly. Then spend one afternoon a month being an amateur at a second, and be content to be bad at it. Bad at two is the position Bramall would have wanted in that February.

---

> "A tooth will kill you sooner than a bite." — Marta Kowal, hairdresser, day 240

**Why it works.** Count them in the notebooks. After the cold and the water, the next thing on the list is jaws, and it is a long way ahead of anything with teeth of its own. An abscess in a lower molar has a short road into the floor of the mouth and the neck. From there it is days. There is no surgeon and no antibiotic worth the name in this county. The diet does not help: nothing sweet any more, which people think saves them, but everything is gritty and acid and hard, and enamel goes. Kowal's regime is a rag, salt and two minutes, every night, for everybody, forever, and she checks mouths on the first Sunday of the month like a schoolteacher.

**How it goes wrong.** She began pulling teeth in the second summer because there was nobody else, and she is a hairdresser, and she says so on the page in a way I have not softened. Eleven extractions. Three went badly, one of those very badly, and a woman who had a treatable abscess in March had a broken root and a worse infection in April. The rule also drives people towards the pliers too early, because a rule that says teeth kill makes every ache an emergency. Clean the mouth every day and you will not need the pliers. That is the entry. The pulling is not the technique, it is the confession of the technique having failed.

---

> "Six days, in a room with a door that opens from the outside." — Yusuf Errai, veterinary surgeon, day 75

**Why it works.** Turning takes between four hours and nine days and the spread is not random. It goes with body mass and with how much got in. A small child bitten badly is hours. A heavy man with a graze is most of a week. That is the whole of the science and it is enough to act on. Errai's protocol is the one that has held up. The bitten person goes in a room alone, with water, with light, with the door bolted on the outside and a hatch. Somebody sits with them and talks to them through it. Nobody is tied up. The last part is not sentiment. Restraint has killed people in these papers who were never infected at all, of thirst, of cold, and once of a fire.

**How it goes wrong.** Six is not nine. He chose six because six covers most cases and because a household cannot hold a room and a watcher for nine days. He has written, twice, that this was a compromise with the labour available and not a finding. Two people in these notebooks came out of that room on the sixth day and turned on the seventh, and one of those took three others with him. And the protocol is unbearable in a way that no writing conveys. It is a rule for a stranger. Almost nobody has ever managed to perform it on their own child, and I am not going to pretend the failure rate there is anything other than total.

---

> "Cut it off." — Priya Raghunathan, A&E nurse, day 88

**Why it works.** It is in the book because it works sometimes and because a rumour that it never works has cost lives. There are eleven amputations in these notebooks, all of them below a knee or an elbow, all within a few minutes of the bite. Three of those people are alive and none of the three turned. The reasoning is that it does not travel instantly and a limb removed above the wound at speed can take the whole of it. That is the entire basis. It is thinner than anybody would like. Raghunathan wrote it out as a procedure, with a tourniquet, a time and an order of operations. If it is going to be done at all, it will be done by somebody who is not a nurse.

**How it goes wrong.** Eight of the eleven died. They died of blood loss and of shock and, two of them, of the wound going bad a fortnight later in a house with no way of stopping it. Worse than that number is what sits behind it. At least two of the eight were bitten through a coat and a shirt, and there is a real chance neither of them was ever infected. They had a leg taken off in a kitchen on a maybe. Nobody can tell in the first two minutes whether skin was broken, and the first two minutes is the entire window. Raghunathan's own note is the honest one and I will not improve on it. This is a procedure with three survivors and eight graves, and the survivors are the ones who tell you about it.

---

> "Take the boring shelf." — Nadia Rahman, pharmacist, day 300

**Why it works.** By the second year every obvious thing had gone from every shop in this county and the boring shelf was untouched, and it still is. Salt, bicarbonate, vinegar, plain flour, dried pulses, sugar in ten kilo bags, TCP, sanitary goods, needles, reading glasses, boot laces, matches, and every tin of anything nobody has heard of. Rahman's insight is about the psychology of a hurried person. People scavenge for a meal, not for a year. What survives four years of stripping is exactly the material that does not look like dinner. Half the useful stock in the Long Room came off boring shelves in the third summer.

**How it goes wrong.** She spent three days on a chemist at Guiseley in the second November. It was a serious piece of work with a real risk in it. She came out with almost nothing that mattered. Most of what is in a pharmacy needs a diagnosis you cannot make, a cold chain you do not have, or a course you cannot complete. The shelf that would have paid for those three days had the salt and the plasters on it. It was in the shop next door. She walked past it twice. The boring shelf rule is right and it is much broader than she wrote it. It is not only the dull items in the interesting shop. It is the dull shop.

---

> "Eat it now." — Bea Winslow, retired postmistress, day 700

**Why it works.** A store that is never drawn on is not a store, it is a museum, and Winslow has watched three households guard one into the spring while the people guarding it got thin. Her rule has two halves and both are permission. The first is that food kept past its usefulness is a loss, so the jar that is turning gets opened on a Tuesday for no reason. The second is harder. A meal that is better than it needs to be, on a bad day, for no occasion, is not an indulgence and it is not weakness. It is maintenance on the only machine you have got. She keeps a shelf of unreasonable things and spends off it about once a month.

**How it goes wrong.** Somewhere past the end of this rule is the seed corn. It has been eaten, twice that I know of, by settlements that panicked in a February that turned out to be over in eleven days. Both of them are gone. The line between drawing on the store and eating next year is not obvious from inside a hungry winter, and there is no formula for it. So Winslow does the thing that works instead. The seed and one month's food live in a different building, counted, by two people, and are not part of the decision at all. Eat what is on the shelf, freely, and put the future somewhere your hungry self cannot reach it.
