# Chapter 8 — The Group

Nearly every settlement that has failed in this valley failed from the inside, in the autumn, over something small.

I did not expect that when I started sorting the notebooks and I have had to change my mind about it twice. The picture from outside is always the same: a place was overrun, a place was raided, a place had bad luck. Read the papers from inside and the overrunning turns out to be the last event in a sequence that started nine months earlier, with two people who stopped speaking, and a rota that everybody knew was unfair and nobody would say so. A decision that got made three times because it never got made properly once. By the time anything with teeth arrived there were four people doing the work of eleven and nobody on the north wall. The thing that finished them would not have got within half a mile of the place it had been in March.

So this chapter is about governance, which is a heavy word for what it is. It is a set of answers to about six questions. Every group of more than four people has to answer them, whether it notices or not. Who decides. How the unpleasant work is shared. What happens to somebody who cannot work. What happens when somebody wants to leave. Who counts the food. What is done when a person dies. Groups that answered those out loud, early, in writing, while nothing was wrong, are with one exception still here. Groups that left them to be settled by whoever was loudest in the moment are, without exception in these papers, not.

One thing before the entries. Every rule here was written by somebody who had watched a group come apart. None of them was written by somebody who had built a good one from nothing, because the people who did that were busy. There is a bias in this material towards the diagnosis and away from the cure, and I have no way of correcting for it. What I can tell you is that Moray's settlement is the largest and healthiest thing anybody in this county has made. It does eight of these eleven things. It has never written any of them down and could not tell you which eight.

---

> "Decide how you will decide, before there is anything to decide." — Rosa Kaminski, schoolteacher, day 150

**Why it works.** A group in a crisis can do one thing at a time. It will either resolve the crisis or resolve the question of who is allowed to resolve it. It cannot do both. Kaminski's group spent one wet afternoon in the second June on a single sheet of paper. These four things are decided by whoever is on watch. These three by the whole household in the evening. This one by two named people together. And here is what happens if those two disagree. It took ninety minutes. It has been used perhaps four hundred times since and it has never once been argued about, because the argument happened in June when nothing was at stake.

**How it goes wrong.** Rules written in the calm are written by the people who are comfortable in the calm, and they are almost always the articulate ones. What a wet afternoon with a sheet of paper produces is a system that suits people who like wet afternoons with sheets of paper. Kaminski's own document gave three decisions to whoever was on watch and did not notice that the watch rota put two particular men on watch for most of the winter nights. The paper needs revising once a year, by somebody who was not in the room the first time. It needs to be revised while nothing is wrong, which is the exact condition under which nobody wants to.

---

> "Every rule has a name on it." — Nell Okafor, librarian, day 200

**Why it works.** An anonymous rule cannot be argued with, because there is nobody to argue with, and so it becomes furniture and then becomes doctrine. Okafor made everything in the Long Room carry the name of whoever proposed it and the date, and the effect was immediate and not what she expected: bad rules got repealed. It turns out that people will not challenge the way things are done, which is impersonal and enormous. They will happily say that they think Nell's rule about the lamps is wrong. That is small and specific and it has an obvious remedy.

**How it goes wrong.** A name on a rule turns repealing it into a defeat for a person, and people defend rules to defend themselves long after they have stopped believing in them. Okafor's rule about the lamps was wrong within a fortnight and stayed for eleven months. She is the one who wrote it and she is the one who kept it. She knew. The correction is a small piece of theatre and it works. The person who proposed a rule is the person who is asked, once a season, whether it should stay. Answering no in that particular chair is the most respected thing anybody in this building can do.

---

> "The rota is the government." — Rob Hatherleigh, logistics corporal, day 180

**Why it works.** Whoever chairs the evening meeting has the visible power and it is not where the power is. The power is in who empties the latrine bucket, who takes the four-in-the-morning watch in January, who goes on the water run when it is raining. And whether those are the same three people every week. Hatherleigh's argument is that a group's real constitution is its rota, and that everybody in the group already knows this. A settlement where the unpleasant work is visibly shared can survive almost any amount of disagreement about anything else. He built rotas for a living. His are fair to a degree that nobody has ever faulted.

**How it goes wrong.** They are also joyless, and a rota that is perfectly fair and never varies turns eleven people into eleven functions, and does it slowly enough that nobody can name the week it happened. Hatherleigh's household in the second year was the best run and least happy thing in this book. The three people who left it left for a place that was worse organised and warmer. Fairness is necessary and it is not sufficient. The fix costs nothing. Let people swap. Let people volunteer for the bad shift and be thanked out loud. And put one thing on the rota that is not work.

---

> "Do not make the strongest person the leader." — Priya Raghunathan, A&E nurse, day 210

**Why it works.** The first year selected hard for one skill and it was the wrong one. Being good at the dangerous part is a specific and valuable competence. It has almost nothing to do with allocating food across a winter, judging a stranger, or holding a group of frightened people together in February. Putting the same person in charge of all four is how you get a settlement that is superb in a crisis and cannot organise a harvest. There is a second reason and it is practical. The strong one is needed at the wall, and a leader who is at the wall is not available for the eleven decisions that get made while they are out.

**How it goes wrong.** There are weeks when the group needs the strong one to lead and everybody knows it. A rule that forbids it produces a fiction, where the nominal leader relays what the strong one has said. Raghunathan has watched that and calls it the worst of both. The other cost lands on the person doing the dangerous work. They take the risk and they are excluded from the decisions. By the second winter that is a specific and legitimate grievance, and in one settlement in these papers it turned into something much worse. Separate the roles, name both out loud, and give the dangerous one an absolute veto over anything that sends people outside.

---

> "Let people go." — Solomon Quaye, barber, day 500

**Why it works.** A group that cannot be left is not a group, it is a situation, and everybody inside it can feel the difference even when nobody says so. Quaye's position is that the right to walk out is what makes everything else voluntary. A person who stays when they could go is a different and better colleague than a person who stays because the alternative has been made impossible. His settlement gives anybody leaving three days of food, the loan of a coat and no argument. Four people have used it in three years. Two of them came back, and both say the going was what let them come back.

**How it goes wrong.** An open door bleeds in the good months. People leave in May, when everything is easy and somewhere else looks better. They are missed in November, when the roof needs doing and there are four fewer pairs of hands than the place was built around. And what leaves with them is the map: where the store is, how many are on the wall, which night the watch is thin. Quaye's household accepts all of that and has decided it is the price. The one thing he does insist on is that nobody leaves in the fortnight after a death, or in a hard frost. Both of those have been argued about. He has held both.

---

> "Two people count the store, and they are not friends." — Bea Winslow, retired postmistress, day 260

**Why it works.** Winslow spent forty years in a job where the money was counted twice by two people every single day, and she understood exactly what that practice is for, which is not catching thieves. It is making theft unnecessary to worry about. A store counted in the open, by two people who do not socialise, on a known day, removes an entire category of suspicion from a group's life. Suspicion about food is the thing that ends settlements. She has never found a discrepancy she could attribute to a person. That is the system working, not the system being unnecessary.

**How it goes wrong.** It says out loud that the group expects to be stolen from. In a small household that is a hard thing to have said. Two settlements in these notebooks abandoned the practice within a month for exactly that reason, and both regretted it. The other failure is Winslow's own and she tells it against herself. The count found nothing for two years and then found a shortfall of eleven pounds of oats. There was a fortnight of quiet horror. It turned out to be her own arithmetic in a cold room in December. Count twice. Then have a rule, written down in advance, about what happens on the first discrepancy, because the first discrepancy will be a mistake and everybody will behave as though it is not.

---

> "Bury them properly, and everybody stops work." — Wes Ordish, undertaker, day 130

**Why it works.** Ordish buried people for thirty-one years and his claim is one that the notebooks support without much doubt. The settlements that stopped holding funerals came apart within about six months, every time. The ones that kept them did not. A funeral does nothing for the dead. It is a machine for telling the living three things. That a person who was here mattered. That a death will be marked when it is theirs. And that the group is a thing that owes its members something. Stopping work is the load-bearing part. A burial that people attend in shifts, around the rota, says the opposite of what it is meant to say.

**How it goes wrong.** A burial party is the most exposed a settlement ever is. Everybody in one place, outside the walls, occupied, upset, not watching, at a time and place that has been known about for a day. A settlement at Rawdon lost three people at a graveside in the second August, which is one of the worst things in these papers. Ordish's own practice by the third year was to bury at first light in the frost, with two people at distance who do not attend, and to hold the service afterwards and inside. He has said that separating the two was the hardest professional decision of his life and that he would do it again.

---

> "Nobody alone. Nobody in more than three." — Ewan Tait, mountain rescue, day 130

**Why it works.** One is no rescue. A person alone who breaks an ankle two miles out is a death, and Tait had spent nineteen years walking towards the consequences of that. Four or more is loud, slow, hard to hide, and produces the thing where everybody assumes somebody else is watching. Three is the number that works: one doing the task, one watching the ground, one spare pair of hands who can go for help or carry. It is also small enough to lie down in a ditch. Every mountain rescue team in this country arrived at some version of three by about 1960 and none of us should have had to rediscover it.

**How it goes wrong.** In a household of eight, three out is more than a third of your strength gone for a day, and if that day goes wrong you have lost nearly half of everything. There is a subtler problem that finished a settlement at Yeadon. Three people out for eleven hours make decisions together, on the ground, with information the household does not have, and they come back having committed to something. Do it four or five times and you have a group inside the group, with a shared history the others do not share, and the household is now two things. Rotate who goes. Never send the same three twice running.

---

> "Teach somebody else your job." — Yusuf Errai, veterinary surgeon, day 320

**Why it works.** Every settlement is a list of single points of failure and nobody writes the list down. Bramall's bees. Cattermole's canal. Kowal's teeth, and Errai's own, which is the whole of the medicine for four settlements and is one man of sixty-one. The rule is not about generosity. It is about arithmetic. A household whose only water engineer is one person has a fifty percent chance, over a few years, of losing water engineering altogether. The cost of preventing that is an hour a week for a year. Errai has taught his second three times over and has stopped being irreplaceable, which he describes as the best fortnight's work he ever did.

**How it goes wrong.** Teaching costs the expert their time and, if they let it, their standing. There is a real and unattractive way in which being the only person who can do a thing has been keeping some of these people fed. Worse is the half-taught apprentice, which is Kowal's chapter six entry in another form. Somebody with four months of instruction and complete confidence is more dangerous than somebody with none. They will attempt the thing rather than wait. Teach the second. Then write down, and make them say out loud, the three procedures they are not to attempt without you, and expect that list to be the thing they resent.

---

> "Somebody has to be allowed to be useless for a week." — Sister Agnes Moray, day 330

**Why it works.** People break. Not dramatically and not for good. The pattern is the same one Moray saw for twenty years before any of this. A person who is given a week to be no use at all comes back and works for another three years. A person who is not given it stops working properly some time in the following autumn, and never starts again. Her settlement holds about a fortnight of slack in the year for this and treats it as a stock, like food, that is laid in and drawn down. It is the single most unusual thing about that place and I think it is why it is the largest.

**How it goes wrong.** Slack is bought with somebody's labour, and the somebody is always the same three people. If the week is not rationed it gets drawn by whoever is loudest about needing it, rather than whoever is closest to breaking. Moray's own difficulty is that she has never once refused it and has been taken advantage of twice by the same man. And a household of five cannot do this at all. The arithmetic does not close. Everybody knows it does not close. Pretending otherwise produces a rule that exists on paper and is never used, which is worse than not having it. Below about eight people, this entry is not available to you, and that is a real argument for being more than eight.

---

> "Go, if it is that kind of group." — Callum Reith, day 1,180

**Why it works.** He was in a crew for most of two years that took food off settlements, using the reasoning in his chapter seven entry. He left at nineteen with nothing, in September, and walked forty miles. His rule is the counterweight to everything else in this chapter, and it needs saying because loyalty has been doing a great deal of unexamined work in these papers. A group is a tool for keeping people alive. When it stops being that and becomes a thing that keeps other people from being alive, staying in it is not loyalty and leaving it is not betrayal. Every argument you will hear to the contrary will be made by somebody who benefits from you staying.

**How it goes wrong.** Almost every solitary death in these eleven hundred notebooks begins with somebody walking out of a group. That is the flat fact and Reith knows it better than I do. The ones who left in a temper, in the wrong season, without a destination, are a category. It is a large category. He got across those forty miles in September in good weather, and has said plainly that in January he would not have. So: go, if it is that kind of group. Go in spring or early autumn. Go towards a named place. Take three days of food and tell one person you trust which way. And if none of those four conditions can be met this month, plan it for the month when they can. In the meantime, do not let anybody persuade you that the deciding is settled.
