# Chapter 3 — Moving

Here is the arithmetic that everything in this chapter comes out of, and I would rather you had it in one paragraph on one page than scattered through eleven entries.

You walk at three miles an hour on a road with nothing on your back, and at two and a half with a load, and at less than two over ground. You stop. You sleep. Over a full day and night, hard going, you will make twenty miles and be ruined. They walk at a shade under four. They do not stop, they do not sleep, they do not sit down at four in the afternoon and take a boot off. Over that same day and night one of them will cover something close to ninety miles, if it has a reason to. Put those two numbers next to each other and the conclusion is not comfortable and it is not negotiable. Over any distance that matters, you cannot outwalk them. You never could. Every plan built on getting far enough away has failed.

What saves you is the last four words of that sentence, if it has a reason to. They follow what they can hear, then what they can see move, and they follow it while it is there and they lose it when it is not. A thing that stops being perceptible stops being followed, and within an hour or two the one that was coming for you is drifting again with the rest. So the distance you need is not the distance to safety. It is the much shorter distance to the corner, the hedge, the wall, the far side of the wind. You are not running away. You are becoming uninteresting, and the running is only the part of it that gets you out of sight.

Everything else in this chapter is about feet, weight, water and hours, which are the four things that decide whether you get to the corner. None of it is exciting. Six of the eleven people in this chapter were walkers, drivers or riders before, and none of the ones who were not lasted the first year, and I do not think that is chance.

---

> "You cannot outwalk them. You can only stop being worth following." — Ewan Tait, mountain rescue, day 20

**Why it works.** Tait did the sum on the second Sunday, on the back of a leaflet, and it is the sum at the top of this chapter. Once you accept it, everything about how you move changes and it changes for the better. You stop thinking in miles and start thinking in corners. You stop grinding out an extra four miles at dusk, which is exactly when the ground gets worse and your judgement goes. You spend that energy on breaking line of sight, once, properly, and then walking away quietly. The people still alive in this county move in short deliberate legs between places they cannot be seen from. Nobody is doing forced marches. The forced marches are all in the notebooks that stop.

**How it goes wrong.** It is a rule for one of them, or four. It is not a rule for a drift. A drift of two thousand does not lose you, because the ones at the back are following the ones at the front, and the front is a mile wide. There is no corner that takes you out of sight of all of it. Tait knew this and wrote it, and the sentence that got copied around three counties is the short one, without the qualifier. I have had it quoted at me by a man who was proposing to walk across the front of a drift on the strength of it. Against a crowd, distance is the only thing that works, and you had better start early and you had better have somewhere to be.

---

> "Twelve miles a day with a load, and no arguing about it." — Rob Hatherleigh, logistics corporal, day 12

**Why it works.** It is a real number and it comes from a century of people measuring what a column can do day after day without falling apart. Twelve miles with weight is sustainable. Fifteen is a good day. Twenty is a day you pay for over the following two, and armies worked this out long before any of us needed it. The value of writing it down is that it stops the argument at four in the afternoon, when somebody is feeling strong and wants to push to the next town. The person feeling strong is never the person who will fail. Hatherleigh's group made the same distance every day for five weeks and arrived able to work.

**How it goes wrong.** It is a soldier's number, taken from fit men of similar age carrying similar loads, and his group had a woman of sixty-three, a boy of nine and a man who had been a bank clerk. A column moves at the speed of the person who is struggling, and if the number on the wall says twelve then what happens is not that everybody does twelve. What happens is that the woman of sixty-three does twelve and is destroyed by it, in silence, for four days, because she can read the number too. Set the day's distance from the slowest pair of legs and set it out loud. Then write that number on the wall.

---

> "Never the road. The thing beside the road." — Fen Doherty, poacher, day 30

**Why it works.** A road is the best navigation there is and it costs you everything else, because it is a corridor. Every walker, every drift and every man with a rifle and an idea is using the same corridor for the same reason. What Doherty worked out in his first fortnight is that you can have the navigation without the corridor. Walk the hedge line, the ditch, the field margin, the strip of scrub inside the fence. You are forty yards off. You can see the road and it cannot see you. You keep the direction and you keep the cover, and if something comes down the tarmac you lie down in a ditch and it goes past. He crossed the county four times in the first year and was seen twice.

**How it goes wrong.** It is half the speed on a good day and a third of it on a wet one, and a hedge line in February is a serious piece of work. The margins are also where everything is: the wire, the old wells, the drainage cuts under the grass, the badger holes. Doherty went into a rabbit hole on the flat, in daylight, in the second spring, and put his ankle out nine miles from anybody. He says himself that a man on the road would have been at Tadcaster by four. The road when you are strong and the time is short and you can see a long way. The hedge the rest of the time.

---

> "Follow the cut." — Bill Cattermole, lock keeper, day 25

**Why it works.** A canal is the best walking in England and it always was. It is flat, because it has to be. It is waymarked to the quarter mile. It goes between towns without going through them. There is water in it, there are bridges every half mile that let you swap sides, and there are gates and locks that make a natural place to stop and see what is behind you. Cattermole had worked the Leeds and Liverpool for thirty-one years and could walk it in the dark, and eleven people came down it with him in the second April and every one arrived.

**How it goes wrong.** A towpath is a bottle. On one side is a hedge or a wall and on the other is the cut, and if something comes at you from both ends of a straight you have two options and one of them is the water. Here is the part people learn once. They do not drown. They do not float, they do not panic and they do not need to breathe. What happens when one goes in the canal is that it walks along the bottom. It comes out on the far bank six or seven minutes later, and it comes out where you are. Two of Cattermole's eleven found this out in the same afternoon. The cut is the best road in the county and it is the worst place to be surprised.

---

> "Boots off at every stop. Every stop." — Priya Raghunathan, A&E nurse, day 33

**Why it works.** Skin that is wet for long enough stops being skin. It goes white, it goes soft, it comes off in sheets with the sock, and then you are a person who cannot walk in a world where walking is the only thing there is. Raghunathan saw four of these in one week in the first April and wrote the rule that night. Off, out, wrung, feet dry in the air for ten minutes, dry pair on, wet pair inside the coat against the body where they will be half dry in two hours. Ten minutes an hour is expensive. A foot is more expensive, and unlike almost everything else in this book, a ruined foot cannot be traded for, prayed over or fixed later.

**How it goes wrong.** There is a stop where you cannot afford eight minutes and you will not know it is that stop until it is. A man called Devlin died at Micklefield in the second November sitting on a wall with both boots off and both socks in his hands, and he had done everything right for two hundred days. The rule needs its exception said out loud so that people can use it: boots off at every stop where somebody else is watching the ground. Alone, or with one other, you dry your feet at the place you are sleeping and nowhere else.

---

> "Move at four in the morning." — Kit Anand, courier, day 15

**Why it works.** Four in the morning is the coldest hour of the day, and cold is the one thing that reliably takes them apart. Below four degrees they are slow and stupid. In a hard frost they stop where they stand and become street furniture that you walk around. So the coldest hour is the hour when a thing that is faster than you is not faster than you. It is also the hour when every human being for nine miles is asleep, which in this county matters at least as much. Anand had been riding a bike round this city at that hour for six years before any of it and knew the streets in the dark better than in the light.

**How it goes wrong.** You cannot see, and everything in the last four entries about the ditch and the wire and the ankle is four times as likely in the dark. The cold that slows them slows you first and slows you more. You are the one who has been asleep and is now walking at two miles an hour with no breakfast. Hypothermia does not need a hard winter to work on a thin person at four in the morning. And in July four in the morning is fourteen degrees, and they are as quick then as at noon. This is a rule for October to March. In summer it is just walking in the dark for no advantage, and Anand herself stopped doing it in the second June.

---

> "Carry the means to drink, not the drink." — Gwen Prydderch, water engineer, day 55

**Why it works.** Water is two and a bit pounds a litre and you need three or four litres a day, and if you carry that you are carrying eight or nine pounds of the twenty-five you can carry at all. But this is England. There is water every mile, in gutters, streams, tanks, roofs, radiators and the header tank of every loft you pass. What you lack is not water, it is water you dare drink. Prydderch carried a litre, a filter, a bottle of tablets and a small pan, which came to under three pounds, and she drank six litres a day for two years and was never once ill. The weight you save goes into food, which is the thing there is genuinely not enough of.

**How it goes wrong.** Means fail and water does not. A filter clogs, and it clogs without warning on the day you push it through silt. Tablets get wet. A pan needs fire and fire is chapter five's problem. In the second August there was a stretch across the flat land east of here with twenty miles and nothing in it, in a summer when everything standing had gone green and foul. The man carrying four litres of stupid heavy water walked it. The two people carrying filters did not. Carry the means. Then carry a litre anyway, and be aware of what it is for, which is the day the means let you down.

---

> "Walk to somewhere. Never away from something." — Lila Semenova, climber, day 8

**Why it works.** Retreat has no end condition. That is the whole argument and it is a good one. A group that is moving away from a thing will keep moving, because there is no distance at which anybody can say we have arrived. So it walks until it is too tired to make decisions, and then it makes one. A named place fixes that. It fixes the day's distance. It fixes the direction when you are tired. It gives you something to talk about at night that is not what you left. And it lets the argument happen at the map, and not at four in the afternoon in the rain. It does not have to be a good place. It has to be a place with a name.

**How it goes wrong.** A named place is a thing you press on towards, and everybody who has climbed knows what that does, and Semenova knew it better than anybody in this book and did it anyway. Her group had Malham in their heads. They kept the number of days it would take and they kept it after the weather turned and after the older of the two brothers started coughing. Turning back means admitting the plan was the problem, and the plan was hers. They got to Malham. Two of the five got to Malham. A destination is a tool for making decisions and it becomes, without anybody noticing the hour it happens, a reason not to make them.

---

> "Nothing in the bag that you have not carried up a hill." — Pearl Adjei, bus driver, day 6

**Why it works.** Everything is light for the first mile. That is the trap. It is why every abandoned pack on every verge in this county has the same four things in it, and why the verges of the first spring were a museum of what people thought they needed on a Tuesday. Adjei loaded her bag on the second day, walked it up the hill at Beeston and back down, and took out a third of it. She did that three times over a fortnight, until what was left was what she could carry all day. Twenty-five pounds is a load. Thirty-five is a load you will put down in a hurry one day, and the day you put it down is the day you needed it.

**How it goes wrong.** Watch what people cut. They cut the thing they have not used yet. The things you have not used yet are the medical kit, the spare socks and the second knife, and those are the items whose entire purpose is the day that has not happened. Adjei cut her kit down to a beautiful weight over three weeks and had no dressings in it at all when a woman in her group opened her hand on a gate in June. Weigh the bag. Then take the medical kit out, put it on the floor, and pack it first, and let everything else compete for what is left.

---

> "Run for the corner. Then walk." — Callum Reith, day 900

**Why it works.** It is the practical form of the sum at the top of this chapter. Running does not get you away, because nothing gets you away. Running gets you round the corner, over the wall, into the alley, out of sight, and then its whole job is done. Continuing to run is now working against you, because running is loud, and loud recruits. The thing you have just lost has friends within earshot who have not seen you at all yet. Sprint for as long as it takes to break the line. Then stop, breathe through your nose, and walk out of the area at a speed that makes no noise on gravel. Reith has done this several hundred times and he is twenty years old and he is very hard to kill.

**How it goes wrong.** The corner has to be a real corner. A corner that leads into a yard is not a corner. It is a room with a door in it. The whole method depends on knowing what is round the thing you are about to run round, which means it belongs to people who know the ground. Reith grew up on this ground. The woman he was with in the second winter did not, and she could not do the second half of it. She kept running because everything in her body was telling her to. The noise of it across two hundred yards of car park brought in what was in the multi storey. He got round the corner and walked. She did not. He has written about it once and I am not going to add anything to it.

---

> "Stop walking." — Maggie Lunt, hill farmer, day 400

**Why it works.** The roaming years killed more people than they saved and the notebooks are not subtle about it. Walking is exposure, every day, all day, and it produces nothing: no store, no crop, no roof, no fence, no relationships with anybody who might help you in February. Lunt watched three groups pass her gate in the first year, all of them going somewhere better. She was still on her land when the third of them came back past, going the other way, with fewer people in it. Everybody alive round here at scale is somebody who stopped. The rule gives you permission to be the person who is not moving, in a year when moving looked like the only competence there was.

**How it goes wrong.** She stopped on eleven acres with a spring on it, a stone barn, walls she could see over and a slope that meant nothing could come at her out of dead ground. That is not stopping. That is stopping somewhere, and the somewhere is doing all the work in the sentence. Two settlements I could name stopped in the wrong place and stayed out of the same instinct. They defended it past the point where anybody with clear eyes would have walked out, and were finished in the second summer inside a week. Stopping is right. Being unable to leave the place you stopped is the same disease as never stopping at all, and it is chapter four's subject, and Lunt keeps a bag packed by her door to this day.
