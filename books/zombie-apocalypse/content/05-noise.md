# Chapter 5 — Noise

Everybody learns in the first week that noise brings them, and then spends three years being wrong about which noise.

The wrongness is specific and it is worth an entire chapter, because the mistake runs the same way every time: people are frightened of the sounds that are loud where they are standing, and the sounds that travel are not those. A dropped spanner on a stone floor is startling and it is a sharp, high, short sound, and high sounds are eaten by air, hedges, walls and the ground itself, and forty yards away it is not there at all. A small generator is boring and you stop hearing it in ten minutes. It is low, and low sounds bend round buildings instead of stopping at them. It goes on for six hours. It is audible across a mile of this valley to anything with ears. One of those two sounds has emptied a village. It is not the spanner.

Three facts will do most of your thinking for you. Sound loses six decibels every time the distance doubles, which sounds severe and is much less severe than people expect over the first few hundred yards. High frequencies die faster than low ones, so what reaches a listener at a distance is never the sound you made. It is the bottom half of it. Not the words but the shape of a voice. Not the chop but the thud. And on a clear still night the air a few feet up is warmer than the air at the ground. That bends sound back down instead of letting it escape. The same noise made at two in the morning goes two or three times as far as it did at noon. That last one killed people for a year before anybody wrote it down.

The rest of the chapter is technique. But I want the argument of it stated here, because eight of these eleven entries pull towards silence and the last two pull hard the other way, and the last two are right. Absolute quiet is not a survival strategy. It is a thing a group can hold for about five months. After that it has produced nothing, built nothing, warned nobody and stopped speaking to itself. I have opened up two houses where everybody inside was thin, mute and still following the rule.

---

> "Low and long carries. Sharp and short does not." — Femi Adeyemi, live sound engineer, day 35

**Why it works.** He had spent eleven years putting sound into rooms and knew, without having to reason about it, which parts of a noise survive a journey. Air absorbs the top end. Foliage absorbs the top end. A brick wall stops the top end and lets the bottom walk round the corner. So the practical ranking is not by how loud a thing is in your hand. It is a generator, a diesel engine, a pump, a big steady voice, a hammer on wood, an axe. And then a very long way down, everything sharp and small that people are afraid of. Adeyemi ranked the household's noises on a sheet of paper in the second month, and the sheet is still pinned up in three settlements I know of.

**How it goes wrong.** Distance is one axis and novelty is the other, and he built his method on the first and forgot the second. What turns a head near you is not the loudest thing, it is the thing that was not there a second ago. A steady hum becomes background inside a minute, to them as much as to you, and a single tap in a silent street at four in the morning is the most attention-getting sound a human being can make. He was killed by neither, as it happens. He was killed on the ridge above Bramley by a piece of corrugated sheet that he stood on, which is short, sharp, and was the only sound in a quarter of a mile.

---

> "Shoes at the door, felt on everything, cloth in the cutlery drawer." — Bea Winslow, retired postmistress, day 90

**Why it works.** A house makes a sound whether anybody is being careful or not, and it is made of about thirty things: two hinges, a latch, four floorboards, a stair tread, a bucket handle, a kettle lid, the drawer. Winslow's method was to find all thirty in one afternoon and fix them, because a fault you have fixed costs nothing thereafter and a fault you are being careful about costs attention every hour of every day for four years. Grease on the hinges, felt under the bucket, a strip of blanket over the drawer runners, and a nail through the loose tread. After that the household can move normally, which is the entire point and the part people miss.

**How it goes wrong.** A silent house is a house you cannot hear anybody moving in, including somebody who is not supposed to be there. Winslow's second household had a man walk the length of the ground floor and out again with most of a week's food. Four people were awake upstairs. She now leaves one board loose on purpose, at the bottom of the stairs, and everybody knows which. The other cost is speed. People who have been trained out of noise move slowly at exactly the moments when they should be moving fast. There is at least one death in these notebooks that comes down to a woman closing a door quietly instead of running through it.

---

> "Loud work in the rain." — Erin Mackay, fisherwoman, day 50

**Why it works.** Rain on leaves and roofs is broadband noise across the whole range, which is the only thing that masks properly, and hard rain will take twenty feet of chopping and reduce it to something you cannot pick out at a hundred. Wind in trees does the same job less reliably. A river does it all the time in one place, which is why the mills were always the noisiest buildings in England. Mackay ran a rule that any job involving an axe, a hammer, a saw or a scream waited for weather, and she got her boat repaired, her shed built and two winters of firewood done in the rain.

**How it goes wrong.** Masking works both ways and there is no version where it does not. You cannot hear them either, and you cannot hear a person, and hard rain takes your eyes down to about sixty yards as well. She lost a man off the staithe at Paull in the second October to something that walked up behind him in weather that was doing exactly what she had planned for it to do. Rain is also when everything is slippery and cold and your hands are bad. Do the loud work in the rain. Put somebody on the corner who is doing nothing but looking, and rotate them, because looking at nothing in the rain for an hour is harder than the work.

---

> "Two taps come here. Three taps run. There is nothing else." — Rosa Kaminski, schoolteacher, day 100

**Why it works.** Whatever signalling system you design, the version that gets used is the version people can perform while frightened, and that is a very short list. Two signals is a list people can perform. Kaminski had watched twenty-two years of children under drill conditions and she knew that the vocabulary shrinks to nothing under stress, so she built for the worst state rather than the calm one. Tapping also has the right acoustics, which she did not know and got right by accident: a knuckle on wood is mid-range and directional and it does not carry past the building.

**How it goes wrong.** A vocabulary of two cannot say the thing that most needs saying. There is no signal in that system for I am hurt and I am not in danger. On the eleventh of March in the second year a boy called Aaron Petch tapped three, which means run, because it was the only thing he had. The household ran. He had a broken leg under a fallen gate two hundred yards away, and they were a mile off before anybody worked it out. He was alive when they came back the next morning and he was not there. Add a third signal. Add only one, and make it mean come slowly and bring the bag.

---

> "Never shout a name." — Priya Raghunathan, A&E nurse, day 120

**Why it works.** A human voice calling a name is the most effective recruiting sound there is and it beats a gunshot, which surprises people. A shot is one event, over in a fifth of a second, and half a mile away it is a thing that might have been a door. A name is a voice, pitched where voices are pitched, repeated, getting louder each time because you are getting more frightened, going on for a minute or more. It says a person is here, it says where, and it says that person is distressed and stationary. Raghunathan had watched it happen twice in a hospital car park and never shouted again.

**How it goes wrong.** A rule against calling out is a rule against calling for help, and it does not distinguish. Her own group carried it too far and got quiet about everything. A woman named Ruth Colley went into a flooded cellar on Barkerend Road after a dropped box and was in there for two days. There were four people in the house above her. They had all heard something, and had all decided on their own not to shout. Nobody in these notebooks ever mentions this. It is the failure of the good pupils. Take the rule as written for open ground, and hang the opposite of it on the inside of the door: in this building we call out, and we call out early, and we do not wait to be sure.

---

> "The generator runs one hour a week." — Jonah Kite, radio operator, day 130

**Why it works.** A small petrol set is around sixty-five decibels of low-frequency noise at a couple of yards, which is not much, and it is unbroken, and unbroken is what does the damage. Everything within a mile that can hear it has all afternoon to walk towards it, and unlike a bang, it does not stop and let them lose interest. Kite worked out in the first autumn that his set was not attracting things from a distance, it was accumulating them over days, and the crowd at the bottom of the lane was the sum of a fortnight of Wednesdays. One hour a week, in daylight, in wind, with the set in a pit under a straw bale.

**How it goes wrong.** Rationing the power rationed the listening, and the listening was the entire point of the machine. He was off the air for the six days that mattered most in the second year and heard the news about the Bradford road eleven days after everybody else. The bigger failure was the schedule. One hour a week is one hour a week at the same time, because that is what a schedule is. By the third summer there was a man who knew when Kite's set ran, and he had told other people. They came on a Wednesday. A ration is right. Roll the day and the hour every week, and take the small cost of being unpredictable to your friends as well.

---

> "If you want a street quiet, make a noise in the next one." — Idris Pyle, day 100

**Why it works.** They follow sound and they keep following it while it lasts, and they do not check whether the sound is a person. A wind-up alarm clock in the footwell of a car with the window down will run for twenty minutes and hold a drift's attention for an afternoon. Once the front of a crowd is moving, the back of it follows the front. No clock is needed after that. It is the cheapest tactic in this book. Pyle used it to empty the whole of a shopping parade at Hunslet, twice, and walked in behind them with a wheelbarrow, and there is no arguing with the results.

**How it goes wrong.** You have not removed a crowd. You have aimed it. Whatever comes to the clock is now two streets from where it was, in a place you chose and did not look at first. On the second occasion the parade Pyle emptied was three hundred yards from the almshouses on Church Street. There were eleven people in there who had been getting away with it since the first spring. Six of them. He is the only person in this book who has written down a thing of that kind about himself, which is a point in his favour and is not very many points. Look at where the noise is going. It is going to somebody.

---

> "Muffle the dog. Do not get rid of the dog." — Duncan Ferrier, gamekeeper, day 140

**Why it works.** A dog is the best perimeter anybody has and nothing built out of wire and tins comes close. It smells them at two hundred yards, upwind and in the dark, and it knows the difference between a fox and a man, which no bell has ever managed. The problem is only the barking, and barking is trainable, and Ferrier had trained working dogs for twenty years. What he taught was a silent alert: the dog comes to you, presses its head against your leg, and holds. Two of the settlements up the valley run his dogs' grandchildren and neither has been surprised in two years.

**How it goes wrong.** That training takes a calm handler, a hundred repetitions and about four months, and almost nobody in the first year had any of the three. What most people had was an ordinary frightened family dog, which barks, and a rule from a gamekeeper saying keep the dog. There are at least four households in these notebooks that were found because of a dog they had been told not to get rid of. A dog costs about the food of a small adult, every day, all year. Keep the dog if you can train it or it is already trained. Otherwise you are keeping an alarm that goes off at the wrong things and eats.

---

> "Nothing is answered after dark." — Fen Doherty, poacher, day 160

**Why it works.** At night the air above the ground is warmer than the ground, and that bends sound downward and keeps it travelling, so a voice at two in the morning reaches two or three times as far as the same voice at noon. That is the physics, and the human consequence is that anybody calling in the dark has chosen the ground, knows you cannot see, and has the whole night to work with. Doherty's household answered nothing between dusk and light for three years, on any pretext, and were never once robbed, and he is a man who had a great deal worth taking.

**How it goes wrong.** Real people call at night, because night is when things go wrong. A boy walked up the Elland Road side of the estate in the second February calling for anybody, for about forty minutes, in a hard frost. Eleven households heard him and every single one of them had this rule. He was found in the morning between two cars. There is no version of this where everybody follows the rule and that boy lives. The revision people have arrived at up here has three parts. You do not answer with your voice. You do not open your door. You put a light in a window that means come to the light at dawn, and somebody watches the light all night. It is not much. It would have been enough for him.

---

> "Silence is a season. It is not a rule." — Femi Adeyemi, live sound engineer, day 300

**Why it works.** This is the correction to his own first entry and to most of this chapter. A household needs to chop, build, hammer, dig, argue and sing. If all of that is forbidden then none of it happens. A group that does none of it for a year has no fuel, no repairs and no way of being a group. So you budget noise instead of banning it, and you spend the budget when it is cheapest, and in this country that means the frost. Below freezing they are standing still. Wind and rain mask. Winter is when you take the roof off and put a new one on, and there is a rhythm to the year up here now that comes directly out of this sentence.

**How it goes wrong.** A budget with permission in it gets spent, and then gets spent again, and the drift is invisible from inside. Adeyemi's own settlement went from an axe in a January frost to a forge running in October in about eighteen months, one reasonable decision at a time, with nobody at any point doing anything indefensible. That is how it always goes. Somebody has to hold the number, out loud, and be unpopular. Adeyemi's note is that the person holding it should not be the person who decides what the work is. Those two jobs in one head is how the number moves.

---

> "Make some noise." — Solomon Quaye, barber, day 1,100

**Why it works.** Quaye cut hair on Peckham High Street for nineteen years and his position is that a settlement of people who have whispered for four years is not a settlement, it is forty individuals standing near each other. Sound is most of how people are together. Singing, arguing across a yard, a child shouting, the noise of a room where something is being made. He started a Sunday in the frost where the rule is suspended for three hours between the wall and the barn: instruments, talking at a normal volume, and a haircut. Attendance is total. Two of the four settlements up this valley have copied it and both of them report the same thing, which is that people stopped leaving.

**How it goes wrong.** It was quiet for a reason and the reason has not gone anywhere. A settlement at Guiseley took up a version of this in the second year, without the frost condition and without the walls. They were found in the autumn by people, not by them. There are nine of them left out of thirty-one. Quaye's rule has three conditions on it and they are all load-bearing: hard ground, hard frost, and a wall you have walked yourself that week. He has never once suspended it for a wedding in June, and he has been asked, and the asking is the hardest part of the job.
