# Chapter 9 — False Choices

Most decisions have more than two options, and almost every persuasive account of a decision presents exactly two. This is not usually dishonesty. It is compression, and compression is necessary, because nobody can hold nine possibilities in mind while being asked to choose. The abuse begins when the compression is done by somebody with a preference, and the option they dislike is the one that vanishes.

What makes the family hard to counter is that objecting sounds like refusing to decide. The person offering two options is being practical. The person saying *there might be a third* is being difficult, and they usually cannot describe the third one on the spot, because they have just been handed the framing thirty seconds ago.

That asymmetry is worth naming to yourself before you walk into a meeting where a decision is being framed. You will be at a disadvantage that has nothing to do with being wrong. It comes from the fact that the person who chose the options has been thinking about them for a week.

The counters below are about buying the time to find the missing option, and about declining to accept a choice as complete simply because it has been presented as complete. The most useful sentence in the chapter is the least impressive one: *those are two of the options.*

---

> "We either act now or we accept it will never happen." — The False Dilemma

**Why it works.** Two options presented as exhaustive, with the speaker's preference made to look like the only responsible one. The audience evaluates which of the two is better rather than whether there are only two.

**What to say back.** "Those are two of the options." Then pause. The sentence is unremarkable and it reopens the field, and the pause is what gives you and everybody else the moment needed to think of a third that nobody has mentioned.

---

> "You're either with us on this or you're against us." — The Loyalty Binary

**Why it works.** It converts a question about a proposal into a question about allegiance, and allegiance is answered instantly and emotionally. Anybody with a specific reservation is sorted into the opposition before they can state it.

**What to say back.** Refuse the sorting and be concrete. "I'm for the goal and against clause four, which I think will backfire." Specificity is the only escape. A general answer to a binary is a binary answer.

---

> "Would you rather we did what the last lot did?" — The Worse Option Defence

**Why it works.** A bad thing is measured against a worse thing and comes out looking like a success. The comparison is real and the standard is wrong, since the alternative on offer is not the only alternative that existed.

**What to say back.** Move the comparison. "Better than the worst option available isn't the test. Compared with what we said we'd do, how did it go?" Choosing the benchmark is most of the argument, and asking for a different one is not a dodge.

---

> "It's not perfect, but what's the alternative?" — Argument from No Alternative

**Why it works.** It grants the criticism, which disarms you, and then makes the absence of a ready replacement into a positive case. It is effective because it is often sincerely meant and sometimes correct.

**What to say back.** "Let's find out. Nobody's looked yet." Then propose the search rather than the substitute. The claim that no alternative exists is a claim about the world, and it is nearly always made without anybody having checked.

---

> "We've already put eighteen months into this." — The Sunk Cost Appeal

**Why it works.** The effort spent is real and it feels like an asset. It is not: the eighteen months are gone whatever you decide next, and they carry emotional weight precisely because abandoning them means admitting they were wasted.

**What to say back.** Frame the decision forward. "If we were starting today, knowing what we know, would we choose this?" It is the same question stripped of the past, and it often produces a different answer from the same people in the same room.

---

> "Fine, so what would you do instead?" — Demanding a Full Alternative

**Why it works.** It makes the right to criticise conditional on having a finished plan, which almost nobody has. Pointing out that a bridge will fall down does not require you to be able to design a bridge.

**What to say back.** Separate the two jobs. "I don't have a plan and this one has a hole in it. Both things can be true." Say it without apology. Once accepted, the criticism can be examined on its own, which is the only way it can be either fixed or dismissed properly.

---

> "It's a trade-off. You can't have both." — The Invented Trade-off

**Why it works.** Real trade-offs exist everywhere, so an invented one is camouflaged among them. Presenting two goods as necessarily opposed forecloses the possibility that some arrangement gets more of each.

**What to say back.** Ask for the mechanism. "What makes them exclusive?" Genuine trade-offs have a reason: a fixed budget, a physical constraint, a finite hour. Invented ones have only an assertion, and the question distinguishes them immediately.

---

> "Look, something is better than nothing." — Ignoring That Something Can Be Worse

**Why it works.** It sounds like pragmatism and it feels generous towards action. It quietly assumes the floor is zero, when a badly designed intervention can leave things worse than leaving them alone.

**What to say back.** "Sometimes. What happens if this one doesn't work?" Asking for the downside case is not obstruction; it is the missing half of the comparison, and a proposal that has not considered it is not finished.

---

> "We have to start somewhere." — The Unexamined First Step

**Why it works.** It grants that the step is imperfect while making any objection sound like a demand for perfection. It also implies that the direction has been agreed, when the direction is the thing in dispute.

**What to say back.** "Agreed. Does this step get us closer, or does it just get us moving?" The distinction is the point. Motion and progress feel identical from inside a meeting and are different a year later.

---

> "That's just the price of doing business." — Naturalising the Cost

**Why it works.** It reclassifies a choice as a law of nature. Once a cost is described as inherent, arguing about it looks naive, and the fact that somebody decided to accept it disappears from the conversation.

**What to say back.** "Who set that price?" Costs presented as inevitable are usually the outcome of a decision that could be revisited, and often the decision was made years ago by people who are no longer here.

---

> "There isn't time to explore other options." — Urgency as an Argument

**Why it works.** Time pressure is the most reliable way to close a decision, because it converts thinking into delay. Sometimes it is genuine, which is what makes the manufactured version so hard to challenge.

**What to say back.** Test the deadline. "What happens if we decide on Thursday instead of today?" A real deadline has consequences that can be named. A manufactured one has a mood, and the question separates them without accusing anybody.

---

> "So you'd rather do nothing at all." — The Inaction Smear

**Why it works.** It assigns you the one position nobody wants to hold. Inaction is the default and the default is always available to be blamed, so the person defending scrutiny is made to own everything that happens while scrutiny takes place.

**What to say back.** Name the third option out loud, even if it is small. "I'd rather do the pilot first, and then decide." A concrete, modest alternative is the only reliable answer to this, because it makes the choice visibly wider than two.
