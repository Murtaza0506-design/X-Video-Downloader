# Chapter 2 — Rigging the Terms

Whoever settles what the words mean has settled the argument, and they have done it before anybody noticed a decision was being made. This is why the opening minutes of a disagreement matter more than the next hour. A term accepted early is a term you are stuck with, and by the time its shape becomes inconvenient it has hardened into common ground.

The moves in this chapter all work on the same seam. Language is loose. Almost every word worth arguing about has a range rather than a point, and inside that range there is room to move without ever saying anything false. A cut becomes a realignment. A loss becomes a rounding error. A promise becomes an aspiration, and nothing has been retracted, because nothing was ever nailed down.

What makes these hard to counter is that the correction sounds petty. Objecting to a word, when everybody can see roughly what was meant, casts you as the person who cares more about vocabulary than about the thing itself. That impression is exactly what the move is buying.

So the counter is almost always to convert the word into something with edges. Not to argue about the definition, which is a swamp, but to ask what would be true in the world if the word applied, and what would be true if it did not. A term that cannot answer that question was doing no work in the first place, and once that is visible, the move stops paying.

One warning before the list. Precision is not automatically virtuous. Some people demand definitions to avoid conclusions, and that move is in here too, near the end.

---

> "Well, it rather depends what you mean by fair." — Definitional Retreat

**Why it works.** It answers a claim with a question, and the question is unanswerable in the time available. Any definition you offer can be met with an edge case, so you spend the argument defending a dictionary instead of a position.

**What to say back.** Refuse the abstraction and supply an instance. "I mean that Ali was paid forty percent less for the same job. Is that fair?" A concrete case cannot be dissolved into definitional fog, and if they still will not answer, everybody watching now knows why.

---

> "That isn't really capitalism, though. Real capitalism has never been tried." — No True Scotsman

**Why it works.** It saves the idea by expelling every example of it, and it does so with no visible cost, because the speaker never has to admit anything failed. The category simply loses members until only successes remain.

**What to say back.** Ask what would count. "What would a real one look like, and has it ever existed anywhere?" If nothing in history qualifies, the claim has quietly become unfalsifiable, and that is worth saying plainly rather than arguing about Sweden.

---

> "I'd call that a rounding error rather than a loss." — Renaming the Thing

**Why it works.** The new word carries a different emotional weight while pointing at the same facts, and because it is not technically false, correcting it looks like quibbling. Repeated a few times, the new name sticks and the thing feels smaller.

**What to say back.** Put the number back in. "It's eighty thousand pounds. Whatever we call it, is eighty thousand acceptable?" Names bend. Figures do not, and returning to the figure makes the renaming look like what it was.

---

> "It's not a cut. It's a realignment of resources." — The Euphemism

**Why it works.** Euphemism does not deceive anybody, which is not its job. Its job is to make objecting feel hysterical. Complaining about a realignment sounds unhinged in a way that complaining about a cut does not.

**What to say back.** Ask the mechanical question. "Will there be less money in that budget next year than this year?" A yes converts the euphemism back into the thing, without you ever having accused anyone of spin.

---

> "So you accept the project was a failure." — Upgrading Your Concession

**Why it works.** You conceded something small and it comes back enlarged. Because you did concede, denying the enlarged version looks like backtracking, and you spend the next five minutes defending a position you never held.

**What to say back.** Restate your own concession precisely and immediately. "I said the launch date slipped. That's what I accept." Do it the moment it happens. Left for even a minute, the upgraded version becomes the one in everybody's memory.

---

> "You're using disinterested wrong. It means impartial." — Policing the Word, Not the Point

**Why it works.** The correction is often right, which is what gives it force. It also transfers authority: the person who knows the word looks like the person who knows the subject, and your argument is now on probation for a fault that has nothing to do with it.

**What to say back.** Concede instantly and move. "Fair enough, uninterested. So does the objection stand?" The concession costs nothing and denies them the thing they were buying, which was the pause.

---

> "By any reasonable definition, this is a success." — Smuggling the Conclusion In

**Why it works.** The conclusion is inside the definition, so agreeing to the definition is agreeing to the conclusion, and the word *reasonable* makes disagreement sound unreasonable before you have opened your mouth.

**What to say back.** Unpick it before answering. "Let's separate those. What's the definition, and then separately, does this meet it?" Splitting the two makes the smuggling visible, and it turns out that the definition was built around this case.

---

> "Oh come on, everyone knows what I meant." — Refusing to Be Pinned

**Why it works.** It converts a request for precision into an accusation of bad faith, and it works especially well in front of an audience, who do know roughly what was meant and now think you are playing games.

**What to say back.** Give them the easiest possible route to precision. "I think you meant X. Is that right?" Offering the interpretation rather than demanding one removes the excuse, and if X is wrong they now have to say what is right.

---

> "That's just semantics." — Dismissing Definition When It Suits

**Why it works.** Deployed by whoever benefits from the looser word, right after they have themselves insisted on a precise one. It sounds like impatience with pedantry and functions as a refusal to be specific.

**What to say back.** "It's semantics when the meaning doesn't change the answer. Here it does, because X." Then show the two readings and the two different conclusions. That is the whole distinction, and stating it once tends to end this particular move for the rest of the conversation.

---

> "Before we go further, we should define our terms." — The Definitional Filibuster

**Why it works.** It is unimpeachable in principle, and in practice it can consume every minute available. Nobody can object to defining terms, so the argument never reaches the part where somebody might lose it.

**What to say back.** Timebox it out loud. "Agreed, five minutes, then we take the question as it stands." Naming a limit converts an infinite process into a finite one and makes any further stalling visible as stalling.

---

> "I never said that." — The Retroactive Edit

**Why it works.** Memory is weak and nobody has a transcript. In most rooms the confident denial beats the accurate recollection, and pursuing it makes you look like you are keeping score.

**What to say back.** Do keep score, and quote rather than characterise. "You said the figures were signed off. I wrote it down at the time." Then move on immediately, without demanding an admission. The record is the point; the apology is not worth the ten minutes.

---

> "If you're going to be pedantic about it." — Making Precision a Character Flaw

**Why it works.** It reframes accuracy as a personality defect. Once the room accepts that you are the pedant, every subsequent correction you make is discounted before it is heard, however material it turns out to be.

**What to say back.** Own it lightly and carry on. "I am, about this one, because the answer changes depending on it." Cheerful ownership defuses the label. Defending yourself against it confirms it.
