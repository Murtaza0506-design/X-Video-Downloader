# Chapter 8 — Weaponising Doubt

Doubt is the correct response to most claims, which is what makes it such good cover. Everything in this chapter is a virtue turned into a tool: scepticism, the demand for evidence, the awareness that experts have been wrong before, the recognition that correlation is not causation. Each of those is a genuine intellectual instrument. Each of them can be pointed at any claim whatsoever, indefinitely, without ever proposing anything.

That is the tell, and it is worth stating early because it is the only reliable one. Honest doubt is specific and it can be satisfied. It names what would change its mind. Weaponised doubt is general and permanent: whatever evidence arrives, there is a reason it does not count, and no evidence is ever described in advance that would settle the matter.

So the counter for the whole chapter is a single question, asked once and asked genuinely: **what would change your mind?** A serious sceptic has an answer, often a good one, and the conversation becomes worth having. Somebody using doubt as a shield will not answer it, and their refusal is more informative to everybody listening than any evidence you could produce.

Ask it without triumph. It is a real question, and about a third of the time you will get a real answer that improves your own position. The other two thirds it ends the move without a fight.

---

> "The science on this isn't settled." — Manufactured Uncertainty

**Why it works.** No science is ever fully settled, so the sentence is always true and always available. It converts a strong finding into an open question by describing the small remaining uncertainty as though it were the whole picture.

**What to say back.** Ask about the shape of the disagreement. "What's still open, and what's agreed?" Real live controversies have a location. When the answer is that everything is open, that is not a description of the field, it is a description of the strategy.

---

> "That study was funded by the industry, of course." — The Funding Smear

**Why it works.** Funding is a genuine source of bias, so the observation is legitimate and the inference is not. Noting who paid feels like doing due diligence while functioning as a complete substitute for reading the paper.

**What to say back.** Grant it and go to the method. "That's worth knowing. Does it change what they measured?" Bias operates through design and reporting, which are visible. If the funding did distort it, you can point at where. If you cannot, the objection has not yet done any work.

---

> "Correlation isn't causation." — A True Sentence Used as a Stop Sign

**Why it works.** It is correct and it sounds like the end of the discussion. Deployed as a full response rather than an opening one, it exempts the speaker from engaging with whatever the study did to address exactly that problem.

**What to say back.** Agree and continue. "No, so what would you want to see? They controlled for income and did a five-year follow-up." Causation is established by accumulation, not by a single result, and describing the accumulation is the answer this sentence pretends does not exist.

---

> "You can prove anything with statistics." — Blanket Discrediting

**Why it works.** It discredits an entire category of evidence in six words, and it costs nothing, and it feels worldly. Once accepted, no number can be introduced by anybody, which reliably suits whoever has fewer of them.

**What to say back.** "You can't, actually. What's wrong with these ones?" Said lightly. The general claim collapses the moment it has to become a specific criticism, and it turns out there is no specific criticism available.

---

> "We need more research before we do anything." — Perpetual Insufficiency

**Why it works.** More research is always possible, so the condition can never be met, and the delay it produces looks like prudence rather than opposition. It also puts the burden of certainty entirely on one side.

**What to say back.** Ask for the threshold. "How much would be enough, and by when?" A number and a date turn an indefinite delay into a plan. The absence of both is the answer, and it is a much clearer answer than any argument about the existing evidence.

---

> "There are good people on both sides of this." — False Balance

**Why it works.** Even-handedness is a virtue and this borrows its appearance. Where the evidence is lopsided, presenting two sides as equally weighted is itself a distortion, delivered in the voice of fairness.

**What to say back.** Accept the civility and restore the weights. "Both sides deserve a hearing. They don't have the same amount of evidence, and that's worth saying too." You have conceded nothing about tone while declining to concede the substance, which is the only version of this that does not sound partisan.

---

> "They told us eggs were bad for us, and now they're fine." — The Past Reversal Gambit

**Why it works.** It is true, it is memorable, and it generalises from a handful of famous reversals to everything. The implied conclusion is that current advice is as likely to flip as past advice, regardless of how well established it is.

**What to say back.** Point at the strength rather than the fact of the finding. "Some things reversed. The ones that reversed were thin to begin with. How strong is this one?" That distinction is the whole answer, and it also happens to be a useful way to read health news.

---

> "Show me one study that proves it definitively." — The Impossible Standard

**Why it works.** Proof is a word from mathematics and does not apply to empirical questions, so the demand can never be met. It sounds rigorous while asking for something that does not exist in any field.

**What to say back.** Name the achievable standard. "Nothing outside maths gets proved. What we've got is nine studies, three designs, same direction. What would you accept?" Refusing the word without refusing the scrutiny is the move here.

---

> "There are still a lot of unanswered questions." — Unspecified Doubt

**Why it works.** It gestures at problems without naming any, so there is nothing to answer and the impression of a shaky case lingers. It is also unfalsifiable: there are always unanswered questions.

**What to say back.** "Which ones? I'll go and look." The request is friendly and it is fatal. Either you get a list, which is useful and answerable, or the questions turn out not to exist in any specific form.

---

> "I'm just a sceptic by nature, that's all." — Scepticism as Identity

**Why it works.** It converts a position into a personality, which puts it beyond argument. You cannot rebut somebody's temperament, and framing doubt as a permanent trait excuses it from ever being satisfied by anything.

**What to say back.** Take the identity seriously and test it. "So am I. What would convince you?" Real scepticism is a method with an exit; it can say what would change its mind. Identity scepticism has no exit, and the question makes the difference plain.

---

> "Ask yourself who benefits from you believing that." — The Motive Inversion

**Why it works.** It replaces the question of whether a claim is true with the question of who gains, which is unanswerable and endless. It also flatters the listener as somebody who sees behind things.

**What to say back.** Answer it and move past it. "Plenty of people, probably. It's still either true or false, and here's how we'd check." Following the money is a legitimate investigative technique and a terrible epistemology, and the difference is whether it ever returns to the evidence.

---

> "I'm not saying it's wrong, I'm just raising the possibility." — The Costless Suggestion

**Why it works.** Raising a possibility puts it in the room while disclaiming responsibility for it. It requires no support, cannot be rebutted, and leaves a residue of doubt that outlasts any correction.

**What to say back.** Ask what the possibility rests on. "Is there anything behind it, or is it just possible?" Everything is possible. The distinction between a possibility with evidence and a possibility without one is the entire question, and drawing it politely ends the move.
