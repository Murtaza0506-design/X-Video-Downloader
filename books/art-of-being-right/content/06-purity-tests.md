# Chapter 6 — Purity Tests

There is a kind of argument that is not really an argument. It is an examination, and the subject is you. Not what you have said, but whether you have standing to say it: whether you care enough, whether your hands are clean, whether you were there before it was easy, whether your life is consistent with your position.

These moves are the most effective in this book among people who broadly agree with each other, which is what makes them worth a chapter. They are rarely used across a real divide, because they only work if you want the tester's approval. Between allies they are devastating, and they are how good arguments get lost inside groups that would have benefited from hearing them.

They also have a quality the other chapters mostly lack: they are frequently made in complete sincerity, by people who are upset. Something matters enormously to them, you have said something that sounds insufficiently urgent about it, and the test is the shape their distress takes. Treating that as a debating trick is both unkind and ineffective.

So the counter runs on two tracks. Acknowledge the thing underneath, which is usually real, and decline the test itself, which is not a claim and cannot be passed. The acknowledgement is what makes the refusal survivable. Without it you look cold, and looking cold is the outcome the test is best at producing.

There is one honest version in here and it is the last entry, because sometimes the person has no standing, and pretending otherwise helps nobody.

---

> "If you actually cared about this you'd have said something months ago." — The Sincerity Test

**Why it works.** It makes the timing of your concern the evidence for its authenticity, and there is no timing that passes. Early is opportunism, late is bandwagoning, and now is suspicious.

**What to say back.** Answer the question underneath rather than the charge. "I should have. I'm saying it now, and here's what I think we do." Agreeing you were late costs almost nothing and removes the only fuel the move had, which was your defensiveness about the date.

---

> "Honestly, you're part of the problem here." — Complicity Assignment

**Why it works.** It converts disagreement into causation. You are no longer someone with a different view; you are one of the reasons the situation persists, which means engaging with your argument would itself be a form of collaboration.

**What to say back.** Decline once, without indignation, and stay on the substance. "I might be. The proposal still has the flaw I described." Arguing about whether you are part of the problem is the trap, because it is unfalsifiable and it takes the rest of the evening.

---

> "Where were you when we needed people?" — The Attendance Record

**Why it works.** It substitutes a biography for an argument, and most biographies have gaps. It also has real moral weight behind it, because people who did the work when it was costly are owed something, and everybody in the room knows it.

**What to say back.** Give the credit and keep the claim. "You were there and I wasn't, and I'm grateful. This part still doesn't add up." Refusing to compete on record makes the move stop, and acknowledging the debt is not a concession on the substance.

---

> "That's a very comfortable thing to say from where you're sitting." — Position as Disqualification

**Why it works.** It attaches your argument to your circumstances, and comfort is disqualifying in a way that nothing else is. It cannot be denied, because you are indeed sitting where you are sitting.

**What to say back.** Concede the position, contest the inference. "It is comfortable, and I might be wrong for that reason. Is the number wrong?" Where somebody stands is a reason to check their work harder. It is not a finding about the work.

---

> "You can't say that and still fly to Spain every summer." — The Hypocrisy Catch

**Why it works.** Hypocrisy is unattractive, and pointing it out feels like a substantive win. What it actually establishes is a fact about the arguer, not about the argument, but the two are hard to keep apart in the moment.

**What to say back.** Admit it flatly and separate the questions. "I'm a hypocrite about this and the policy would still work." Immediate admission is disarming, because the move relies on your reluctance to concede. A hypocrite can be right, and an argument does not improve when a better person makes it.

---

> "So you're on their side now, are you." — Forced Alignment

**Why it works.** It converts a specific disagreement into a change of team, which raises the cost of the disagreement enormously. Most people will abandon a good point rather than be reassigned.

**What to say back.** Refuse the binary in one sentence. "I'm not on anyone's side. I think this particular claim is wrong, for this particular reason." The specificity is what breaks it. Sides deal in generalities, and a narrow claim is hard to conscript.

---

> "This isn't some academic debate. These are people's lives." — Denying the Argument's Standing

**Why it works.** It reframes analysis as indifference. If the stakes are high enough, examining the argument becomes a luxury, and the person examining it becomes someone who has failed to grasp the seriousness.

**What to say back.** Turn the stakes around. "That's exactly why the plan has to be right. If it doesn't work, those are the people it fails." High stakes are the strongest possible reason for scrutiny, not against it, and saying so makes the move backfire cleanly.

---

> "Read the room." — Social Pressure as Refutation

**Why it works.** It reports a fact about the crowd and offers it as a fact about the world. It is entirely honest about being an appeal to conformity, which is somehow what makes it so difficult to answer without sounding contrarian.

**What to say back.** Acknowledge the room and hold the point. "I know I'm the only one saying it. Is it wrong?" Being outnumbered is not evidence. Saying that calmly, without any suggestion that being outnumbered makes you right either, is the whole answer.

---

> "I really shouldn't have to explain this to you." — The Refusal to Argue

**Why it works.** It positions your question as so basic that answering it would be beneath the speaker, which converts their inability or unwillingness to explain into a comment on your ignorance.

**What to say back.** Accept the ignorance cheerfully. "You don't have to. But I'd understand it better if you did." Taking the low status on purpose removes the sting and leaves the request standing, which is the only thing you wanted.

---

> "Just do better." — The Unfalsifiable Demand

**Why it works.** It sounds like a standard and contains none. There is no action that satisfies it and no way to demonstrate compliance, so it can be repeated indefinitely regardless of what you actually do.

**What to say back.** Ask for the specific. "I'd like to. What would you want me to do differently?" A real complaint can answer that, and then you have something to act on. One that cannot has told you it was about position rather than behaviour.

---

> "That's an interesting position for you of all people to take." — The Implication

**Why it works.** It makes an accusation without stating one, so there is nothing to deny. Whatever the room imagines you are being accused of is worse and vaguer than anything the speaker would have said out loud.

**What to say back.** Bring it into the open, without heat. "Go on, say what you mean." Insinuation depends on not being asked to become a claim. Most of the time it will not survive the invitation, and either way the vagueness ends.

---

> "Nobody serious believes that any more." — The Fashion Argument

**Why it works.** It dates your position rather than refuting it, and being out of date is a social failure rather than an intellectual one, so there is nothing to argue with. It is also sometimes true, which is the honest version of this move.

**What to say back.** Ask what changed. "What came out that changed people's minds? I'll read it." If there is a genuine development, you have learned something and should update. If the answer is only that the mood moved, everybody has now heard that, and mood is not evidence.
