# Chapter 7 — Numbers That Lie

Almost none of the figures in this chapter are false. That is the thing worth understanding before the list. A dishonest statistic is rare, easy to catch and legally dangerous. A true statistic, chosen carefully and presented without its context, is safe, common, and far more effective.

The work is done by omission. Every number is a comparison in disguise, and the comparison is where the meaning lives. Three hundred percent of what. Two million out of how much. Nine out of ten of whom, asked by whom, out of how many who were asked and did not reply. Give a number without its denominator and it can mean whatever the room needs it to mean.

This puts an unpleasant obligation on you, which is that you have to do arithmetic in public, slowly, while somebody who has already done it watches. There is no way round that. The counters below are all versions of one question asked in different clothes: **compared to what?**

Two habits make it survivable. Ask for the base before you argue about the conclusion, because once the conclusion is on the table the base looks like a dodge. And be willing to say you cannot evaluate the figure yet, which sounds weak and is the single most defensible position available when you have been handed a number you have not seen before.

---

> "Complaints are up three hundred percent since the change." — The Percentage With No Base

**Why it works.** A percentage sounds like a measurement and is a ratio, and the ratio is meaningless without the starting number. Three complaints becoming twelve is three hundred percent, and so is thirty thousand becoming a hundred and twenty thousand.

**What to say back.** "Up from what to what?" Ask before responding to the implication. Four words, and about half the time the answer makes the point evaporate on the spot without you having to make any argument at all.

---

> "We've saved two million pounds." — The Number With No Denominator

**Why it works.** Two million is large in ordinary life and can be trivial or enormous in an institution, and without the total there is no way to know which. The figure borrows its weight from the listener's household rather than from the budget it came out of.

**What to say back.** "Out of what total, over what period?" Two million from four hundred million over five years is a rounding adjustment. The same figure from a twelve million pound budget in one year is a different story, and the phrasing is identical.

---

> "Nine out of ten users said they preferred it." — The Sample You Are Not Shown

**Why it works.** The ratio is reported and the sampling is not. Ten out of eleven people who bothered to respond to an email sent to people who had already opted in is a real nine out of ten, and it tells you nothing about users.

**What to say back.** "How many were asked, and how many answered?" Response rate is where most survey claims die. It is a neutral, technical question that nobody can take offence at, and it does more damage than any argument about methodology.

---

> "Since the policy came in, waiting times have fallen." — After, Therefore Because

**Why it works.** Two things happened in an order and the order implies the cause. It is the way human beings naturally read events, so it needs no argument to be persuasive and considerable argument to dislodge.

**What to say back.** Ask what else moved. "What were waiting times doing before the policy, and what happened in comparable places without it?" A trend that was already falling, or one that fell everywhere, answers the question and does so with the speaker's own data.

---

> "The average salary in the team is sixty-eight thousand." — The Mean Hiding the Spread

**Why it works.** The mean is dragged by extremes, so one very large number at the top can lift the average well above what almost everybody actually earns. The word *average* implies typical, and here it means nothing of the sort.

**What to say back.** "What's the median?" One word. The gap between mean and median is the whole story, and asking for it is so obviously reasonable that refusing looks worse than any answer.

---

> "The result was statistically significant." — Significance Mistaken for Size

**Why it works.** The technical meaning is that an effect is unlikely to be zero. The everyday meaning is that it matters. The two get conflated , and a real but tiny effect gets discussed as though it were a large one.

**What to say back.** "Significant, but how big is the effect?" A drug that reliably lowers something by half a percent is significant and useless. Separating the two questions is not scepticism about the study; it is reading it correctly.

---

> "That difference is within the margin of error." — Margin of Error as a Dismissal

**Why it works.** It sounds like rigour and is often deployed selectively, to dismiss unwelcome results while accepting equally uncertain ones that point the other way. Most listeners will not track the asymmetry.

**What to say back.** Apply it evenly. "Then the one you cited earlier is too, isn't it?" Consistency is the counter to selective rigour, and pointing at the earlier figure does it in one sentence without any accusation.

---

> "Just look at the graph, the line is nearly vertical." — The Truncated Axis

**Why it works.** A vertical axis starting at ninety-four rather than zero turns a two percent change into a cliff. Nothing is falsified. The eye reads slope as magnitude and the axis label goes unread.

**What to say back.** "What does the y-axis start at?" Then read the two end values aloud. Saying the actual numbers out loud is what undoes the picture, because the picture was doing work the numbers cannot support.

---

> "Compared to 2020, we're doing enormously better." — The Chosen Baseline

**Why it works.** Every series has a best year and a worst year to be compared against, and the choice is made silently. 2020 is the great modern example, because almost any measure improves when the comparison is the year the world stopped.

**What to say back.** "What about against 2019, and against the ten-year average?" Asking for two more baselines rather than one avoids a fight about which single year is fair, and the shape of the answer is the answer.

---

> "Only nought point one percent of cases were affected." — The Small Rate on a Big Number

**Why it works.** The rate is chosen because it is small, when the count is the thing that matters. A tenth of a percent of forty million is forty thousand people, and the sentence is designed so that nobody performs that multiplication.

**What to say back.** Perform it out loud. "That's forty thousand people. How many is acceptable?" Converting the rate to a count takes four seconds and changes the emotional weight of the discussion , without disputing the figure at all.

---

> "There's no evidence of any harm." — Absence of Evidence

**Why it works.** It is technically true whenever nobody has looked, and it sounds like a finding of safety. The gap between *we found nothing* and *nobody has checked* is invisible in the phrasing.

**What to say back.** "Has anyone looked, and how hard?" A well-designed study that found nothing is reassuring and worth saying so. No study at all is not, and the same sentence covers both.

---

> "I'll let the data speak for itself." — Pretending Numbers Are Not Interpreted

**Why it works.** It presents a selection and a framing as though it were raw reality, and it positions any disagreement as an argument with arithmetic rather than with the person who chose what to show.

**What to say back.** "Data doesn't speak. What did you leave out?" Not hostile, just accurate. Every chart is a set of decisions about inclusion, axis and comparison, and naming that returns the argument to the person who made them.
