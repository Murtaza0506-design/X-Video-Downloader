# Chapter 1 — Changing the Subject

You made a point. It was a good one, and you can tell it was good because nobody addressed it. What came back was adjacent: a related grievance, a wider principle, a question about your tone. Ten minutes later you are defending something you never said, and the original point is lying in the road behind you where it was dropped.

This is the commonest move in the book and the hardest to catch in real time, because each individual swerve is small. Nobody announces that they are leaving the subject. They arrive somewhere else by a series of reasonable-looking steps, and by the time you notice, going back looks pedantic.

That last part is the engine of the whole family. The cost of returning to the point is social, and it is paid by you. You are the one who has to say *hang on, we were talking about something else*, and in front of an audience that makes you the difficult one while the person who moved the ground looks relaxed and expansive.

Which is why the counter to nearly all of it is the same, and why it feels rude the first few times you do it: name the swerve, out loud, without heat, and put the original question back on the table. Not as an accusation. As bookkeeping.

The moves below are sorted roughly by how respectable they look. The first few are recognisable as evasion by anybody watching. The last few are indistinguishable from good faith, which is what makes them worth knowing.

---

> "Fine, but what about what your lot did in 2019?" — Whataboutery

**Why it works.** It is not a defence and it is not meant to be. It is a change of subject that feels like a rebuttal, because it puts you on the back foot morally. You now have two choices, and both lose: defend 2019, or refuse and look like you are dodging.

**What to say back.** Take the third option. "That might well be true. It doesn't make this true or false either way, so let's do that one after." You have conceded nothing, refused nothing, and put the swerve on a shelf where it can be collected later. It almost never is.

---

> "I think you're missing the bigger picture here." — The Zoom Out

**Why it works.** Any specific claim can be made to look small by standing further back. The move trades a checkable statement for an unfalsifiable one, and it flatters the person making it as the one with perspective while you fuss over particulars.

**What to say back.** Agree and hold. "Probably. The bigger picture still has to be made of true things, and this one is the bit I'm asking about." The bigger picture is not a place where specific facts stop mattering, and saying so out loud makes the move visible to everyone listening.

---

> "The real question is whether we should be doing this at all." — Substituting the Question

**Why it works.** It sounds like depth. It reframes your question as superficial and replaces it with one the speaker would rather answer, and because the new question is larger, arguing to keep the old one looks small-minded.

**What to say back.** "That's a real question and I'd like to get to it. This one has an answer we can reach today, so can we finish it first?" Sequencing beats refusing. You are not saying their question is bad, only that it is next.

---

> "Look, honestly, I think we're saying the same thing." — The False Merge

**Why it works.** It ends the disagreement by declaring it over, and it does so generously, which makes contradicting it feel churlish. If you accept, the difference between your positions quietly becomes yours to have imagined.

**What to say back.** "We might be. Let me check: do you think X?" Name the specific claim you believe you differ on. Either they agree and the merge was real, or they do not and the disagreement is back on the table with its outline sharper than before.

---

> "Let's not get bogged down in the details." — Dismissing the Specific

**Why it works.** Details are where claims are checkable, so the move removes the ground on which the speaker can lose. It also borrows the language of leadership. Big people see the shape of things; small people count.

**What to say back.** "Happy not to, but this one is load-bearing." Then say what it holds up. A detail that changes the conclusion is not a detail, and once you have shown which conclusion depends on it, refusing to discuss it stops looking like decisiveness.

---

> "That's a whole other conversation." — Deferring Forever

**Why it works.** It sounds like discipline. Perfectly reasonable in a meeting with an agenda, and the trick is that the other conversation is never scheduled. The point is not postponed; it is disposed of politely.

**What to say back.** Take the deferral literally. "Agreed. When?" A date makes the difference between managing an agenda and burying a topic, and the answer tells you which one you are in without either of you having to say so.

---

> "Anyway, as I was saying before, the thing about the budget is..." — Talking Over the Answer

**Why it works.** It does not engage the answer at all. It behaves as though the answer did not happen, and because interrupting to point that out costs you the room's sympathy, the non-response stands.

**What to say back.** Wait for the pause, then flatly: "Before the budget, I answered your question about the timeline. Do you want to respond to that, or shall we move on?" The offer to move on is what makes it survivable. It gives them an exit while making the skip a matter of record.

---

> "You've clearly thought about this a great deal." — The Compliment Exit

**Why it works.** Praise closes a conversation more effectively than disagreement. It signals warmth, ends the exchange, and concedes nothing, and anyone who pushes past a compliment looks like they cannot take yes for an answer.

**What to say back.** Take the warmth and keep going. "Thank you. So where do you land on it?" Pleasant, unmoved, and it converts the compliment back into a question, which was what you asked for.

---

> "Well, it's more complicated than that." — Complexity as a Shield

**Why it works.** It is almost always true, which is what makes it so useful. Everything is more complicated than any summary of it. Stated without a follow-up, it functions as a refusal dressed as sophistication.

**What to say back.** "It usually is. Which part have I got wrong?" Complexity that cannot name the specific simplification is not complexity, it is a stop sign. Asking makes them either supply the correction, which is useful, or reveal there wasn't one.

---

> "I mean, at the end of the day, who's to say what's true?" — The Fog Machine

**Why it works.** By making everything uncertain, it makes your specific claim no better than theirs. It is the argumentative equivalent of turning off the lights: nobody can see anything, which suits whoever was losing.

**What to say back.** Bring it down to something small and checkable. "Was the report delivered on the fourteenth?" Universal doubt evaporates on contact with a date, an invoice or a document, and it becomes obvious that the fog was tactical.

---

> "I think we're just going to have to agree to disagree." — The Premature Truce

**Why it works.** It sounds like maturity and it is often exactly right. The abuse is in the timing: deployed the moment a position is in trouble, it converts a losing argument into a respectable draw, and refusing it makes you the aggressor.

**What to say back.** Accept it and put down a marker. "Fine by me. For the record, the bit I don't think has been answered is X." You have granted the truce, which costs you nothing, while declining to let the unanswered thing be filed as answered.

---

> "Sorry, what were we even talking about?" — Losing the Thread on Purpose

**Why it works.** After enough diversions, genuine confusion is indistinguishable from manufactured confusion, and the person who restates the question does the work while sounding obsessive for having tracked it.

**What to say back.** Have it written down. This is the entire counter, and it is why people who argue for a living take notes. "You asked whether the figures were signed off. I said no. You asked who by." Reading it back is unanswerable, and the fact that you can do it once changes how the next hour goes.
