# A Note on Sources

The moves in this book were not invented here. Most of them have been catalogued for a very long time, under names that are worth knowing if you want to read further.

## Where the tradition comes from

Aristotle's *Rhetoric* and *Sophistical Refutations* are the origin of the whole enterprise, and the second in particular is a list of ways an argument can appear valid without being so. It is two and a half thousand years old and the examples still land.

Schopenhauer's *The Art of Being Right*, from which this book takes its title, is a short and unusually cynical catalogue of thirty-eight stratagems for winning a dispute regardless of the truth. He wrote it as a description of what people do, and its bleakness is the reason it has never gone out of print. Several entries here are his, restated in modern speech: the diversion, the extension of your opponent's claim beyond what they said, the retreat into definitions, the appeal to authority the audience will not check, and the final stratagem, which is to become personal when everything else has failed.

The Latin names come from the medieval logicians and are still the most precise labels available. *Ad hominem* for attacking the person, *ignoratio elenchi* for refuting something other than the point at issue, *petitio principii* for smuggling the conclusion into the premises. This book mostly avoids them, because naming a fallacy in Latin during an argument is itself a move from chapter four.

## What is original here

The classification, the modern examples, the explanation of why each move works on people rather than why it is invalid, and every reply. The last of those is the reason the book exists. The classical sources are excellent at identifying what is wrong with an argument and almost silent on what to say when somebody does it to you in a meeting.

## On the naming

Some moves have accepted names and those are used. Others have several, and the clearest was chosen rather than the oldest. A number have no standard name at all, and have been given one here that describes what they do. Where a name is this book's invention rather than established usage, no claim is made that anybody else calls it that.

## What this book is not

It is not a logic textbook, and several entries are not fallacies in the technical sense at all. Running out the clock is not invalid reasoning; it is not reasoning. Emotional leverage may accompany a perfectly sound argument. The organising question throughout is not *is this formally invalid* but *is this how the question gets closed without being answered*, which is a wider and more practical net.

Nor is it complete. It is a working catalogue of the moves that appear most often in ordinary rooms, and there are many more.
