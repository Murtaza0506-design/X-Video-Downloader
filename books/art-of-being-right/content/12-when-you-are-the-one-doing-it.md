# Chapter 12 — When You Are the One Doing It

Everything so far has been written from one side of the table. That was a convenience, and it was also a lie, because the honest version of this subject is that you use these moves. Not the crude ones. The respectable ones: the zoom out when the detail was against you, the request for a source you had no intention of reading, the sarcastic restatement that got a laugh, the offline that you never followed up.

You will have recognised some of them while reading and felt the small hot flush of it. That reaction is the most useful thing this book produces, and it is worth more than any of the counters, because the counters only protect you from other people.

There is a reason it happens to everyone. Once you believe a thing, the mind stops looking for reasons and starts looking for arguments, and those are different activities that feel identical from the inside. Reasons are what you use to decide. Arguments are what you use to win. The moment the conclusion is fixed, every rhetorical move becomes available and none of them feels dishonest, because you are not trying to deceive anybody. You are trying to bring them to something you already know is true.

That is the condition this chapter is about, and it has no clean solution. What it has are symptoms, and the entries below are the ones worth learning to notice in yourself, in the middle of the argument, while there is still time to stop.

The last one is the point of the chapter, and possibly the point of the book.

---

> "I'm just being honest." — Honesty as Licence

**Why it works.** It converts an unkindness into a virtue, and it does so sincerely. Being honest and being unkind are independent, and the phrase is almost always deployed for something that was true and did not need saying.

**What to say back.** Ask yourself the second question. Would you have said it if it were kind rather than cutting? Honesty that only surfaces when it wounds is not a commitment to truth, it is a permission slip, and you can hear yourself reaching for it.

---

> "They started it." — The Retaliation Excuse

**Why it works.** It feels like justice and it is a decision to lower your own standard. It is also structurally unavailable to check, since both sides remember starting second.

**What to say back.** Notice that you now have their standard rather than yours. If the move was bad when it was done to you, it did not improve on the way back. The only person whose conduct you were ever going to control has just stopped controlling it.

---

> "Well, it worked, didn't it." — Effectiveness as Justification

**Why it works.** Winning is real and visible, and the cost is neither. It is true that the move succeeded, and irrelevant to whether the position it defended was correct.

**What to say back.** Ask what you won. If you got agreement without changing anybody's understanding, you have a compliance that will not survive the first time somebody checks. That is not a win with a long shelf life.

---

> "I knew I was right, I just couldn't explain why at the time." — Conclusion First

**Why it works.** Sometimes it is true; intuition runs ahead of articulation and is often correct. It is also the exact sensation of motivated reasoning, and the two are indistinguishable from inside.

**What to say back.** Write the reason down afterwards, when nobody is watching. If it comes easily, the intuition was doing real work. If you find yourself constructing rather than recalling, you have learned something uncomfortable and useful about that particular certainty.

---

> "I'd stopped listening about halfway through." — Deciding Before They Finished

**Why it works.** Attention is expensive and prediction is cheap, so the mind fills in the rest of somebody's argument from the first few sentences and spends the remainder preparing a reply. It feels like efficiency.

**What to say back.** Try summarising their position back to them before you answer it. If you cannot, you were not listening, you were queueing. This one habit removes about half of the straw men you would otherwise commit.

---

> "There was no point explaining the real reason to them." — Condescension as Kindness

**Why it works.** It presents a decision to be less than honest as consideration for somebody's limits, and it is occasionally correct. Mostly it is a way of avoiding a reason you would rather not defend.

**What to say back.** Ask whether you could say the real reason out loud to them. If the answer is that they would disagree, that is not a reason to withhold it. If the answer is that they would be right to, you have found the actual problem.

---

> "I had them on the ropes, so I kept going." — Not Stopping at Enough

**Why it works.** Momentum is pleasurable, and there is no natural signal to stop once the point is made. Continuing past the point converts a persuaded person into a humiliated one, and humiliated people do not concede.

**What to say back.** Watch for the moment they stop defending and start absorbing. That is the end. Anything after it is not argument, it is collection, and it costs you the agreement you had just earned.

---

> "I never actually said it was false." — Technically True

**Why it works.** It preserves deniability by leaving the claim implied rather than stated, which means you can believe yourself to have been accurate while knowing exactly what impression you left.

**What to say back.** Ask what they walked away believing, and whether you intended that. Communication is measured at the receiver. A sentence that is defensible in a transcript and misleading in a room was chosen for that property.

---

> "I was fairly sure, so I said it." — Confidence Without Checking

**Why it works.** Checking is slow and confidence is free, and in most conversations nobody verifies anything. You will be right often enough that the times you are not will not correct the habit.

**What to say back.** Calibrate out loud. "I think it's about forty percent, but I'd want to check." It costs one clause and it makes you more credible rather than less, because the times you do state something flatly then mean something.

---

> "I wanted them to feel it." — Wanting to Win More Than to Be Right

**Why it works.** There is genuine pleasure in landing a line, and it is a different appetite from wanting to be understood. It usually appears when something else has made you feel small earlier in the day.

**What to say back.** Notice the appetite and treat it as information about you rather than about them. It is a reliable sign that this argument has stopped being about the subject, and the best available move is to stop talking for a minute.

---

> "I'm not going to apologise for caring about this." — Refusing to Concede Anything

**Why it works.** Caring is real and it makes any retreat feel like a betrayal of the thing you care about. So you defend every part of your position equally, including the weak parts, which is how a good case gets discredited by its worst component.

**What to say back.** Concede the weakest part first, deliberately, out loud. It is the single most persuasive thing available to anybody in an argument, and it costs you the part you could not defend anyway.

---

> "You know what, you're right. I hadn't thought of that." — Losing on Purpose

**Why it works.** Nothing in this book is more effective and almost nobody uses it. Conceding immediately and without conditions ends the contest, and what is left is two people looking at the same problem, which was supposed to be the point.

**What to say back.** This is the exit, and it is the last thing in the book for a reason. You are allowed to lose. You are allowed to say you have changed your mind, in public, without a face-saving clause attached. And you are allowed to walk away from an argument you could win, with somebody who is never going to change their mind, about something that will not matter by Friday. Recognising the moves is useful. Not needing to win is better, and it is available at any point, including right now, in the argument you are already having.
