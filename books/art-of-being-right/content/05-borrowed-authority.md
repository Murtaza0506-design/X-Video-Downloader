# Chapter 5 — Borrowed Authority

Nobody has time to verify anything, and everybody knows it. That is the gap these moves live in. An argument that would take an hour to establish can be won in four seconds by attaching it to something the room already trusts: a study, a discipline, a famous name, thirty years of experience, common sense.

None of this is illegitimate in itself. Deference to expertise is not a fallacy; it is how anyone functions in a world too large to check personally. You trust the pharmacist. The abuse is not in citing authority, it is in citing an authority that has not said the thing, or that has no standing on the question, or that cannot be located at all.

Which gives the whole chapter a single counter, applied with different manners depending on the room: ask for the reference. Not aggressively, and not as a challenge to the person's honesty. As interest. *Where can I read that?* is a sentence that costs nothing to say, is impossible to object to, and is fatal to about eighty percent of the moves below.

The reason it works is that the borrowed authority is real but vague, and vagueness cannot survive a request for specifics. Either you get a source, in which case you have learned something and the conversation improves, or you do not, and everybody has watched you not get one.

Ask once, mildly, and then let the silence do the work. Asking twice turns it into a fight about your scepticism.

---

> "Studies show that it actually increases costs." — The Unnamed Study

**Why it works.** The word *studies*, plural and anonymous, borrows the entire authority of research without accepting any of its accountability. There is nothing to check, and doubting it sounds like doubting science.

**What to say back.** "Which one? I'd genuinely like to read it." The word *genuinely* matters, because it removes the accusation. Most of the time no study is produced, and the claim deflates without you ever having called anyone a liar.

---

> "Every expert in the field agrees." — The Consensus Claim

**Why it works.** It makes disagreement into eccentricity. Whatever you say next is positioned against the whole profession, and the audience has no way to check whether the consensus exists or whether it covers this exact question.

**What to say back.** Narrow it to the claim. "Agrees on what precisely? There's usually consensus on some parts and not others." Real consensus survives specificity easily. Invented consensus is always broader than any real one, and asking exposes the width.

---

> "As Einstein said, insanity is doing the same thing and expecting different results." — The Misattributed Quote

**Why it works.** The name does the persuading, not the sentence. Four names attract orphaned quotations more than any others: Einstein, Twain, Churchill and the Buddha. A neat line under any of them deserves suspicion, and this particular one is not Einstein's at all.

**What to say back.** Leave the attribution alone unless it matters, and go at the claim. "Whoever said it, is it true here? We've changed three things since last time." Correcting the source scores a point and loses the thread; testing the sentence keeps the argument where you want it.

---

> "I've been doing this for thirty years." — Experience as Trump Card

**Why it works.** Experience is valuable and impossible to acquire quickly, so it cannot be matched in the room. It also converts disagreement into disrespect, which most people will avoid at some cost to their argument.

**What to say back.** Ask experience to do what only experience can. "Then you'll have seen this before. What happened those times?" Thirty years used as a claim is an assertion of rank; thirty years used as data is useful, and the question invites the second.

---

> "Do your own research." — Outsourcing the Burden

**Why it works.** It shifts the entire cost of establishing a claim onto the person who doubts it, while allowing the claim to stand in the meantime. It also implies your ignorance is the reason for the disagreement.

**What to say back.** Decline the transfer, once, pleasantly. "You made the claim, so you'll have the source to hand. Where is it?" Whoever asserts, supports. That rule is not pedantry; it is the only thing that makes discussion possible at all.

---

> "It's basic economics, honestly." — The Discipline Invocation

**Why it works.** Naming a field implies that the answer is settled within it and that you would know this if you were educated. It works especially well with economics, physics and law, which most people have encountered enough to feel unqualified about.

**What to say back.** Ask which part. "Which bit of it? I want to look it up." Genuine textbook results are easy to name. When the invocation is a bluff, the specific never arrives, and the field goes back to being irrelevant to the question.

---

> "Common sense tells you that won't work." — Common Sense as Authority

**Why it works.** It presents a contested claim as something obvious to any reasonable person, which makes disagreement a sign of either foolishness or ideology. It also requires no evidence at all, which is its great efficiency.

**What to say back.** "Common sense also said heavier things fall faster. What's the evidence here?" One counterexample is enough. The point is not that common sense is worthless, only that it is a hypothesis rather than a verdict.

---

> "Even the auditors agree with me on this." — The Endorsement Grab

**Why it works.** It recruits a third party who is not in the room to defend a position they may not hold, and because checking would be awkward, the endorsement usually stands unexamined for the length of the conversation.

**What to say back.** Ask what they agreed to. "Agreed on which finding? I read the report differently." Endorsements shrink under specificity. Often it turns out the third party agreed with something adjacent, which is worth knowing and worth saying out loud.

---

> "That's been thoroughly debunked." — The Debunk Claim

**Why it works.** It asserts that the argument is not merely wrong but already dealt with, so engaging with it would be a waste of everyone's time. It borrows the authority of a process that may never have happened.

**What to say back.** "By whom, and where? I'd like to see it." Same request as always. A real debunking is easy to point at and settles the matter properly, which is a good outcome. A claimed one dissolves.

---

> "It's peer reviewed, actually." — The Credential of the Source

**Why it works.** It answers a question about the content with a fact about the container. Peer review is a filter, not a guarantee, and treating it as a guarantee ends the conversation with the appearance of rigour.

**What to say back.** Accept the credential and ask about the finding. "Good, so what did it actually measure?" Sample size, method, and what the paper claims as opposed to what the headline claims are all fair questions that respect the review process rather than dismissing it.

---

> "My cousin works in the hospital and she says it's nothing like that." — The Anecdotal Insider

**Why it works.** A named human being with proximity beats an abstract statistic every time. It also cannot be checked and cannot be doubted politely, since doubting it means doubting somebody's cousin.

**What to say back.** Give the anecdote its due and set it beside the data. "That's one hospital, and it might well be the worst one. What does the national figure say?" Nothing here calls the cousin a liar, which is what makes it possible to say.

---

> "Look, I'm not an expert, but it seems obvious to me that..." — The Humble Shield

**Why it works.** The disclaimer buys immunity in advance. If the claim is challenged, the speaker has already said they are no expert, so a rebuttal looks like bullying somebody who was being modest.

**What to say back.** Take the modesty at face value and treat the claim normally. "Nor am I. Here's what the figures show." The shield only works if you attack the person. Answering the claim as though it were offered seriously, which it was, leaves the disclaimer with nothing to protect.
