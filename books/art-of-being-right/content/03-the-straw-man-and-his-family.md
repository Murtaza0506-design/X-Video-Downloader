# Chapter 3 — The Straw Man and His Family

The straw man is the most famous move in this book and the least well understood. Everybody can name it. Almost nobody catches it happening to them in the moment, because a good one does not misrepresent you wildly. It misrepresents you slightly, in a direction that is easier to attack, and it does so while sounding like a fair summary offered in good faith.

The tell is that the restatement always makes your position more extreme, never less. Nobody has ever accidentally paraphrased an opponent into a more reasonable position than the one they held. That asymmetry is the whole family resemblance, and once you have noticed it you cannot stop seeing it.

There is a second thing worth knowing, which is that the person doing it is often sincere. Arguments are exhausting and the mind economises by simplifying the other side into something manageable. Half the straw men you will meet are not tactics; they are laziness that happens to be self-serving. This matters for the counter, because treating a sincere simplification as a deliberate smear turns a solvable exchange into a fight.

So the counter is nearly always the same shape, and it is boring on purpose: restate your own position, in one sentence, without complaint about the misrepresentation. Not *that isn't what I said*, which invites a row about what you said. Just the sentence, again, cleanly. Then ask the question again.

Do it twice and most people correct course. Do it three times and you have learned something about who you are talking to.

---

> "So what you're saying is we should just do nothing." — The Straw Man

**Why it works.** The exaggerated version is easy to refute, so refuting it produces the feeling of a win in the room. It also puts you on defence, which means you have stopped advancing your own case for as long as the correction takes.

**What to say back.** Give the sentence, not the objection. "What I'm saying is we should do the first two and wait on the third." No indignation, no accusation. The clean restatement is faster than the argument about whether they misrepresented you, and it works on sincere and cynical versions alike.

---

> "So basically you don't care about the people affected." — The Motive Upgrade

**Why it works.** It converts a disagreement about policy into a claim about your character, and character is much harder to defend than a position. Any defence sounds like protesting too much, and refusing to defend sounds like an admission.

**What to say back.** Decline the frame flatly, once. "I care about them and I think this makes it worse for them. That's the disagreement." Then return to the substance without waiting. Litigating your own decency is a trap with no exit; declining it in one sentence is the only version that works.

---

> "By that logic we'd have to ban ladders." — The Reductio That Isn't

**Why it works.** A real reduction to absurdity shows that your principle produces a conclusion you would reject. The fake version applies your principle to a case it never covered, and it lands because the audience rarely checks whether the extension was legitimate.

**What to say back.** Name the missing condition. "My argument had a threshold in it. Ladders don't cross it." If the principle does entail the absurd case, they have found a real hole and you should say so; if it does not, showing the join ends the move immediately.

---

> "What you're really arguing for is a two-tier system." — Telling You What You Think

**Why it works.** The word *really* claims access to your actual position underneath your stated one. It is unanswerable by denial, because denying it can be presented as further evidence that you have not admitted it even to yourself.

**What to say back.** Refuse the archaeology. "I'll tell you what I'm arguing for. It's X." Say it once, without heat. You are the authority on your own claim, and asserting that calmly is more effective than proving a negative about your hidden intentions.

---

> "Suppose someone said all landlords are criminals. Would you defend that?" — The Hypothetical Sock Puppet

**Why it works.** An invented opponent is introduced who holds a position nobody in the room does, and you are asked to answer for them. Refuse and you look evasive; engage and you have spent your time on somebody who does not exist.

**What to say back.** Decline the hypothetical and offer the real one. "I've no idea, nobody here said that. Ask me about what I did say." Short and unembarrassed. The invented opponent only survives while somebody agrees to debate them.

---

> "Once you allow this, where does it end?" — The Slippery Slope

**Why it works.** The chain always sounds plausible because each individual link is small, and refuting it requires arguing about a future nobody can check. It also carries real force, because sometimes slopes are genuinely slippery.

**What to say back.** Ask for the mechanism. "What's the step from here to there, and what stops it?" A real slope can name the mechanism, and then it is a serious argument you should take seriously. A fake one names only the destination, and asking makes that gap obvious.

---

> "That's exactly what they said in the 1930s." — Guilt by Historical Association

**Why it works.** It attaches a repellent association to a position without claiming any actual similarity, and the association does all the work. Denying it means talking about the 1930s, which is time not spent on the question.

**What to say back.** Test whether the analogy carries anything. "Which part of the comparison do you think holds?" Almost always the answer is the surface, and asking makes the speaker either supply a real parallel worth discussing or drop it quietly.

---

> "I'm just asking questions." — Just Asking Questions

**Why it works.** It advances a claim while retaining the right to deny having made one. The question plants the implication and the disclaimer removes the responsibility, so there is nothing to rebut and everything to worry about.

**What to say back.** Convert the question into the claim it contains. "The question implies X. Do you think X?" A yes is progress, because now there is an actual position on the table. A no should be followed by dropping the question, and if it is not, everyone can see it.

---

> "A lot of people are saying the figures were manipulated." — The Anonymous Ally

**Why it works.** It borrows the weight of a crowd without naming anybody, so there is no source to check and no claim the speaker owns. Disagreeing puts you against an unspecified many rather than against one person in the room.

**What to say back.** Ask who, without sarcasm. "Which people? I'd like to read it." If the crowd is real you get a source and learn something. If it is not, the request is enough, and it is noticeably harder to run the move a second time.

---

> "Oh, so we should just trust them, should we." — The Sarcastic Restatement

**Why it works.** Sarcasm delivers a straw man while pre-emptively mocking the correction, so pointing out the misrepresentation also means being the person who cannot take a joke. Two moves in one sentence, and the second protects the first.

**What to say back.** Answer the literal question as though it were sincere. "Not blindly, no. I'd check the audit, which is what I'm suggesting." Treating sarcasm as a real question strips it of its cover, and it does so without ever addressing the tone, which is where the fight would be.

---

> "Nobody is saying we should abolish the whole thing." — The Pre-emptive Straw Man

**Why it works.** It is aimed at a position you have not taken, and it inoculates the room against you taking anything near it. Whatever you now propose is measured against the extreme version that was raised on your behalf and disowned.

**What to say back.** Take back the range. "Agreed, and I'm not. What I'm proposing is the narrow version, which is X." State it early and specifically. Once the extreme is in the air, the only cure is to be conspicuously more precise than the situation seems to need.

---

> "That's a strawman." — The Straw Man Accusation as a Move

**Why it works.** The name of the fallacy has become a move in its own right. Deployed against an accurate summary, it lets somebody dodge a fair characterisation of their position by accusing you of the very thing you have not done.

**What to say back.** Offer them the pen. "Then give me the version you'd sign." If they produce one, you now have their position in their own words, which is what you wanted. If they will not, the accusation was doing work their argument could not.
