# Introduction

This is a catalogue of one hundred and forty-four ways an argument is won without being right, and what to say when one is used on you. Each entry is a line you would hear, the name of the move underneath it, why it works on people, and a reply.

It is a defensive book. That distinction matters enough to make at the start, because a list of effective dishonest techniques is also, unavoidably, a manual for using them. Everything here works. Several of these moves will win you arguments this week if you decide to use them, and the reason to know them is that they are being used on you at a rate you have probably underestimated.

**How to read it.** Not in order. Twelve chapters of rhetorical analysis taken in sequence produces the sensation of being lectured at, and none of it survives contact with a real conversation. The book is arranged by the shape of the move. Somebody keeps changing the subject: chapter one. Somebody is attacking you instead of your point: chapter four. Somebody is citing studies nobody can find: chapter five. Somebody has buried the thing in process for five months: chapter eleven.

**On the replies.** Every entry ends with something to say, and they share a family resemblance you will notice by about the third chapter. Nearly all of them do the same two things: concede whatever is true, at once and without reluctance, and then return the conversation to the question that was asked. Almost nothing here is clever. Cleverness escalates, and escalation is how you lose a room.

**On tone.** The replies are written flat on purpose. Delivered with any heat at all, most of them become attacks, and an attack invites the whole of chapter four in return. The counter to a rhetorical move is not a better rhetorical move; it is a boring, specific question asked in an ordinary voice, and it works because it is hard to look reasonable while refusing to answer one.

**On the people using these.** Assume good faith for longer than feels natural. Perhaps half of the moves in this book are committed by people who are tired, who have simplified your position because holding it accurately is effortful, or who believe the thing they have not checked. Treating a sincere simplification as a deliberate smear converts a solvable exchange into a fight, and you will do that at least once.

**On yourself.** Chapter twelve is about the moves you use, and it is not decoration. If you read this book and come away with nothing but a sharper eye for other people's tactics, it will have made you worse company and no closer to the truth about anything.

**One caution.** Not every disagreement is an argument to be won, and quite a few of the moves catalogued here are used by people who are frightened rather than dishonest. If somebody you love is running chapter ten on you, the correct response is probably not a counter from this book. It is to stop arguing and ask what is actually wrong.

Otherwise: start with whichever chapter matches the conversation you keep losing.
