# Chapter 11 — Running Out the Clock

Some arguments are not won. They are outlasted. The point is never refuted, it is deferred, referred, widened, minuted and scheduled until the moment for acting on it has passed, and then it stops being relevant on its own. Nobody has to say no. The calendar says it for them.

This is the characteristic move of institutions rather than individuals, and it is worth a chapter because it is nearly invisible from inside. Every step is procedurally correct. Taking something offline is sensible. Getting the right people in the room is sensible. Checking with legal is sensible. It is only when you stand back and notice that eleven sensible steps have produced no decision in five months that the shape becomes clear.

The person doing it often does not experience themselves as doing anything. They are being careful, and being careful is indistinguishable from stalling except in one respect: stalling never has a date attached.

That is the whole counter, and it is the same for every entry below. **Attach a date and a name to everything.** Who is doing it, by when, and what happens if it does not happen. Say it out loud in the meeting and put it in the note afterwards. Do that consistently and most of this chapter becomes unusable against you, without you ever having accused anybody of anything.

Write things down. It is unglamorous and it is the single most effective habit in this book.

---

> "Let's take this offline." — The Offline Burial

**Why it works.** It removes the item from the room where a decision could be witnessed and moves it somewhere with no minutes and no attendees. Perfectly reasonable for detail, and reliably fatal to anything raised inconveniently.

**What to say back.** "Happy to. You and me, Thursday morning?" Naming two people and a day converts the offline into an actual appointment. Without that, offline means nowhere.

---

> "Let me come back to you on that." — The Indefinite Deferral

**Why it works.** It is a promise with no due date, which means it is not a promise. It also feels like progress in the moment, so nobody objects, and the absence of a deadline is only noticed weeks later.

**What to say back.** "By when? I'll put it in the note." The second half is what matters. A deferral that appears in writing with a date has to be either honoured or explicitly broken, and most people would rather honour it.

---

> "We should get the right people in the room before we decide." — Widening to Stall

**Why it works.** It is unanswerable, because the right people should be in the room, and every additional person makes scheduling harder. Widening is the most respectable way to slow anything down.

**What to say back.** "Who's missing, and can we get them this week?" Naming the gap makes it fillable. Left unnamed, the missing people multiply each time the question comes up.

---

> "Could you put that in writing for me?" — Process as Friction

**Why it works.** It converts a two-minute conversation into an hour of work, and it is legitimate, which is the beauty of it. Repeated a few times, it makes raising things expensive enough that people stop.

**What to say back.** Do it, and make it short. Three sentences and a question, not a document. Then send it with a date on the reply. The friction only works if you produce something long enough to be worth avoiding.

---

> "Let's park that for now." — Parking Forever

**Why it works.** It sounds like ordinary agenda management, and it is, until you notice that nothing ever comes out of the car park. There is no mechanism by which a parked item returns unless somebody builds one.

**What to say back.** "Parked until when?" Then write it in the list of parked items with the date. The list is the mechanism. Without it, parking is a euphemism for discarding.

---

> "We're nearly out of time, so let's move on." — Clock Management

**Why it works.** The person controlling the agenda controls which items get the minutes, and the item that gets the last four minutes cannot be decided properly. Nothing needs to be refused; it simply does not fit.

**What to say back.** Move it, don't drop it. "Then let's start with it next time." Say it before the meeting closes, so it is in the record. An item that has been squeezed twice and named twice is much harder to squeeze a third time.

---

> "Just to give a bit of background before I answer." — The Preamble

**Why it works.** It consumes the available minutes with material nobody can object to, and by the time the background is finished the question has lost its urgency, or the meeting has.

**What to say back.** Ask for the order to be reversed. "Can I get the short answer first, and then the background?" Almost nobody refuses this, and it turns out the short answer is often thirty seconds long.

---

> "I'd need to run that past legal." — Borrowed Delay

**Why it works.** It transfers the decision to somebody not present, who has their own queue, and it is impossible to argue against without appearing reckless. The delay is real and the responsibility for it has been outsourced.

**What to say back.** "Of course. Shall I send them the question today?" Offering to do the sending is what separates a real check from a shelf. It is helpful, it is hard to refuse, and it puts a clock on it.

---

> "I think this needs a proper working group." — Referral to Committee

**Why it works.** It is the most respectable form of burial available. A working group takes weeks to form, months to report, and its report arrives after the decision has been made by events.

**What to say back.** "Good. Who chairs it, who's on it, and when does it report?" Three questions. A serious proposal answers them; a burial cannot, and asking makes that visible without any accusation.

---

> "Sorry, remind me of the question again?" — Forced Repetition

**Why it works.** Each repetition costs you time and credibility, since restating the same point three times makes you look fixated rather than persistent, while the person asking appears merely attentive.

**What to say back.** Read it, do not re-explain it. "It's the second line of the note I sent: were the figures signed off?" Reading from a document takes eight seconds and cannot be made to look obsessive.

---

> "We've been through all this before." — Claiming It Is Settled

**Why it works.** It asserts that the discussion has already happened and reached a conclusion, which makes reopening it a waste of everybody's time. Whether it actually concluded is not examined.

**What to say back.** "We discussed it. What did we decide?" If there is a decision, you have your answer and should accept it. If nobody can state it, then it was discussed and not settled, and that difference is the entire point.

---

> "Sorry, I'm going to have to jump." — The Hard Stop

**Why it works.** It ends the conversation unilaterally at a moment of the speaker's choosing, usually just after a difficult question, and it is unchallengeable because everybody has other meetings.

**What to say back.** Get one line in before they go. "No problem. I'll send the question by email so we can pick it up." Said quickly, it survives the departure. The item leaves the room in writing rather than evaporating with the call.
