# Chapter 11 — Praise and Thanks

Almost all praise is wasted, and it is wasted the same way every time. It is general. It is delivered to the person alone. And it arrives in a form that asks for a modest deflection, after which both parties forget it within the hour.

Three changes make it stick. None of them costs anything.

Be specific about the thing. *You were great in that meeting* is pleasant and evaporates. *The way you rephrased Sarah's objection so she could back down from it without losing face* is a sentence somebody remembers for a decade. It tells them that a thing they did on purpose, and thought nobody saw, was seen.

Say what it did. Praise describes the performance; gratitude describes the effect. The second is rarer and it lands harder. It establishes that you were changed, and that is not something the recipient can shrug off.

And where it is safe to do so, say it to somebody else. Praise delivered to a person's manager, or to the room, does something private praise cannot: it changes their standing, not just their mood.

The last thing worth saying is about timing. These letters are almost never urgent, which is why they do not get written. There is no deadline on a thank you. So it waits. Then it is late, then it is awkward, then it does not happen at all. The letters in this chapter are therefore all a little late, which is realistic, and not one of them apologises for it, which is deliberate, because an apology for lateness invites the reader to think about the delay instead of about the thing you are thanking them for.

---

> "Somebody did something significant for you and you said thanks in passing." — The real thank you

**The letter.** Dear Ruth,||I said thank you at the time and it was not adequate, so this is the proper version.||When I was falling apart in the autumn you did three things that I have thought about since. You moved the deadline without making me ask for it. You told me, in the corridor, that the work was still good, at a point when I had stopped believing that. And you never once mentioned it afterwards, which mattered more than either of the others.||I do not think you will remember any of this as anything much. It kept me in the job.||Thank you.||Murtaza

**What is doing the work.** Three named actions, one of which the recipient may not have thought of as a decision at all. Naming the omission, the not mentioning it afterwards, is the strongest move available in a thank you. Restraint is invisible by design. Nobody is ever thanked for it. The last line does the other job: it says what it caused.

---

> "A colleague did excellent work and their manager does not know." — Praise where it counts

**The letter.** Dear Adam,||I wanted to tell you about something Priya did last week, which I suspect she will not mention herself.||The Fenwick handover was in real trouble. She rebuilt the reconciliation from scratch over two days, found an error in our figures that had been there since November, and told the client about it before they found it themselves. That last part cost her a difficult conversation she could have avoided entirely.||I have worked with a lot of people who would have quietly fixed it and said nothing. She did the harder and better thing.||I thought you should know, from someone outside her team.||Murtaza

**What is doing the work.** Writing to the manager instead of to her, and saying why: *from someone outside her team* is what gives it evidential weight. It also singles out the choice that involved a cost, which is the thing managers most struggle to see and most value hearing about. This letter changes somebody's career and takes six minutes.

> "A stranger's work changed something for you." — The unsolicited letter

**The letter.** Dear Ms Adeyemi,||You will not know me. I read your book on the Windrush cases in 2023, when I was about to give up on a case of my own.||There is a passage near the end about how the evidence was reconstructed after the records were destroyed. I read it four times. It gave me an approach I had not thought of, and eighteen months later the case was decided in my client's favour.||I am not asking for anything. I have wanted to write this for two years and kept not doing it, and it seemed foolish to carry on not doing it.||Thank you for the book.||With respect,||Murtaza Raza

**What is doing the work.** Asking for nothing, and saying so. Writers, artists and academics get a great deal of post that turns out to be a request in the fourth paragraph. A letter that closes off that possibility can be enjoyed instead of assessed. The specific passage is what proves the reading was real, and the outcome is what makes the letter worth having received.

---

> "Somebody you are quietly jealous of has had good news." — Congratulating properly anyway

**The letter.** Sam,||Congratulations on the book. Genuinely, and I will admit that it took me a couple of days to be able to write this without it being through gritted teeth, which says everything about me and nothing about you.||You have wanted this since we were twenty-three and you did the work in a way I did not. I have watched you get up at five for four years and I have not, and the outcome is exactly correct.||Very well done. I mean it.||Ade

**What is doing the work.** Admitting the envy, with a light hand, and without making it the recipient's problem. The alternative, which is a warm letter with a detectable coolness underneath, is worse, because everybody can feel it and nobody can name it. Owning it disarms it and produces something more affectionate than an unmixed congratulation would have been.

---

> "You are writing a reference for somebody you rate." — The recommendation that helps

**The letter.** Dear Ms Chen,||I am glad to be asked about Priya Nair and I will be more useful if I am specific rather than general.||She led the Fenwick migration for eighteen months. Two things are worth knowing. She is the only person I have managed who consistently brought me problems while they were still cheap to fix, including problems she had caused, which is rarer than it sounds and is the single quality I would most look for.||And she is a good manager of people who are struggling. Two members of her team were in difficulty during that period and both are still with us and doing well, which was her work.||Her weakness, since you will want one: she is slow to delegate and takes on more than she should. She knows this and has been working on it.||I would hire her again without hesitation.||Murtaza Raza

**What is doing the work.** The named weakness. A reference with no weakness in it reads as a formality and gets discounted. One honest, minor, self-known limitation makes everything else credible. The two strengths are behavioural and specific instead of adjectival, which is the difference between a reference that is read and one that is skimmed.
