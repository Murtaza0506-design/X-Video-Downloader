# Chapter 1 — Condolence

The reason condolence letters go unwritten is not that people do not care. It is that they are afraid of getting it wrong, and the fear of getting it wrong produces the one outcome that is certainly wrong. Silence.

Nearly everybody who has been bereaved will tell you the same two things afterwards. The letters mattered more than they expected, including the clumsy ones. And the people who said nothing left a mark that lasted. They did not mean to. They were waiting until they could think of something adequate.

Nothing is adequate. That is the first thing to accept, and it is liberating rather than defeating. You are not going to write the sentence that helps, because no such sentence exists. What you are doing is much smaller and within reach: telling somebody that their loss has been noticed, by a person who is not obliged to notice.

There are four things that go in a condolence letter and one thing that does not. Say who has died, by name. Say something specific about them, or if you did not know them, about what the person has told you of them. Offer one concrete thing, not a general offer. And close without asking for anything.

The thing that does not go in is a question. A bereaved person is answering forty messages while arranging a funeral. A letter that needs an answer is one more job. Do not give them a job.

Send it late. Two months on, when everybody else has stopped, is when the letters are worth most.

---

> "Your close friend's mother has died. You knew her a little." — Condolence, where you knew them

**The letter.** Dear Priya,||I am so sorry about your mum. I keep thinking about that afternoon in the garden when she made us all move the table twice and then decided the first place had been right, and how much she laughed at herself about it. She was very funny, and she was very kind to me when I did not know anyone.||I am not going to pretend to know what this is like. I am around, and I will keep being around in a few months when it goes quiet, which I gather is the hard part.||I will drop some food off on Thursday and leave it on the step. No need to come to the door and no need to reply to this.||With love, Alex

**What is doing the work.** The specific memory. It says *I knew her, and here is proof*, which is the thing a bereaved person is most starved of once the funeral is over and everybody reverts to generalities. Second is the promise about later, because the support collapses at about week three and everyone knows it. Third is the food with the door shut in advance: an offer that requires nothing, not even the effort of being seen.

---

> "A colleague's father has died. You have met them twice and never met him." — Condolence, at a distance

**The letter.** Dear Sam,||I have just heard about your father and I wanted to say how sorry I am.||We do not know each other well, so I will not pretend to a closeness we do not have. I hope you are being looked after, and that work is the least of your concerns at the moment.||Everything on the Fenwick account is covered. Rob has it and there is nothing outstanding that needs you. Please do not think about any of it.||Take whatever time you need.||Sam, with sympathy, Murtaza

**What is doing the work.** The honesty about distance. Overclaiming intimacy is the commonest failure in professional condolence, and it reads as false at once. Naming the gap and writing anyway is warmer than pretending. The second half is the real gift: a colleague's dominant anxiety is that things are falling apart in their absence, and removing that is worth more than any sentiment.

---

> "Someone's baby died before it was born." — Condolence, for a loss others may not name

**The letter.** Dear Ellie,||I heard about the baby and I am so terribly sorry.||I know some people will not mention it, and I did not want to be one of them. She was real and she was yours and you were going to be her mother.||I am not going to say any of the things people say. I do not think there is anything that helps.||I love you. I am here whenever, for anything, including for talking about her, if you ever want to.||Ellie x

**What is doing the work.** Naming the child, and using the word *she*. Losses like this are handled by everyone around them as though they were medical events and not deaths, and the result is that the bereaved parent is left alone with a grief nobody has acknowledged as grief. The line about not saying the things people say is doing something too: it tells her you know the standard consolations are useless, which means you are safe to talk to.

---

> "An old friend you have not seen in years has lost their partner." — Condolence, after a long silence

**The letter.** Dear Tom,||We have not spoken in far too long and I am sorry that this is what brings me back. I heard about Jamie and I am so sorry.||I only met him twice, at your fortieth and at that awful barbecue, and both times I came away thinking you had done extraordinarily well.||I am not writing to reappear in your life or to make you manage an old friendship on top of everything else. I only wanted you to know that somebody who has known you since we were nineteen is thinking about you.||If you ever want a walk with someone who has no opinions about any of it, I am at the same number.||Tom, I am sorry. Alex

**What is doing the work.** Addressing the awkwardness of the gap in the first line, so it stops being the elephant. Then lifting the obligation to reciprocate, out loud, which is what stops people writing after a long silence: the fear of imposing a resumed friendship on somebody at their worst. Naming that fear and disclaiming it makes the letter a gift rather than a demand.

---

> "You have missed it. The funeral was six weeks ago." — Condolence, late

**The letter.** Dear Rachel,||This is late, and I am not going to explain why, because the reasons are not interesting and they are not really reasons.||I have been thinking about your dad and about you.||My guess, from the little I know, is that the phone has stopped ringing by now and the house is quieter than it was in November. If that is right, I hope somebody is still checking on you.||I am, from a distance, and with no expectation of a reply.||Rachel, with love, Sarah

**What is doing the work.** Refusing to explain the delay. Explanations turn a condolence letter into a letter about you, and the bereaved person then has to absolve you, which is another job. The other move is the guess about the phone having stopped: it demonstrates that you have thought about what this week is like for her, not what the week of the funeral was like, and that is exactly what late letters are for.
