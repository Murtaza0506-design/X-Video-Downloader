# Chapter 3 — Apologising Properly

Most apologies fail for one of three reasons. All three are about the writer.

The first is the conditional. *I am sorry if you were upset* makes the injury hypothetical and locates it in the other person's reaction rather than in your conduct. Everybody hears it. Nobody says so, because objecting to an apology is not a thing you can do.

The second is the explanation. You describe the pressure you were under, the misunderstanding, the thing you had going on that week. Every word of it may be true and all of it functions as a defence, which converts an apology into a case. The reader can either accept the case, in which case there was nothing to apologise for, or reject it, in which case they are being unreasonable about your difficult month.

The third is the request. *I hope we can put this behind us* asks for forgiveness in the same breath as offering the apology, which means the apology has a price. A real apology is given without terms and the other person decides what happens next.

So the shape that works is short and uncomfortable: name what you did, without softening it. Say what was wrong with it, in a way that shows you understand the damage and not just the rule. Say what you will do instead. And then stop, without asking for anything at all.

Under a hundred words beats over three hundred. Length reads as pleading.

---

> "You said something cruel during an argument and you meant it at the time." — The apology for something you meant

**The letter.** Rachel,||I said you were exactly like your mother, and I said it because I knew it would hurt you, and it did.||That was not a slip. I was losing and I reached for the thing I knew would land. It is the worst thing about how I argue and it is not the first time.||I am not asking you to be all right about it. You do not have to answer this or forgive it on any timetable.||I am seeing someone about the way I get in arguments. I started before this, which is not an excuse, but I want you to know it is not just a thing I am saying now.||I am sorry, Rachel. It was cruel and you did not deserve it.||Ade

**What is doing the work.** Admitting the intent. Most apologies for cruelty describe it as a loss of control, because *I did not mean it* is easier to say and easier to hear. It is also untrue, and the other person knows it, which is why the standard apology does not land. Owning the deliberateness is the only version that is believable, and the absence of any request at the end is what makes it a gift rather than a transaction.

> "You forgot something that mattered enormously to somebody." — The forgotten occasion

**The letter.** Dear Mum,||I forgot. There is no version of this where I did not just forget, and I am not going to dress it up as being busy, because I was not doing anything that mattered more than your seventieth.||I know you will say it is fine. It is not fine, and I would rather you were annoyed with me than that you pretended.||I have booked the Saturday after next, if you are free, and I will come to you. Not as a replacement, which it is not, but because I want to see you.||I am sorry. I love you.||Alex

**What is doing the work.** Pre-empting the forgiveness. *I know you will say it is fine* refuses the escape route the injured party offers to make things comfortable, and it is the difference between an apology and a small piece of theatre. Note also that the proposed date is not offered as making up for it, since claiming to repair something is a way of closing it early.

---

> "You let a colleague down and it cost them." — Professional apology, where there are consequences

**The letter.** Dear Sam,||I did not send the figures on Thursday and you went into the board meeting without them. That was my error and it made you look unprepared for something that was not your fault.||I have spoken to Ruth this morning and told her the delay was mine, in those words, so the record is straight.||The reason it happened is not interesting and I will not take up your time with it. What I have changed is that anything with a board deadline now sits in the shared calendar with a two-day buffer, so it does not depend on me remembering.||I am sorry, Sam. It should not have landed on you.||Murtaza

**What is doing the work.** Correcting the record with the third party, and saying so. In a professional apology the damage is reputational, so an apology that stays private repairs nothing. Second, refusing to explain: *the reason is not interesting* is a deliberate closing of the door that most people leave open, and it signals that you are not looking for absolution.

---

> "You said something confidently in public and it was wrong." — The correction

**The letter.** All,||In Tuesday's meeting I said the renewal rate was above ninety percent. It is seventy-six. I was working from last year's dashboard and I did not check it before I said it.||That number was load-bearing for the decision we took about hiring, and I would want us to revisit that in light of the real figure. I have put the correct dashboard in the shared drive.||Apologies for the noise, and for the confidence with which I said it.||Murtaza

**What is doing the work.** Correcting it to the same audience that heard the error, at the same volume, without being asked. The last line is the one that costs something: apologising not for the error but for the certainty. Being confidently wrong damages a room's ability to calibrate you, and naming it is what repairs it.

---

> "It has been years and you have never apologised." — The late apology

**The letter.** Dear Chris,||This is about something from 2011, so you may have to think for a moment to remember it, or you may not.||I told a room of people about your brother when you had asked me not to. I have thought about it a great many times since.||I am not writing because I want anything, and I do not expect a reply. I would rather you had the apology than that I kept being comfortable about not giving it. If you would prefer not to hear from me, this will be the last time.||I was twenty-four and that is an explanation rather than a defence.||I am sorry. It was a betrayal and I knew it at the time.||Ade

**What is doing the work.** Disclaiming the reply in the letter itself, and meaning it. Most late apologies are a request for relief dressed as remorse, and the recipient feels the difference at once. Offering to disappear is the proof of good faith: it says the apology is for them and not for your comfort, which is the only thing that makes a fifteen-year-old letter welcome rather than intrusive.
