# Chapter 12 — Letters With No Purpose

Every other chapter in this book is about a letter with a job. Something has gone wrong, or something is needed, or something has to be refused, and the writing is the instrument that handles it. This chapter is about the letters that have no job at all, which are the ones almost nobody writes and the ones people keep.

If you doubt that, look at what survives in other people's houses. Nobody keeps a delivery confirmation. The shoebox on top of the wardrobe holds the letter somebody wrote for no reason, and it has been read more times than its author would believe.

The reason these go unwritten is not laziness. It is that the ordinary rules of correspondence assume an occasion, and without one there is a faint embarrassment about writing at all. What am I doing, exactly. Why now. That hesitation is answered by the letters in this chapter, all of which are written on the same principle: the occasion is that you thought of them, and you are allowed to say so.

The commonest form of this failure is the letter that is delayed until the person can no longer read it. Nearly every funeral in the world contains somebody standing at the front saying true and loving things to a room, having never once said them to the person concerned. It is not that they did not mean it. It is that there was never a Tuesday that seemed like the right Tuesday.

There is one letter here that is never sent. It belongs in a book about what to say because a great deal of what needs saying is not addressed to anybody who should have to read it, and writing it down anyway does most of the work. The unsent letter is not a trick or an exercise. It is the correct form for a certain kind of thing.

Two practical notes. Write these by hand if you can, because a letter with no purpose that arrives by email reads as a message, and the physical version is the one that gets kept. And do not apologise for writing. An opening line that excuses the letter tells the reader it was an odd thing to do, which is exactly the belief this chapter is trying to break.

---

> "Nothing has happened. You simply thought about somebody on a Tuesday." — The letter with no occasion

**The letter.** Dear Nan,||No news, no reason. I was making tea in a mug you gave me about nine years ago, which is now chipped at the handle and is still the one I reach for first, and I thought I would write and tell you that.||Work is fine. The flat is cold. I have started running, badly, on Sunday mornings, and I keep thinking about you saying that the first mile is rent and the rest is yours.||That is genuinely all. You do not have to write back, though I would love it if you did, and I am not building up to asking you for anything.||I think about you a lot. I hope you are warm.||Love, Ruth

**What is doing the work.** The chipped mug. Anyone can write *thinking of you*, and it evaporates on contact, because it is a sentiment with no evidence attached. An object, a chip on the handle, a habit you have kept for nine years: these prove the thought was real and located somewhere in an actual afternoon. The second move is the disclaimer near the end, which sounds throwaway and is not. Older relatives in particular have learned that an unexpected letter can precede a request, and saying outright that nothing is coming lets the letter be what it is.

---

> "A teacher, a boss, an aunt. Somebody who changed the direction of your life and has never been told." — The letter you owe a living person

**The letter.** Dear Mr Okonjo,||You taught me GCSE physics between 2003 and 2005, in the lab at the end of the science corridor, and I doubt you remember me. I sat at the back and I was not a good student.||I am writing because I want to tell you something while it is still possible to tell you.||In the spring of the second year you kept me back after a lesson and said that I was lazy rather than stupid, and that the difference was entirely in my hands and nobody else's. You were not kind about it. It was the first time an adult had told me the truth about myself in a way I could not argue with.||I am an engineer. I have been one for fourteen years. I do not think that happens without that conversation, and I have never told you, which seemed increasingly ridiculous the longer it went on.||Thank you. I hope the corridor is still standing.||With real gratitude, Daniel Achebe

**What is doing the work.** The precision. Naming the years, the room, the season and the actual sentence spoken converts a vague tribute into a piece of evidence the reader can check against their own memory, and that is what makes it land rather than embarrass. Note also that the letter does not soften what was said. Reporting the moment as it was, including the fact that it was not kind, is what makes the gratitude credible. And the reason for writing is stated in one line without decoration: while it is still possible.

---

> "A friendship ended in nothing. No row, no falling out, just years." — The letter after a long drift

**The letter.** Dear Priya,||It has been about six years, I think, which is absurd.||Nothing happened, is the thing. We did not fall out. You moved, I had the twins, we each let three or four messages go unanswered at a busy time, and then enough time passed that writing started to feel like an event, and I kept not doing it.||I am writing now because I heard a song in the car that we both liked in a year I would rather not name out loud, and I sat in the car park until it finished.||I do not know what you want from this, and there may be nothing. If you would rather leave it where it is, that is genuinely all right and I will not chase.||But I miss you, and I did not want another six years to go by with nobody having said so.||Priya, I hope life has been good. Meera

**What is doing the work.** Naming the absence of a cause. A long silence between friends acquires, in both heads, an imagined reason, and the fear that a letter will excavate it is what keeps people quiet for decades. Saying outright that nothing happened dissolves the thing that was never there. The other necessary move is the exit offered near the end. A letter that reopens a friendship by force is a demand, and it is much easier to answer one that has already told you it will accept a no.

> "You want to leave something for your children to read when you are no longer here." — The letter for afterwards

**The letter.** To Sam and Isabel, to be read afterwards,||If you are reading this then the thing we all knew would happen eventually has happened, and I am sorry, and I am also fine about it, and both of those are true at once.||I want you to know three things.||The first is that I was happy. Not always, and not evenly, but on the whole and by a wide margin, and most of that was the two of you and your mother. If you find yourself worrying that I did not know that at the time, I did. I knew it constantly.||The second is that you are not obliged to grieve in any particular way, or for any particular length of time, or to agree with each other about how it is done. Look after each other through it and forgive whatever gets said in the first fortnight.||The third is about the house and the boat and all of it, and it is this: it is only money and objects, and I would rather you had a bad year and stayed close than divided everything correctly and did not speak again. If it comes to a choice, choose each other. That is an instruction.||Go and have a good life. It goes quickly and it is worth it.||All my love, always, Dad

**What is doing the work.** The permission. Almost everything a letter like this can do is release the reader from an obligation they will otherwise carry: to have made you happy enough, to grieve properly, to honour your memory by falling out over an estate. Listing three things and numbering them is not a stylistic tic either. A letter read in the worst week of somebody's life needs to be navigable, and structure survives distress in a way that flowing prose does not.

---

> "You are furious with somebody who cannot hear it, or who is dead, or with whom the conversation would cost more than it is worth." — The letter with no recipient

**The letter.** Dad,||I am not going to send this and you are not going to read it, and I have decided that does not make it pointless.||You were not there. Not in the large way people mean when they say it about fathers, but in a hundred small verifiable ways: the Tuesday nights, the two years of Saturdays, the concert with the empty seat that I could see from the stage. I have carried a version of you in my head that I have been arguing with for twenty years, and the arguing has been going about as well as it went in life.||Here is what I actually wanted. I wanted you to be interested. Not proud, particularly. Interested.||I am forty-one. You have been dead for three. I am not going to get it, and I have spent a considerable amount of my one life waiting to be given a thing by somebody who did not have it to give.||So this is me putting it down. Not forgiving you, because I am not sure I do, and I am tired of being told that is the required destination. Just putting it down.||Goodbye.||Nathan

**What is doing the work.** The absence of a reader. Every other letter in this book is shaped by what it will do to the person who opens it, and that constraint, useful everywhere else, is what prevents this from being said at all. Removing the recipient removes the negotiation. Notice too that it refuses the expected ending. An unsent letter that arrives on cue at forgiveness is performing, and the honest version stops at the true place, which here is setting the thing down. That is a real outcome and it is enough.
