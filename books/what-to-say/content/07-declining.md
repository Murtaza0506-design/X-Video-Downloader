# Chapter 7 — Declining

The reason saying no is hard is that most people believe a refusal needs to be earned, and that the way you earn it is with a reason good enough to survive scrutiny. So the letter fills up with explanation, and every additional reason is another surface for the other person to push against. *You could do it if you moved the Tuesday thing.* Now you are negotiating about your own diary with somebody who is not entitled to.

A refusal is not a proposal. It does not need agreement. Justification does not improve it. One reason, offered as information and not as a case, is the maximum. None at all is better still.

The second failure is the soft no. *Let me see if I can make it work.* *Maybe, let me check.* These are kind in the moment and unkind over a week, because they leave the other person planning around a maybe. The clean early no is the generous version, and people say so afterwards.

Three components. Decline in the first sentence, so nobody is reading in hope. Say one warm true thing, because a bare no reads as a judgement on the asker. And where you can, offer something smaller that you are willing to do. That last part is optional and does a great deal of work.

Then stop. The commonest error in a refusal is the third paragraph.

---

> "Your manager has asked you to take on something and you have no capacity." — Declining upwards

**The letter.** Ruth,||I do not think I can take the Halden review on, and I would rather say so now than deliver it badly in April.||Right now I have the migration through to March and the audit follow-up, both of which have dates on them. Adding a third means one of the three slips, and I would rather you chose which than found out later.||If Halden is the priority, I can do it and move the audit follow-up to May. If the audit date is fixed, then it needs somebody else.||Happy either way, but I do not think all three fit.||Murtaza

**What is doing the work.** Refusing without refusing: the no is real, and it is framed as a prioritisation question handed back to the person entitled to answer it. Managers respond badly to *I cannot* and well to *here is what it displaces*, because the second gives them the decision and the first takes it away.

---

> "You have been invited to a wedding you do not want to attend." — The declined invitation

**The letter.** Dear Priya and James,||Thank you so much for the invitation. I am so sorry, but I will not be able to come.||I would have loved to be there and I am genuinely sorry to miss it. I hope the day is everything you want it to be, and I would love to take you both out when you are back and hear about all of it.||The present is in the post.||With love and every good wish,||Ade

**What is doing the work.** No reason at all. A wedding decline with an explanation invites the couple to solve it, and any explanation short of a funeral will be weighed. The absence of one is read as *I cannot*, which is what you want, and the offer of the dinner afterwards is what stops the refusal reading as indifference. Note the answer arrives in the second sentence.

---

> "Somebody keeps asking you to help and you keep saying yes." — The repeat asker

**The letter.** Dear Sam,||I am going to have to stop doing the newsletter after this issue.||I have done it for three years and I have enjoyed a lot of it, but it has grown from an evening a month to most of a weekend, and it has quietly become the thing I do instead of the things I meant to do.||I will do the March issue and I will write up how it all works so the next person is not starting from nothing. After that I am out, and I am afraid I do mean out rather than occasionally helping.||Thank you for asking me in the first place. It was good of you.||Ade

**What is doing the work.** Closing the back door in advance. Somebody who has said yes for years is assumed to be available, so a refusal that leaves any opening will be tested. *I do mean out rather than occasionally helping* is uncomfortable to write and it is the sentence that makes the letter work. The handover offer is what keeps the relationship intact.

---

> "You have been asked to do professional work for free." — The free work request

**The letter.** Dear Nadia,||Thank you for thinking of me. I am afraid I cannot do it on an unpaid basis.||This is not a comment on the project, which I like, or on you. It is that design is what I do for a living rather than in addition to it, and I have had to make it a rule because the exceptions took over.||My rate for something this size would be around eighteen hundred. If that is out of reach, I would be glad to spend half an hour on the phone giving you my honest view on the brief, at no charge, which might save you more than the design would.||With very best wishes,||Murtaza

**What is doing the work.** Naming a price instead of only refusing. It converts *no* into *here are the terms*, which is a different conversation and one that sometimes converts. The half hour offered free is the crucial softener: it demonstrates goodwill, costs little, and makes it plain that the refusal is about the work rather than about them.

---

> "You do not want to, and you do not have a reason you want to give." — Declining without explaining

**The letter.** Chris,||Thanks for asking me, but I am going to say no to this one.||No dramatic reason, I just do not want to take it on at the moment.||Hope it goes well and do ask me another time.||Ade

**What is doing the work.** Forty words, and *no dramatic reason* is the whole letter. Refusals without stated reasons make people anxious that something is being concealed, and one short line closes that off without inventing anything. *Do ask me another time* is the piece that keeps it from sounding like a door closing, and it is only fair to include if it is true.
