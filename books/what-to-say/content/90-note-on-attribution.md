# A Note on Using These

A few things that did not fit in the chapters, and one warning.

## Every name in this book is invented

There are no real people here. Not one. The situations are composites of the ordinary kinds of trouble that arrive in an average life, and the names, dates, places, employers and account numbers were made up to fill them out. Any resemblance to a specific person's circumstances is a consequence of those circumstances being common, which is the point of the book.

## How to adapt one

Read the letter. Read the note underneath it. Then put the book down and write your own, from memory, in the voice you would use on the telephone to somebody you had not spoken to for a month. That sounds like extra work. It is the fastest route to something that sounds like you.

If you would sooner work straight from the text, change these things at minimum. The opening line, because it is the most recognisable part of any letter. Every concrete detail, since borrowed specifics are worse than none at all. A memory that is not yours will read as false to the one person who knows it did not happen. And the register, which in this book is British, a shade formal and a little dry. If you do not talk like that, do not write like that.

What you should keep is the order of the moves and the things that are absent. The absences are the hardest part to reproduce from instinct, because under pressure everybody adds.

## The letters that are meant to be difficult

Chapters seven, eight and ten contain letters that will not make the recipient happy, and they are not written to. A refusal that has been softened until it can be misread as a maybe has failed at the only thing it was for. If you find yourself adding a sentence to make one of those letters kinder, stop and check what it is doing. Kinder, or vaguer? The two are easy to confuse and only one of them is a mercy.

## On sending things by email

Most of these work by email, and several are better that way: anything involving a company, a deadline or a record. Four kinds are not, and are worth a stamp. Condolence. Thanks that matter. Anything intended to be kept. And any letter whose whole purpose is to show that you took some trouble, since an email costs nothing and shows it.

## The one warning

A letter is not a substitute for a conversation with somebody who is present and available. It is tempting to write instead of speaking, because writing can be edited and a person cannot, and there are relationships that have been emptied out, without a word, by one party handling everything difficult in prose. If the person is in the next room, go and talk to them. This book is for the moments when that is not possible, has already failed, or would put more on somebody than they can carry in real time.
