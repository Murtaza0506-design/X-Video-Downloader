# Chapter 2 — Illness, and Other People's Bad News

Bad news that is not a death has a particular problem attached to it, which is that nobody knows what the form is. A death has rituals: cards exist, there is a funeral, there is a moment when writing is expected. A diagnosis has none of that. A marriage ending has none of that. A redundancy has none of that. So the news travels. Everyone decides in private that it is not their place, and the person at the centre of it experiences a strange quiet. That quiet is the thing people describe years afterwards, more often than they describe the diagnosis or the solicitor's letter or the meeting in the small room with somebody from HR in it, and it is made up entirely of individually reasonable decisions not to intrude.

The other problem is optimism. The instinct on hearing that somebody is ill is to say something hopeful, and hope offered too soon reads as a request. *You'll beat this* asks the person to be brave on your behalf, because you would find their fear difficult. The commonest thing people with a serious diagnosis report wanting is somebody who does not need them to be positive about it.

So these letters do less than instinct suggests. They acknowledge the fact, avoid forecasting, and offer something specific. The specific offer matters more here than anywhere else in this book, because the ill and the newly unemployed are both drowning in vague ones. *Let me know if there is anything I can do* transfers the work of thinking to the person with the least capacity for it. It sounds generous. It is not.

Say what you will do, name a day, and then do it.

> "A friend has been diagnosed with something serious." — Writing to someone who is ill

**The letter.** Dear Nadia,||Ben told me yesterday. I am so sorry. What a frightening thing to be told.||I am not going to tell you that you will be fine, because I do not know that and you have probably had enough of it from people who also do not know it. Whatever this turns out to be, it is allowed to be as bad as it is.||Practical things: I am going to drop a bag of proper food round on Sunday morning, and I will do it every Sunday until you tell me to stop. If you would rather I did not, say so and I will not be offended, but the default is that I am coming.||I would love to see you when you are up to it. There is no rush and no obligation, and I will not read anything into a slow reply.||All my love, Kate

**What is doing the work.** Refusing the forecast. Saying *it is allowed to be as bad as it is* gives permission almost nobody offers. It is the thing ill people most often say they wish somebody had said. Then the offer with a default in it: not *would you like food* but *food is coming, tell me to stop*, which removes the burden of asking.

---

> "Somebody's marriage has ended and you are friends with both." — When you do not know whose side to take

**The letter.** Dear Chris,||I heard about you and Fran. I am sorry. Whatever the details, I know it will not have been easy to get here.||I want to be straight with you, because I would rather say it than have you wonder. I am not going to take a side, and I have said the same to her. That is not neutrality about how you are; I care about you a great deal. It just means I am not the person to discuss the whys with.||What I can be is somebody you get a curry with who does not mention it once, if that is useful.||I am about most Thursdays.||Chris, Ade

**What is doing the work.** Saying the awkward thing instead of hoping it will not come up, and saying it early. Friends of both parties go quiet because they cannot face this conversation, and the going quiet is experienced as a second loss. The other move is offering the specific thing a newly separated person is least likely to be offered: an evening where nobody mentions it.

> "A colleague has been made redundant." — After a redundancy

**The letter.** Dear Priya,||I only heard this morning and I am sorry. For what it is worth, everybody I have spoken to has said some version of the same thing, which is that it makes no sense and it is not a reflection of your work.||Three specific offers, all of which stand for as long as you want them.||I will write you a reference today, and I mean a real one, not a template. Say the word.||I know two people at Halden and one at the Trust, and I am happy to introduce you to any of them, or all three.||And if you would rather not think about work at all for a month, that is also completely reasonable and I will stop sending you things.||I am sorry, Priya. It is genuinely their loss.||Murtaza

**What is doing the work.** Three named offers instead of one vague one, each small enough to accept without embarrassment. The last of the three is the one that matters most and is almost never given: explicit permission to not job-hunt yet. Everybody else in her inbox is being helpful in a way that assumes urgency, and the effect is a person who cannot rest.

---

> "A friend's child is in hospital." — To a parent, mid-crisis

**The letter.** Ellie,||No need to read this now or ever.||I am not going to ask how he is, because you are answering that question thirty times a day and I do not want to be the thirty-first.||I have put twenty pounds of credit on the hospital car park app under your registration. There is also a bag with phone chargers, a blanket, decent coffee and paracetamol at the ward reception with your name on it.||I am going to text you one thing every day. It will not need a reply, it will just be something normal and stupid so your phone does something other than deliver bad news.||I love you both. Say nothing back.||Sarah

**What is doing the work.** Not asking. A parent in a hospital spends the whole day being asked for an update they do not have. The letter that does not ask is remembered for years. Everything else is logistics: car park credit, chargers, coffee, all things that are unbearable to arrange from inside a ward and trivial to arrange from outside one.

---

> "Somebody's parent has dementia and it is going to go on for years." — The illness with no end date

**The letter.** Dear Tom,||I have been thinking about you and your mum.||I know this is not the kind of news that comes with a moment. There is no day when people send cards, and I imagine the hardest part is that it is a loss that keeps happening rather than one that happened.||I am not going to disappear after the initial flurry. I have put a reminder in my phone to check on you on the first of every month, which is either a nice thing or a slightly clinical thing depending on your mood, and I am telling you because I want you to know it is deliberate.||No obligation to answer any of them.||Love, Ade

**What is doing the work.** Naming the shape of the illness: a loss that keeps happening rather than one that happened. People with degenerative diagnoses in the family say the isolating part is that there is no event, so nobody knows when to be kind. Admitting the reminder in the phone sounds unromantic and lands as the opposite, because it is a promise with a mechanism behind it.
