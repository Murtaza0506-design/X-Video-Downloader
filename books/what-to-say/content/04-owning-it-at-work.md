# Chapter 4 — Owning It at Work

The instinct with a work mistake is to wait. Not out of dishonesty. There is a version of the future in which the problem solves itself and nobody ever has to know, and that version is always available and always tempting.

It is worth being clear-eyed about the arithmetic. A problem disclosed early is a problem the organisation can still do something about, and disclosing it makes you a person with judgement. The same problem discovered later is a problem plus a concealment, and the concealment is what people remember. The gap between those two outcomes is enormous. It is a function of how long you waited.

The letters here share one structural feature, which is that the bad news goes in the first sentence. Not the context, not the background, not the mitigations. The fact, before anything else. Anybody who has ever received one of these will tell you that the paragraph of preamble before the actual news is unbearable, because the reader knows something is coming and is now waiting for it while being told about the weather.

Then, in order: what it means, what you have already done, what you need from them. Four short paragraphs. And no apology longer than a sentence, because at work an extended apology reads as a request to be reassured, and the person receiving it has a problem to manage.

---

> "You are going to miss a deadline and it is still three weeks away." — Bad news, early

**The letter.** Ruth,||The migration is not going to be ready for the fifteenth. My honest estimate is the twenty-ninth.||I am telling you now rather than at the end because there is still time to do something with it. If the fifteenth is immovable, we could ship the read-only version on time and follow with the rest, which I think is the better of the bad options.||What went wrong: I scoped the data cleaning at four days and it is nearer twelve. That is an estimating failure and it is mine.||I am free any time today to talk it through.||Murtaza

**What is doing the work.** Telling them three weeks out, not three days. The value of the letter is in its timing, and the second paragraph makes that explicit by offering something that is only possible because there is still runway. Note that the failure is named in one line and not defended, which leaves the rest of the message for the part your manager needs. Options.

> "You have made an expensive mistake and nobody has noticed yet." — Escalating your own error

**The letter.** Ruth,||I have made an error that has cost us somewhere around eleven thousand pounds. Nobody outside my team knows yet and I wanted you to hear it from me first.||I approved the second print run without checking the amended artwork, so four thousand units carry the old address. They are in the warehouse.||What I have done already: stopped the third run, spoken to the printer about a reprint at cost, which they have agreed to, and had a sticker solution priced at nine hundred pounds in case we want to salvage the stock.||What I need from you: a decision on reprint versus stickers, and whether you want to tell Adam or you would rather I did.||I am sorry. It was a check I should have done and did not.||Murtaza

**What is doing the work.** The number first. Before any explanation. Managers report that the delay in getting to the figure is the part that erodes trust, because it feels like being managed. The section that changes the letter's reception is *what I have done already*: it converts you from the source of a problem into the person handling it, and it is why this letter survives an eleven thousand pound mistake.

> "A client's project is late and it is your fault." — The client apology

**The letter.** Dear Sarah,||The site will not be live on the third. It will be live on the seventeenth, and I am confident about that date in a way I was evidently not entitled to be about the last one.||This is our failure, not a supplier issue and not a scope issue. We under-resourced the build.||What we are doing: two additional developers started on it yesterday, and I am personally reviewing progress against the plan every Friday, which I will send you whether or not there is anything to report.||What I am not going to do is ask you to be understanding. You have a launch to run and this has made it harder.||We are not invoicing the March retainer.||I am sorry, Sarah. Ring me any time.||Murtaza

**What is doing the work.** Refusing to ask for understanding, which is what almost every client apology does and what clients find most irritating about them. Then a cost borne in public: waiving the retainer converts the apology from words into a number, which is the currency the relationship runs on. The offer of the weekly update *whether or not there is anything to report* addresses the real fear, which is silence.

> "You reacted badly to being passed over and people saw." — Apologising for your reaction

**The letter.** Dear Priya,||I was short with you in the stand-up on Wednesday and I have been off with you since, and it is because you got the lead and I wanted it.||That is not your fault and it is not a reflection of anything you did. You were the right choice and I would have made the same one.||I am not writing this to be told it was fine, because it was not, and I am aware that an apology that arrives with an explanation attached can read as a request for sympathy. It is not.||Congratulations, properly this time. I would like to work on it with you if you will still have me.||Murtaza

**What is doing the work.** Naming the actual cause. Everybody in the room already knows why you were short, so an apology that omits the reason is incomplete, and reads as an insult. Saying it out loud is the thing that costs you and the thing that repairs it. The pre-emption of *I am not fishing for reassurance* is doing real work too, because that is exactly what this letter would otherwise look like.

---

> "You have taken on something you cannot do." — Asking to be taken off it

**The letter.** Ruth,||I need to come off the compliance workstream. I am not able to do it well and I have been slow to say so.||This is not workload. I do not have the regulatory background it needs, and I have spent six weeks learning things that somebody with the right experience would already know. The work is about three weeks behind where it should be and that gap will grow.||I should have said this in week two. I did not because I thought I could close it, and by the time it was clear I could not, admitting it had got harder.||I am happy to hand over properly, and I would like to stay on the parts that are within what I can do.||Murtaza

**What is doing the work.** Distinguishing incapacity from overload, which almost nobody does, because *I am too busy* is the permitted version and *I cannot do this* is not. The admission about the delay is the second half of the honesty: naming that the difficulty of confessing grew with time is the sentence that makes the letter credible rather than merely awkward.
