# Chapter 6 — Chasing

Nearly every unanswered message is unanswered for a boring reason. It arrived on a bad morning. It needed a decision the person had not made, or twenty minutes they did not have. Then it moved down the screen and stopped existing. Almost none of it is about you. The sender, meanwhile, has spent four days building a theory in which the silence is a verdict on the work, or on the relationship, or on one sentence in the third paragraph that read differently than it was meant to.

Knowing that changes how the chase should be written, because the standard chase is built on the opposite assumption. It is apologetic, faintly wounded, and it re-explains the request at length. All three make it harder to answer. The apology says you think you are a nuisance. The woundedness turns a reply into an emotional task. And the re-explanation means four hundred words stand between the reader and the thing you want.

The chase that works is shorter than the original, contains the specific thing needed, and makes the reply as close to a single word as possible. If you can convert your request into a yes or no question, do it, even at the cost of some precision. A question that can be answered in three seconds from a phone at a bus stop will be answered.

The other reliable device is the deadline that is not a threat: *if I do not hear by Friday I will assume X and proceed*. It gives the reader permission to not reply. That sounds like the opposite of what you want, and it is the thing that produces a reply.

Three chases, then change medium. A fourth email will not work when the third did not.

---

> "You have sent one email and heard nothing for a week." — The first chase

**The letter.** Sam,||Bumping this in case it got buried.||One question: are you happy for us to go ahead with the March date? Yes or no is enough, and I can work out the rest.||Murtaza

**What is doing the work.** Brevity, and the conversion of the request into a binary. The original email probably asked for several things; the chase asks for the one that unblocks you. *In case it got buried* hands the reader a face-saving explanation. That matters. A reader who feels caught out delays again instead of replying.

---

> "Somebody has gone silent in the middle of a piece of work." — The disappearance

**The letter.** Dear Priya,||I have not heard from you since the twelfth and I want to check in without making it awkward.||If something has come up, or the project has changed shape at your end, that is completely fine and I would rather know than guess. If you would like to hand it back, that is also a real option and I will not be difficult about it.||If I do not hear by Friday I will assume you would rather step away, and I will pick the work up from here with no hard feelings at all.||Genuinely, either way.||Murtaza

**What is doing the work.** Offering the exit. Silence in the middle of a project is nearly always somebody who has fallen behind and cannot face saying so. Every further day makes the confession harder. Naming the escape route takes the shame out of it. It then produces the opposite of what it offers. People given permission to quit reply and carry on.

---

> "A decision you need keeps slipping and you are getting impatient." — Escalating without escalating

**The letter.** Ruth,||The supplier decision has moved three times, most recently to the twenty-first. I am not chasing to hurry you; I know it is not straightforward.||What I do need is to understand what it is waiting on, because two things downstream depend on it and I would rather re-plan around a real date than keep holding.||If the honest answer is that it will not be resolved this quarter, tell me that and I will build the plan on the current supplier instead.||No rush on this reply, but a rough steer would help a lot.||Murtaza

**What is doing the work.** Asking what it is waiting on, not when it will be done. A date can be given without thought and slip again. A blocker has to be described. Describing it produces either the decision or the real timeline. Offering to plan around the failure is the pressure, and it is applied without anybody having to be criticised.

---

> "A friend has stopped replying and you do not know why." — The personal chase

**The letter.** Tom,||I have sent two things and not heard back, which is unlike you, so I am doing the thing where I ask directly rather than working it out on my own and getting it wrong.||Is everything all right? And separately, have I done something? I would genuinely rather know than wonder, and if I have, I would like the chance to sort it.||If you are just under water, ignore this entirely and I will assume that.||Ade

**What is doing the work.** Asking both questions and separating them. The two possibilities, *something is wrong with them* and *something is wrong between us*, need different letters. Most people write one and hope it covers both. Asking outright is uncomfortable for about a day and prevents the slow silent drift that ends friendships without anybody deciding to.

---

> "Somebody owes you an answer about something that matters and keeps deflecting." — Asking for a straight answer

**The letter.** Dear Chris,||I have raised the question about the flat three times now and each time we have ended up talking about something else. I do not think that is deliberate, but I need to ask it plainly.||Are you intending to move out in April, or not? I am not asking you to justify the answer and I am not going to argue with it. I need to know because I have to give notice by the fourteenth either way.||If you do not know yet, tell me that and tell me when you will.||Ade

**What is doing the work.** Naming the pattern without accusing anybody of it, and then reducing the question to a single unavoidable sentence. The promise not to argue is what makes it answerable. Repeated deflection is somebody avoiding a row. Remove the row in advance and the reason to deflect goes with it.
