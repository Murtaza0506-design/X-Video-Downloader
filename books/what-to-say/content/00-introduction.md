# Introduction

This is a book of sixty letters for the moments nobody teaches you how to write. A colleague's father has died. You owe somebody an apology you have been avoiding for a month. A friend has cancer. You have to ask for money, or refuse to lend it, or tell a landlord that the boiler has been broken since October. You want to end something without being cruel about it.

Each entry has four parts. The situation, in one line. The form of letter it needs. The letter itself, written out in full. And a short note on what is actually doing the work, because a letter you copy without understanding will fail the moment your circumstances differ from the example by an inch.

**These are not templates.** Or rather, they are, and using them as templates is the one way to get less out of the book than it contains. The names are invented and so are the details. Send one unaltered and it will read like a form letter, which is worse than a clumsy letter in your own words. What is transferable is the shape: what goes first, what is left out, where the offer belongs, and which sentence is carrying the weight. That is what the note after each letter is for.

**On the notes.** They are the argument of the book. Nearly all of them come back to the same handful of principles, and you will start to notice them: be specific instead of warm, because specificity is how warmth is proved. Remove the recipient's obligation to reply, above all when they are suffering. Do not explain yourself at length, because explanation is a request for absolution wearing a disguise. Say the thing in the first three lines. Offer something concrete rather than something general.

**On length.** Most of these are shorter than the letter you would have written unaided, and that is deliberate. The instinct under pressure is to add: more sympathy, more context, more mitigation. Almost every letter in this book got better when a paragraph came out of it, and the paragraph that came out was nearly always the one about the writer.

**On apologies in particular.** Chapters three and four take a harder line than is fashionable. An apology containing an explanation is not an apology, it is a defence with an apology attached, and the recipient can always tell. If you take one thing from this book, take that.

**What a letter cannot do.** It cannot make a bereavement smaller, get you forgiven by somebody who has decided not to forgive you, or make a decision land softly when the decision itself is hard. Several letters here are written in full knowledge that they will not be well received, and their job is only to be honest, clear and unpaddable. A letter that succeeds is not always a letter that gets you what you want.

**Send it.** The common failure is not the badly written letter. It is the letter that was never written, because the moment passed, or the right words did not come, or it seemed too late. It is rarely too late. Somebody who is grieving in the third month, or who has not been thanked in twenty years, is not waiting for the perfect version.

Open it at whichever chapter matches the thing you are avoiding.
