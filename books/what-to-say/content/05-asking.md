# Chapter 5 — Asking

Most people are far more willing to help than most people expect. The research on this is consistent enough to state as a fact. Askers underestimate how often they will get a yes, by a wide margin, in every setting anybody has tested.

Which means the main obstacle to asking is not the risk of refusal. It is the discomfort of the twenty seconds before you send it. That discomfort produces a style of letter you will recognise: long, apologetic, hedged, and so anxious to give the reader an escape route that it suggests, by accident, that the whole thing is not worth doing.

The letters in this chapter are short. Not as a preference. As the mechanism. A long request signals that the asker knows they are imposing, which invites the reader to agree. A short one signals that the ask is ordinary, and most of them are.

Three things go in a good ask. The specific thing you want, stated as a request rather than hinted at. Why you are asking this person rather than anybody else, which is what makes it flattering instead of mass-produced. And an easy no, offered once, briefly, at the end. Once, not three times: an escape route repeated becomes a suggestion.

Do not apologise for asking in the first line. It sets the reader up to feel imposed upon before they know what for.

---

> "You want a pay rise." — Asking for money from your employer

**The letter.** Ruth,||I would like to ask for a rise to sixty-two thousand, and I would like to talk about it properly rather than in a corridor. Could we book half an hour in the next fortnight?||The short version of the case: I took on the compliance reporting in March when Dev left, which was not in my role, and it has stayed with me. The audit went through with no findings for the first time in four years. And I am currently the only person who can run the reconciliation, which is a risk for you as well as an argument for me.||I have looked at what the role pays elsewhere and sixty-two is at the middle rather than the top of it.||Happy to send anything that would be useful ahead of the conversation.||Murtaza

**What is doing the work.** A number in the first sentence. Requests that circle the figure force the manager to guess, and they will guess low. Then the case in three specific facts, not adjectives, including one that is framed as the employer's problem rather than the employee's merit. The phrase *at the middle rather than the top* is modest on purpose and makes the number harder to argue with.

> "You want a favour from somebody senior you barely know." — Asking a stranger

**The letter.** Dear Professor Adeyemi,||We met briefly at the Warwick symposium in June; I asked the question about the longitudinal data.||I am writing to ask whether you would be willing to spend twenty minutes on a call looking at my sampling design. I am at the stage where the errors are still cheap to fix, and you are the person whose 2019 paper made me realise the approach I had was wrong.||If the answer is no or not now, that is completely fine and I will not follow up.||With thanks either way,||Murtaza Raza

**What is doing the work.** A bounded ask: twenty minutes, one specific question, not *pick your brain*. Senior people decline vague requests because they cannot see the bottom of them. The reference to the specific paper is what separates this from a mass email. *I will not follow up* removes the main reason busy people ignore requests, which is the fear of an obligation that never ends.

> "You want an introduction." — Asking someone to spend their credibility

**The letter.** Dear Sam,||Would you be willing to introduce me to Nadia Rahim at Halden? No obligation at all, and I would rather you said no than felt you had to.||I am asking because you have worked with her and I have not, and a cold approach from me will go in the same folder as everybody else's.||To make it easy, here is something you could forward, if you would rather not write anything yourself:||"Nadia, this is Murtaza, who ran the reconciliation project I mentioned last year. He is looking at the same problem you described at the conference and I thought you two should talk. Murtaza, Nadia."||Genuinely fine if not.||Murtaza

**What is doing the work.** Writing the introduction for them. Introduction requests go unanswered for one reason. Composing the email is a small task that never reaches the top of anybody's list. Removing that work is the difference between a request that happens and one that does not. The easy no matters more here than elsewhere, because an introduction spends the other person's reputation.

> "You are struggling and need to ask for help." — Asking for something you find humiliating to need

**The letter.** Sarah,||I need some help and I have been putting off asking, which is why this is a message rather than a conversation.||Things have been bad since January. I am managing work and almost nothing else, and the flat has got away from me in a way I am embarrassed about.||What would actually help is if you came over on a Saturday and we did the kitchen and the paperwork pile together. Not you doing it. Together, with the radio on, so that it is four hours rather than a thing I keep not starting.||I am also going to the doctor on the eleventh.||I am sorry to land this on you. If it is too much, say so and I will not be hurt.||Ade

**What is doing the work.** A concrete task instead of a general appeal. *I am struggling* leaves the friend wanting to help and with no idea how, and the commonest outcome is a supportive message and nothing else. Naming a specific afternoon and a specific job converts sympathy into action. The line about the doctor signals, without saying it, that the friend is not being asked to be the whole solution.

> "You want someone to be a reference." — Asking for a name

**The letter.** Dear Ruth,||Would you be willing to act as a reference for me? The role is Head of Operations at the Trust and they are likely to ring rather than write.||I am asking you rather than Adam because the job is mostly about the reporting side, which you saw more of.||Two things that would help, if you are happy to: they are interested in whether I can run a team through a change programme, and the Fenwick migration is the best example of that. And it would be reasonable for them to ask about the compliance workstream I came off, which I would rather you were straight about than diplomatic; I have been open with them about it.||No difficulty at all if you would rather not.||Murtaza

**What is doing the work.** Giving the referee their brief. A reference who has not been told what the job needs will default to generalities, which are worthless. The unusual move is inviting them to be honest about the failure: it tells the referee they will not be put in the position of concealing something, which is the thing that makes people hesitate to say yes.
