# One

## Wellington Street

The building had been called Waterloo House. Someone at Harrogate had drawn a line through that on the sector map and written 6/WEL/04 above it in a hand that sloped backwards, because the Executive had determined in its second month that names were a vector for sentiment and sentiment was slowing the work. Cowie had liked the name. It was on a brass plate by the revolving door, and the plate had been polished by a man who came on Tuesdays, and Cowie thought about that man more than he thought about most of the people he had actually known.

They cleared from the top. This was doctrine, arrived at expensively. You went up the stairwell in the dark with the door of each landing chocked behind you, and you started at the roof and came down through the building like water finding its way, and anything that was still standing had nowhere to go but down into the lobby where Sandhu waited with the shotgun and a folding stool she had taken from a pub on Boar Lane and would not be parted from.

Floor six was open plan. It had been open plan in the way that meant somebody had paid a consultancy to remove the walls and then paid a second consultancy to install a quantity of felt.

"Grid it," said Sandhu, on the radio, from her stool, four floors below.

"Gridding it," said Hutch, who was nineteen and said everything twice, once to the radio and once to himself, as though the first version had been for the record and the second was the true account.

The desks ran in banks of six. Every one of them had been left mid-sentence. That was the thing about the office blocks that the residential streets did not have, because a house was where people had gone to make their decisions and an office was where they had simply stopped: a cardigan on the back of a chair, a Pret bag gone to a brown paste inside its own carbon, a screen with a spreadsheet still open on it under two years of dust, the cursor blinking in cell F41 for eleven months until the building's power finally went and it stopped blinking and became simply a mark. Somebody had been about to type a number. Cowie had a private theory, which he had never shared with the unit because the unit would have made a great deal of it, that the number had been fine. That whatever went in F41 was a perfectly ordinary number and that the world had ended in the middle of an ordinary Thursday afternoon that was going quite well.

At the far end of the floor there was a banner. It said HAPPY RETIREMENT SUE in letters that had been individually gold and had, in the damp, become individually green.

Cowie stood in front of it for longer than the protocol allowed.

---

Before the Handover he had been an assistant category manager. He had told people this at parties and watched the specific way their faces worked, the way they went politely still while they decided whether to ask, and he had learned to get in first and say crisps, I do crisps, which bought a laugh and closed the subject and meant he never had to explain the actual work, which he had loved.

The actual work was this. Three times a year the chain reviewed the range. A range review was a document of some two hundred pages in which the crisp aisle of every store in the north of England was reasoned about from first principles, and the reasoning was genuinely difficult, because it turned out that people did not buy crisps the way they said they bought crisps, and the gap between those two things was where Cowie had spent his twenties. He had sat in a meeting room named after a river, they were all named after rivers, Wharfe and Aire and Calder and one called Nidd that nobody wanted, and he had argued for forty minutes about eleven centimetres of shelf. He had won. The eleven centimetres had gone to a Yorkshire crisp company that made a hand-cooked crisp in a bag the colour of a hedge, and the company had put on two people because of it, and one of those two people had sent Cowie an email that said, in full, *Cheers mate, my mum's dead chuffed.*

He thought about that email a great deal now. He had thought about it while lying under a Transit on the A65 for eleven hours. It had seemed to him, then and since, to be the single most useful thing he had ever done, and he had done it by moving a line on a diagram, and there was no version of the present world in which that kind of usefulness was available to anybody.

The banner said HAPPY RETIREMENT SUE.

Somewhere in this building, on a floor he had already cleared or a floor he had not yet reached, there had been a Sue who had done thirty years of something and been given a card. Cowie had signed a hundred of those cards. He had written *All the best* in a hundred of them, in blue, because he had believed for reasons he could no longer reconstruct that black was cold, and every one of the hundred had gone into a drawer in somebody's house and was in that drawer now, and the houses were still standing, most of them, out along the roads where the Executive's writ did not run.

---

"Cowie."

Hutch had stopped at the end of bank four with his hand up flat, the signal, and he was not saying it twice.

It was in the accessible toilet. They were nearly always in a small room. Harrogate had produced a paper about this, sixteen pages with a foreword, and had concluded that the behaviour was consistent with a terminal impulse toward enclosure, which was the Executive all over: sixteen pages to say that when people got frightened they went and shut a door.

She was standing between the basin and the wall. Non-viable, in the language of the sector maps. Nivs, in the language of everybody who had ever actually stood in a room with one. She had been perhaps fifty and was wearing a lanyard, and the lanyard had her photograph on it, and in the photograph she was laughing at somebody just off to the left of the camera.

Cowie did it. There was a way of doing it that the training films called controlled and that the unit called getting on with it, and it took under two seconds, and the sound was the sound of a bag of shopping being set down hard on a kitchen floor.

Then he did the other part, which was the part the training films did not have footage of, because there was no footage of it anywhere and there never would be. He turned the lanyard over and read the name and wrote it in the book.

*S. Ellerby.*

He looked at it for a moment. Then he wrote, in the column headed OTHER, the word *Sue*, and closed the book, because the column headed OTHER was the only place in the entire architecture of the National Recovery Executive where a person could put down a thing they had noticed.

---

The forms went to Harrogate in a bag on a Thursday. Nobody in the unit had ever met anybody who had read one, and there was a standing theory, which Sandhu held with real conviction and Hutch held the way he held everything, at a volume, that the forms went into a room and the room was full and there was a second room being prepared.

Harrogate was the seat of government, which was funny, and had been funny since the first week, and had gone on being funny for two years in a way that nothing else had managed. It was a conference town. The Executive sat in a spa hotel with a ballroom, and its communications came out on a headed paper that still carried, in the bottom left corner, in a grey that had been chosen by somebody, the words *A Yorkshire Welcome*.

The tabards were sponsored. This was the detail that visitors could never get past, in the days when there had been visitors. Somebody at Harrogate, in the first autumn, at a point when the actual clearance of the actual cities had not yet begun, had signed a memorandum of understanding with the Pennine Mutual Building Society, and so the high-visibility tabard worn by every sweeper in every cordon in the north of England carried, across the shoulder blades, in a font the society had commissioned in 2011 to convey approachability, the words *PENNINE MUTUAL: HERE FOR THE LONG RUN.*

You stopped seeing it. That was the thing about it, and it was the thing about all of it. Hutch, who had been seventeen at the Handover and had therefore done his growing up inside the new arrangements, did not think the tabards were funny at all, and could not be made to think so, and had once asked with genuine puzzlement why a building society would not sponsor the sweep, and Cowie had opened his mouth to explain and had discovered that he could not get to the bottom of it either.

They were all being adjusted. That was the term. Post-Event Adjustment Disorder, the Executive's clinicians had it, and the Executive's clinicians had been very clear that it was universal, that it would be strange and in fact concerning not to have it, and so the word had come loose from its moorings the way words did now and had turned into an adjective and then into a joke and then into the ordinary way of saying that somebody was having a bad afternoon. *He's adjusting.* Sandhu said it about Hutch. Hutch said it about Cowie. Cowie said it about nobody, because he had noticed that he only ever said it about people he had decided to stop looking at properly.

---

He had been in the flat on Kirkstall Road when it went. That was the phrase people used, *when it went*, and the Executive's phrase was the Handover, and there had been eleven days between the first genuinely alarming Tuesday and the night the electricity stopped, which was a fact that Cowie could not make anybody believe afterwards. Eleven days. People remembered it as an afternoon. They remembered a single afternoon in which everything had turned over at once, and it had not been like that at all, it had been eleven days of things being nearly fine, of the shops being open with gaps in them, of a man on the radio at six saying that the advice remained unchanged, of Cowie going in to work on the Thursday because nobody had told him not to and sitting in Nidd with two other people who had also not been told, the three of them going through a range review for a spring that was not going to happen, and at half past eleven somebody's phone had gone and that had been the end of the meeting and, as it turned out, of the meetings.

He had walked home. It had been a nice day.

What he could not say, and had never said, not to the clinicians at the reception centre with their laminated cards, not to Sandhu on the long nights on the roof at Quarry Hill, was that some part of him had been relieved. Not glad. He was clear with himself about the distinction and he held to it. But there had been a moment on that walk home, going past the retail park with the sun on it and the traffic already wrong, when a thought had arrived fully formed and unbidden and said: *well, that's the range review off.*

He had been thirty-one. He had thought he had another forty years of Nidd.

---

"Six is clear," said Hutch, to the radio, and then, to himself, to the felt, to the banner, to the eleven centimetres of shelf in a shop on the other side of a cordon that was not going to be reopened in his lifetime, "six is clear."

They chocked the door and went down to seven, which was the wrong direction, but the stairwell at the north end had gone in the winter and the only way to seven was to come at it from beneath, and so the building was cleared out of order, which was not doctrine, and would have to be written up, and would go in the bag on Thursday, and would be read by nobody in a room that was filling up.

Cowie went last. At the landing he stopped and put his head back through the door and looked down the length of floor six, at the banks of desks and the felt and the brown paste and the screen with its unfinished number, and at the green letters at the far end, and he thought that the honest thing, the thing that Harrogate would never write on any form and that he would never put in the column marked OTHER, was that Sue had made it to her retirement.

She had been in the building for thirty years and she had got the card and the banner and the eleven centimetres of everybody's attention, and then she had had two years of standing quietly in a small room with the door shut, which was, he understood, on the balance of the evidence, and by the standards currently obtaining, quite a long retirement.
