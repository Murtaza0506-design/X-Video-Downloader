# Chapter 18 — Money

Most people can tell you their salary. Very few can tell you, without looking, what leaves their account each month before they have chosen anything, and fewer still could say what figure would be enough. The subject that governs more of the week than any other is the one we examine least, and the avoidance is not laziness. It is that looking properly involves finding something out.

Money is also the last thing left that people will not discuss. Sex, illness, therapy and divorce have all come out from under the table. What you earn has not, and neither has what you owe, which means almost everyone is estimating their position against other people's spending with no access to the accounts behind it. The neighbour's car is visible. The loan on it is not.

This chapter is about the ordinary dread and not the arithmetic. There are better books for the arithmetic and they all say the same four things. What they cannot say is why a person with enough still lies awake, why a windfall changes the mood for six weeks, why the sum you needed at thirty has quietly become the sum you need every year, or why an unopened envelope on the hall table can dominate a Sunday.

One warning, up front and repeated at the end. Everything here assumes your needs are met. Advice about wanting less, spending better and worrying about it more calmly is written for people with margin. If there is no margin, none of it applies, and the shortage is a structural problem and not a personal failing.

---

> "Annual income twenty pounds, annual expenditure nineteen nineteen and six, result happiness. Annual income twenty pounds, annual expenditure twenty pounds ought and six, result misery." — Charles Dickens

**What it means.** Micawber's law, delivered by a man who never once obeyed it. The margin is the whole thing. Sixpence either side of the line changes the character of a life, and the size of the income barely features in the calculation.

**How to use it.** Find your own sixpence. Not a budget. One number: what comes in, what goes out, monthly. Most people have never sat down with both figures at once and are carrying a vague dread instead of a fact. The fact is almost always less frightening than the dread, and unlike the dread it can be worked on.

---

> "The love of money is the root of all evil." — 1 Timothy 6:10

**What it means.** Note the exact words, because the popular version drops the important one. Not money. The love of it. The line is about a relationship, not a substance, and it makes no claim that being paid is a moral problem.

**How to use it.** Test the relationship, not the balance. Do you check the account when nothing has changed? Does a good month improve your mood more than it should? Is there a number you keep moving? None of that is about how much you have. All of it is about how much of you the subject currently occupies.

---

> "Riches are a good handmaid, but the worst mistress." — Francis Bacon

**What it means.** Bacon is describing who is serving whom. Money used as an instrument is excellent: it buys time, safety and options. Money as the objective reorganises everything else around itself, including the decisions that were meant to be about your life.

**How to use it.** Look at your last three significant decisions. Job, house, how you spent a year. If money was a factor in all three, that is sensible. If it was the deciding factor in all three, notice that a servant has been promoted, and ask what the promotion has cost you in the categories that do not appear on a statement.

---

> "Debt is the worst poverty." — Thomas Fuller

**What it means.** Being short is a condition. Being in debt is a condition with a clock attached, and the clock is what does the damage. It converts every future month into a month that is already spoken for, which is a different kind of poverty from having little.

**How to use it.** Rank debts by what they do to your head, not only by their interest rate. The small one you are ashamed of may be costing you more in sleep than the large one you have made peace with. Clear it early even if the spreadsheet disagrees, because the spreadsheet is not the thing keeping you awake.

---

> "Beware of little expenses; a small leak will sink a great ship." — Benjamin Franklin

**What it means.** Nobody sinks from one decision. They sink from eleven subscriptions, a habit that costs four pounds a day, and a delivery charge paid two hundred times a year without ever being considered once.

**How to use it.** Print a month of transactions and read them like a stranger would. Not to feel guilty. To find the leaks, which are always in the recurring column and never in the memorable purchases. Cancel three things this week. The ones you miss can be resubscribed to in a fortnight, and most will not be.

---

> "Money often costs too much." — Ralph Waldo Emerson

**What it means.** Four words containing the whole trade. Every pound arrives at a price paid in something else: hours, health, attention, the years your children were small. The price is real and it is almost never written down anywhere.

**How to use it.** Convert the money back into life before deciding. The promotion is not twelve thousand pounds. It is twelve thousand pounds minus tax, minus the extra commute, minus the evenings, divided by the hours it takes. Do the division. Sometimes the answer is obviously yes. Sometimes you find you were about to buy something at a terrible rate.

---

> "It is better to have a permanent income than to be fascinating." — Oscar Wilde

**What it means.** Wilde, who had neither for long, being funny about a real thing. There is a romance about precarity that is only ever written by people with a safety net, and the actual experience of it is boring, frightening and bad for the work.

**How to use it.** Be suspicious of the story that struggle is good for you. It is good for a biography. In the living of it, the freelance year with no float produces worse work, not braver work. Build the boring buffer first. Three months of costs in an account you find dull is what makes the interesting choices possible.

---

> "He who buys what he does not need steals from himself." — Swedish proverb

**What it means.** The framing is the useful part. Not wasteful, which is a mild word, but theft, committed by you against a version of you who will need the money later and has no say in the matter.

**How to use it.** Put a delay between wanting and buying, sized to the amount. Anything over a hundred pounds waits a week. Keep a list of the things you nearly bought. Reading it back after a month is the cheapest financial education available, and the number of items you can no longer remember caring about will be most of them.

---

> "Wealth is the slave of a wise man and the master of a fool." — Seneca

**What it means.** Seneca was among the richest men in Rome and knew he was open to the charge of hypocrisy. His defence was that the question is not whether you have it. It is whether you could put it down.

**How to use it.** Run the thought experiment. If your income halved tomorrow, what would you lose? For some people the honest answer is comfort and a great deal of anxiety. For others it is an identity, and that is the answer worth noticing, because an identity made of money has to be defended every year for the rest of your life.

---

> "The safest way to double your money is to fold it over once and put it in your pocket." — Kin Hubbard

**What it means.** A joke about get rich schemes, made in the 1920s, and it has aged perfectly. The people selling the double are making their money from the selling and not from the thing being sold.

**How to use it.** Apply the oldest test there is. If somebody knows a reliable way to double money quickly, ask why they are telling you instead of doing it. This covers the crypto tip, the property seminar, the friend of a friend with an opportunity, and it has never once produced a satisfactory answer.

---

> "Neither a borrower nor a lender be." — William Shakespeare

**What it means.** Polonius is a windbag and this particular line survives because it is right. The rest of the speech explains why: lending loses you the money and the friend at once, and borrowing dulls the edge of your own management of things.

**How to use it.** If you lend money to someone you care about, decide in advance that it is a gift. Say so. That way there is no ledger between you, no awkwardness at Christmas, and no version of the friendship in which you are quietly keeping score. If you cannot afford to give it, you cannot afford to lend it, and saying no now is kinder than resenting them for two years.

---

> "A man is usually more careful of his money than he is of his principles." — Edgar Watson Howe

**What it means.** Howe is pointing at where the real accounting happens. We check the balance daily and the conscience annually, and we notice a small loss of the first far more readily than a large loss of the second.

**How to use it.** Notice where money has quietly become the tiebreaker. The client you would rather not work for. The thing you did not say in the meeting. Everybody has a price and there is nothing shameful in that. What is worth knowing is what yours currently is, because unexamined it tends to be lower than you would like.

---

> "The rich man is not one who is in possession of much, but one who gives much." — St John Chrysostom

**What it means.** A deliberate redefinition, from a preacher who annoyed a great many wealthy people. Wealth measured by flow instead of by holdings, and by what leaves instead of by what sits still.

**How to use it.** Give some away on a standing order, not by decision. Small and automatic beats large and occasional, because the decision is where the reluctance lives. The reported effect on the giver is out of all proportion to the sum, and it is one of the few claims in this book that is easy and cheap to test yourself.

---

> "If you would know the value of money, go and try to borrow some." — Benjamin Franklin

**What it means.** You learn the true position of a thing by trying to obtain it under pressure. Money is polite and abundant until you need it, at which point its actual terms become visible, and the terms are worst for the people who need it most.

**How to use it.** Arrange credit while you do not need it, if you are going to need it at all. The overdraft, the facility, the conversation with the bank. Doing it from a position of comfort costs almost nothing. Doing it in a crisis costs a great deal, and this asymmetry is the single most reliable unfairness in the whole subject.

---

> "Poverty is not a shame, but the being ashamed of it is." — English proverb

**What it means.** The proverb separates the condition from the feeling attached to it, and identifies the feeling as the part that does the damage. Shortage narrows your options. Shame narrows what you are willing to say about it, which narrows them further.

**How to use it.** The exit, and it matters more than anything above. If money is tight, none of this chapter is a moral test you are failing. The leaks are not the problem when the pipe is too small. Take the help, claim the thing you are entitled to, tell one person the truth about the number, and ignore the entire literature of thrift, which was written for people whose problem is appetite and not arithmetic.
