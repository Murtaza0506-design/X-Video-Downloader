# Chapter 13 — Being Alone

There is a particular quality to a Sunday afternoon with nothing in it and nobody due. For some people that is the best hour of the week. For others it is the worst, and the difference between them has almost nothing to do with how much company they have and almost everything to do with whether the empty hour was chosen.

English is careless here. It has one word, alone, doing two opposite jobs. There is the alone you booked, which is quiet and restorative and ends when you want it to. And there is the alone that was done to you, by a move, a bereavement, a marriage ending, a city where you know eleven people and like two. The room looks identical from outside. Inside, they are opposite experiences, and telling a lonely person to enjoy their solitude is like telling somebody drowning to enjoy the swim.

So this chapter closes the section on other people with the subject of their absence, and it tries to hold both halves at once: that time alone is a skill worth having, and that loneliness is a real injury and not a failure of attitude.

The skill part is learnable, and it is being lost. Not because people are weaker than they were, but because it now takes deliberate effort to be alone at all. There is a device within reach that will end the aloneness in under two seconds, at any hour, and it is hard to build a tolerance for something you have never had to sit in for longer than a bus stop.

The injury part does not respond to reframing. It responds to people, and the way back to people is admitting the thing out loud to one of them, which is the hardest sentence in this book to say.

---

> "All of humanity's problems stem from man's inability to sit quietly in a room alone." — Blaise Pascal

**What it means.** Pascal thought most of what we do is avoidance. The busyness, the noise, the constant small errands: not pursuit of anything, but flight from a room with nothing in it. He was writing before electricity. He would have found the modern version unsurprising.

**How to use it.** Try it once, literally. Twenty minutes, no phone, no book, no music, no plan. Most people find it uncomfortable inside four minutes, which is the finding. You are not bad at relaxing. You have never practised being unaccompanied, and the discomfort is the sensation of a skill you do not have yet.

---

> "The greatest thing in the world is to know how to belong to oneself." — Michel de Montaigne

**What it means.** Montaigne means being a full person when unobserved. Not performing, not narrating, not composing the account you will give of the day. Just being there, with your own company, and finding it adequate.

**How to use it.** Notice how much of what you do is aimed at an audience, including the imaginary one. The photograph of the meal nobody asked for. The walk you would not have taken if it could not be mentioned. Try one thing this week that nobody will ever hear about. The discomfort of that tells you how much of you currently belongs to other people.

---

> "A wise man is never less alone than when he is alone." — Cicero

**What it means.** The claim is that solitude has contents. There is a difference between an empty room and an empty hour, and the person who has read, thought and paid attention arrives in the empty hour with company already installed.

**How to use it.** Build the furniture before you need it. The people who cope best with an unexpected quiet weekend are the ones with an interest they can enter alone: an instrument, a garden, a subject, a craft. Start one badly, now, while you are not lonely. It is much harder to acquire while you are.

---

> "Conversation enriches the understanding, but solitude is the school of genius." — Edward Gibbon

**What it means.** Two different faculties, and they run on different fuel. Talking gets you information, correction and the argument you had not thought of. Making anything requires long unbroken stretches in which nobody says anything to you at all.

**How to use it.** Look at where your good ideas arrive. The shower, the walk, the drive, the hour before anyone else is up. Not the meeting. If your week has no unbroken solitary stretch in it, you have removed the conditions for the thing you are being paid to produce, and no amount of collaboration will replace it.

---

> "Be able to be alone. Lose not the advantage of solitude." — Sir Thomas Browne

**What it means.** Note that Browne frames it as an ability, not a preference. Something you can be capable of or not, like swimming, and something that can be lost through disuse.

**How to use it.** Practise in small doses while it costs you nothing. Eat one meal a week by yourself without a screen. Go to the cinema alone. The first time is strange and the third is unremarkable, and what you are building is not a taste for solitude but an immunity to panic in it, which will matter enormously at some point you cannot currently see coming.

---

> "I never found the companion that was so companionable as solitude." — Henry David Thoreau

**What it means.** The strongest possible version of the case, from a man who overstated it and knew he was overstating it. Worth reading as a corrective and not a description, because Thoreau walked into town for dinner more often than the legend allows.

**How to use it.** Take the exaggeration as permission, not instruction. You are allowed to prefer your own company on a given evening. Cancelling because you would rather be alone is a legitimate reason and does not need to be dressed up as illness. Say you are not up to it tonight. Most people will be relieved.

---

> "Whosoever is delighted in solitude is either a wild beast or a god." — Aristotle

**What it means.** The other side, put with force. Aristotle thought humans were social by construction, and that anyone who needed nobody at all was either less than human or more than human, with the odds heavily favouring the first.

**How to use it.** Use it as a check on the story you tell about yourself. I am just not a people person is sometimes true and is more often a description of a wound. If you have found reasons to decline every invitation for six months, the reasons are probably good and the pattern is worth looking at anyway.

---

> "Solitude is fine, but you need someone to tell you that solitude is fine." — Honoré de Balzac

**What it means.** The joke is a serious one. Even the pleasure of being alone seems to require a witness, which suggests the need for other people goes deeper than company and reaches into how we know anything about our own experience.

**How to use it.** When a stretch alone starts to sour, do not solve it with more solitude. One conversation, however slight, resets the whole thing. The shop, the neighbour, the phone call to your mother. Loneliness is unusual among bad states: a small dose of the remedy works, and a large dose is not required.

---

> "In solitude, where we are least alone." — Lord Byron

**What it means.** Byron is claiming that the private hours are when you are most in your own company, and therefore least abandoned. It is a young man's line and it is beautiful and it is only true for people who like themselves that week.

**How to use it.** Test it honestly. Some weeks your own company is good company and solitude is a resource. Other weeks the interior is unkind and being alone with it is the worst available option. Learn which week you are in before you decide whether to accept the invitation, and stop applying a single policy to both.

---

> "The strongest man in the world is he who stands most alone." — Henrik Ibsen

**What it means.** The line closes a play about a man ruined for being right in public. It is about the specific solitude of holding a position nobody around you shares, which is different from having no friends, and considerably harder.

**How to use it.** Distinguish between being alone and being isolated. You can have a full social life and still be alone in what you think about the thing that matters most to you. If that is your situation, find one person who shares it, anywhere, by any means. One is enough to make a position survivable. Nought is not.

---

> "It is not good that the man should be alone." — Genesis 2:18

**What it means.** Placed at the beginning, before anything has gone wrong, which is the point. Aloneness is not framed as a consequence of some failure. It is a defect in the original design, identified and fixed on the spot.

**How to use it.** Take the pressure off yourself. If you are lonely, nothing has gone wrong with you. You are reporting a standard specification. The response is not to become the sort of person who does not need anyone, which is not available. It is to go and get some people, slowly and awkwardly, the way everybody else does.

---

> "One can be instructed in society; one is inspired only in solitude." — Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

**What it means.** A division of labour. Other people are how you find out what is already known. The other thing, the idea that is yours, arrives when nobody is talking, and it cannot be scheduled into a workshop.

**How to use it.** Protect one solitary hour a week the way you protect a meeting. Same slot, defended. Nothing in particular has to happen in it. The point is that you cannot receive anything if the line is permanently engaged, and for most people the line is now engaged from waking until sleep.

---

> "Woe to him that is alone when he falleth; for he hath not another to help him up." — Ecclesiastes 4:10

**What it means.** Coldly practical. Not about companionship or feeling. About the day something goes badly wrong and there is no one whose number you can dial, which is a logistical problem before it is an emotional one.

**How to use it.** Do the audit. Who has your spare key? Who would notice if you did not appear for three days? Who could you ring at three in the morning without it being strange? If those questions have no answers, that is not a mood, it is an exposure, and it is worth fixing with the same seriousness you would apply to an uninsured house.

---

> "Language has created the word loneliness to express the pain of being alone, and the word solitude to express the glory of being alone." — Paul Tillich

**What it means.** The distinction that this chapter rests on, stated plainly. Same room, same absence of people, two completely different experiences, and the variable is whether you chose it.

**How to use it.** Ask which one you are in before applying any advice. If it is solitude, the material about protecting it applies. If it is loneliness, ignore all of that. Reframing does nothing for loneliness and can make it worse, because it adds failure to isolation. What works is contact, repeated, even when you do not feel like it.

---

> "Better alone than in bad company." — Spanish proverb

**What it means.** A limit on everything above. The instruction to go and find people is not an instruction to accept whoever is nearest. Some company is worse than none, and staying in it out of fear of the empty room is a bad trade made by a great many people.

**How to use it.** The exit. If the choice is between a bad evening with people who make you smaller and a quiet one on your own, take the quiet one, and stop treating that as a defeat. You are permitted to have fewer friends than the number that sounds respectable. You are permitted to leave the group chat, skip the reunion, and end the friendship that has been a duty since 2015. Being alone is not the worst outcome available. It is not even close.
