# Chapter 7 — Friendship and Love

Think of somebody you were close to eight years ago and have not spoken to since. Nothing happened. There was no argument, no falling out, no moment you could point at. One of you moved, replies got slower, and the thing stopped, the way a fire stops when nobody puts anything on it.

That is how most friendships end. Not in a row. In an absence of maintenance.

Which is strange, because we talk about friendship as if it were a state and not an activity. You *are* friends, in the way you are tall. But nothing about it is passive. Every friendship you still have is being held up by somebody's small deliberate acts: the message with no purpose, the offer to help with the move, the remembering that the operation was on Tuesday. It is being held up by one of the two of you more than the other, and neither of you has ever discussed which.

The chapters around this one are about friction: listening properly, saying the hard thing, being wronged. They assume the relationship exists. This one is about the part underneath, which is affection and its upkeep, and which gets far less attention because it produces no drama and no anecdotes.

A word on love, since it is in the title and then stays in the background. The lines here treat romantic love and friendship as the same substance at different temperatures, which is what the old writers thought, and close enough to right. A marriage that survives is a friendship with extra terms and conditions.

The practical content of this chapter is small and a little embarrassing: turn up, say the warm thing out loud, and do it before you have a reason to.

---

> "A friend loveth at all times, and a brother is born for adversity." — Proverbs 17:17

**What it means.** Two different offices. The friend is constant, in good weather and bad, without needing a crisis to justify the contact. The second half is about who appears when things collapse, and it is a smaller list than the first.

**How to use it.** Write down the three people you would ring at two in the morning. Then ask when you last contacted any of them for no reason. The at all times part is what earns you the adversity part, and it is paid in advance, in dull ordinary weeks when nothing is wrong.

---

> "Friendship is a single soul dwelling in two bodies." — Aristotle

**What it means.** Aristotle is describing the rarest kind, the friend you do not have to explain yourself to. Not agreement on everything. Something simpler: the absence of translation. You say the half sentence and it lands.

**How to use it.** Count how many people you have this with. Two is a good number. Four is a rich life. If the answer is none, that is not a verdict on you, it is a construction project, and it takes years instead of weeks, which is the only reason most adults do not start.

---

> "Prosperity makes friends, adversity tries them." — Publilius Syrus

**What it means.** Good times attract company that costs nothing to keep. Bad times sort that company into two groups, quickly and without any announcement, and the sorting is rarely a surprise afterwards.

**How to use it.** When your own bad stretch comes, watch who appears and keep the list. Do not use it for revenge. Use it for allocation: your limited energy should go to the people who turned up, and it is remarkable how many of us spend it instead on the ones who did not, out of habit or old loyalty to a version of them that no longer exists.

---

> "We cannot tell the precise moment when friendship is formed. As in filling a vessel drop by drop, there is at last a drop which makes it run over." — James Boswell

**What it means.** There is no threshold event. No conversation converts an acquaintance into a friend. It is accumulation, and it happens below the level where you notice it happening, which is why you can rarely say when it started.

**How to use it.** Stop waiting for the significant occasion. The drops are the point: the lift to the station, the reply to a nothing message, the second time you sit next to each other. Volume matters more than depth here, at least at first. Say yes to the low-stakes invitation, which is where nearly all of it happens.

---

> "A true friend is the greatest of all blessings, and the one we take the least care of all to acquire." — François de La Rochefoucauld

**What it means.** He is pointing at a plain inconsistency. We plan for the career, the mortgage and the pension, and we leave the thing that most determines whether the years are bearable to chance and proximity.

**How to use it.** Treat it as a thing you do, not a thing that happens to you. Put it in the diary if that is what it takes. The first Tuesday of the month, that pub, whoever comes. It feels mechanical written down and it produces something that no amount of good intention ever has.

---

> "The only way to have a friend is to be one." — Ralph Waldo Emerson

**What it means.** A blunt statement about direction. The supply of people waiting to adopt you is zero. The supply of people who would respond warmly if approached first is large and untouched, because everyone is waiting on the same message from somebody else.

**How to use it.** Go first. Send the message you have been half composing for a month. Suggest the specific date instead of the vague meet up soon, which is a phrase designed to be agreed with and never acted on. The awkwardness of going first lasts about eleven seconds and is the entire price.

---

> "A hedge between keeps friendship green." — English proverb

**What it means.** Closeness needs a boundary or it turns into friction. The hedge is not a wall. It marks where you end and they begin, and its whole function is to make the shared ground pleasant instead of contested.

**How to use it.** Apply it to the practical things that quietly kill friendships: money lent, holidays shared, work done as a favour, a spare room offered indefinitely. Agree the terms out loud, early, while everyone is comfortable. It feels cold in the moment. It is the reason you are still speaking in five years.

---

> "Friendship is like money, easier made than kept." — Samuel Butler

**What it means.** Butler is being unsentimental on purpose. The making is the easy half and it is where all the attention goes. The keeping is unglamorous, repetitive and invisible. It is where friendships are won or lost.

**How to use it.** Audit the direction of effort. Take four friendships and ask who initiated the last three contacts. If it was always you, that is worth knowing without bitterness. If it was never you, that is worth knowing with some urgency. Neither answer requires a conversation. Both should change what you do next week.

---

> "Love is not love which alters when it alteration finds." — William Shakespeare

**What it means.** The test is not the good stretch. It is what happens when the other person changes: gets ill, gets difficult, loses the job, becomes less fun to be around for eighteen months. Love that only survives the pleasant version was attachment to the version, not the person.

**How to use it.** Look at who has stayed while you were at your worst. Then look at whether you have done the same for anyone. Both lists are short for everybody, and the second one is the one you have any say over.

---

> "Let there be spaces in your togetherness." — Kahlil Gibran

**What it means.** Written about marriage, and it applies to any close bond. Two people who do everything together stop having anything to bring back to each other, and the closeness starves on the thing that was meant to feed it.

**How to use it.** Keep something of your own. A friendship they are not part of, an evening that is yours, a thing you are learning badly. This is not distance and it is not a warning sign. It is what gives you something to say on Thursday that they have not already heard on Wednesday.

---

> "It is not a lack of love, but a lack of friendship that makes unhappy marriages." — Friedrich Nietzsche

**What it means.** Nietzsche is separating two things that get confused. Love is a feeling and it fluctuates for reasons neither party controls. Friendship is a practice: liking the person, being interested in their day, wanting their company when nothing is at stake. The second is what carries the years.

**How to use it.** Ask a plain question about your closest relationship. If you met them today, with nothing between you, would you want to be their friend? A yes means the foundation is there whatever the weather is doing. A no is worth sitting with, and it is not the same as not loving them.

---

> "Better is a dinner of herbs where love is, than a stalled ox and hatred therewith." — Proverbs 15:17

**What it means.** Vegetables in a warm house beat a feast in a cold one. The proposition is that the company sets the value of the occasion, and the catering is a rounding error, which everybody agrees with and almost nobody organises their life around.

**How to use it.** Lower the bar for having people over. The tidying, the cooking and the arrangements are the reason it has not happened since March. Feed them badly on a weeknight. Nobody has ever gone home disappointed by the food at a house where they felt welcome.

---

> "The comfort of having a friend may be taken away, but not that of having had one." — Seneca

**What it means.** Written to a man in grief. Seneca's argument is that the friendship is not retrospectively cancelled by its ending, and that mourning it as if the good years had been deleted is a second loss on top of the first.

**How to use it.** Applies to death and also to the ordinary drift. The friendship that quietly stopped in 2019 still happened. You were not wrong to have had it and it did not fail. Some are for a stretch of your life and not all of it, and treating those as failures makes people reluctant to start any.

---

> "There is no remedy for love but to love more." — Henry David Thoreau

**What it means.** There is no technique here and Thoreau is not offering one. The only response to caring about people, with all the exposure that involves, is more of it, because the alternative on offer is to care about nobody and that cure is worse than the disease.

**How to use it.** When you have been hurt by someone and the sensible conclusion seems to be to keep everyone further away, notice that the sensible conclusion has a cost that arrives later and lasts longer. Guarding works. That is the problem with it. Do the specific repair with the specific person. Do not lower the temperature on everybody.

---

> "There is a time to embrace, and a time to refrain from embracing." — Ecclesiastes 3:5

**What it means.** Even here there is a limit. Not every relationship is to be kept, and the effort of holding one up long after the other person stopped is not loyalty. It is habit wearing loyalty's coat.

**How to use it.** The exit for this chapter. You are allowed to let one go. The friend who only ever takes, the one you dread seeing, the one who has been unkind for years while you kept explaining it away: you can stop, and you do not owe anyone the confrontation. Reply more slowly. Do not organise the next thing. Most of these end the way they should, quietly, without either of you having to say the sentence out loud.
