# The project support route

The target: a support role inside a delivery function, with certification
picked up alongside it. This is the standard and generally the fastest way
into project management for someone who has done project work without
holding the title. Its advantage over applying straight for PM roles is
that it does not compete on the one thing the CV can't yet show — a track
record of owning delivery.

## What the job is called

The same job is advertised under all of these, and searching for only one
of them misses most of the market:

- Project Support Officer (PSO)
- Project Coordinator
- Project Administrator
- Project Assistant
- Programme Support Officer
- PMO Analyst / PMO Coordinator / PMO Administrator
- Delivery Coordinator
- Business Support Officer *(within a delivery or change function — the
  title alone is ambiguous, so the JD decides)*

Search all of them. `PMO` on its own is a useful keyword because it appears
in postings that never say "project".

## What these roles screen for

Not delivery experience — that is the point of them. In rough order:

1. **Organisation under load.** Evidence of holding many moving parts
   without dropping them. Scheduling, chasing, coordinating across teams.
2. **Reporting and documentation.** Status reports, RAID logs (risks,
   assumptions, issues, dependencies), meeting minutes and actions,
   maintaining a plan someone else owns.
3. **Excel, genuinely.** Named repeatedly in these postings and frequently
   tested. Worth being specific about what you can actually do in it.
4. **Tooling.** MS Project, SharePoint, Jira, Confluence, Smartsheet,
   Asana, Monday, Planview. Name only the ones you've used.
5. **Stakeholder communication.** Dealing with people more senior than you
   without needing authority over them.
6. **Attention to detail.** Usually tested by the application itself — a
   typo in the covering letter is read as evidence.

Note what is *not* on that list: budget ownership, headcount, and
methodology depth. Those are what separate PM postings from support
postings, and treating a support application as a junior PM application
gets the emphasis wrong.

## Certification, and the order to do it in

Support roles rarely require a certification, but having one in progress
answers "are you serious about this or just looking for any office job",
which is the real question behind the screen.

- **PRINCE2 Foundation** — the most-named credential in UK, European and
  Australian project support postings. Foundation alone is enough at this
  level; Practitioner is for people already delivering.
- **APM Project Fundamentals (PFQ)** — UK alternative, cheaper, less
  recognised outside the UK.
- **PSM I (Scrum.org)** — the cheapest credible agile credential and the
  one to hold if the target is tech or digital rather than
  construction/finance/public sector. Sat directly, no mandatory course.
- **PMP (PMI)** — dominant in the US, but requires documented project
  hours you don't yet have. Not a starting point.

**Which one depends on sector**, so it's worth choosing after the CV read
rather than before: PRINCE2 for traditional and public-sector delivery,
PSM I for digital and tech. Doing the wrong one first isn't fatal but it
is a few hundred pounds and several weeks spent on the wrong signal.

Two things worth knowing before paying: **prices and exam formats change**,
so check current cost directly with the accrediting body rather than
through a training reseller's ad; and **many employers will fund it** once
you're in the role, which is a reasonable thing to ask about at offer stage
and a reason not to rush the purchase.

## The realistic path

Project support → project coordinator → junior PM or PM, typically
somewhere between eighteen months and three years depending on the
employer's size and how fast the delivery function is growing. Larger
organisations with a real PMO promote through this ladder deliberately;
small companies often have no next rung at all. Worth checking, at
interview, whether anyone has actually made that step there — the answer
is informative either way.
