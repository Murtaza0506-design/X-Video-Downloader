TEACHING

Beautification, then Detachment

One of the defining teachings of Sidi Hamza, may God be pleased with him, was that the path begins not with struggle, but with sweetness. “Stability of God's love inside the heart, then moving to the practice” — this is at-takhliya ba'da at-tahliya: the heart first tastes the sweetness (tahliya) of remembering God, and only then moves toward purifying itself of its darkness and diseases (takhliya).

In the past, the seeker was expected to purify the heart first, before being permitted to taste the sweetness of dhikr. Sidi Hamza's renewal of the path reversed the order — not because the destination changed, but because the door to it was made wider. As he explained, the retreat of the Sufi is inside the heart (al-khalwat fi al-qalb): there is no need to withdraw from the world to walk this path. A person can remain fully present in their work, their family, their social life — so long as they are, in his words, “happy in their hearts.”

This is why the Tariqa Qadiriyya Boutchichiyya welcomes people at every level of knowledge, Muslim and non-Muslim alike, as long as they wish to purify and enlighten their hearts.

FROM THE HISTORY

Sidi Mounir al-Qadiri al-Boutchichi

Known for decades affectionately as “Moulay Mounir,” Sidi Mounir is the eldest son of Sidi Jamal-ad-Din and the living master of the Tariqa Qadiriyya Boutchichiyya. He grew up within the family of the Qadiri Boutchich, receiving his earliest education from that source, before pursuing academic study in Morocco and France — graduating from Oujda University in Literary Science (1994), completing a law degree at the Dar al-Hassania Institute in Rabat (1996), a Master's in Social Anthropology in Paris (2001), and a doctorate in Islamic Law at the Sorbonne. He is the author of several publications on Sufism, including The Sufi Presence in an Age of Globalisation (2004).

His grandfather, Sidi Hamza, confirmed both verbally and in writing that Sidi Mounir would inherit the spiritual legacy of the path. His father, Sidi Jamal, reaffirmed this entrustment before his passing, saying: “O Allah, I bear witness to You, Your angels, Your prophets, and Your saints that I entrust this secret to my son, Sidi Mounir, my worthy successor...”

PRACTICE

Understanding the Wird

The Wird is your personal daily practice of dhikr — remembrance of God, done alone, usually once in the morning and once in the evening. It’s not something you assemble yourself: each fiqir receives their Wird individually, by idhn (spiritual permission) from the living Shaykh, matched to where they are on the path. As you progress, further elements may be added.

In practice

  • Find a quiet moment, ideally after Fajr and after Maghrib/Isha
  • Face the qibla if you're able to
  • Recite exactly what you were given — no more, no less, unless instructed otherwise
  • Consistency matters more than length — a small Wird done daily outweighs a long one done rarely
  • Haven't received your Wird yet? Speak to one of the brothers after Dhikr, or message the WhatsApp number below

COMPANIONSHIP

Why We Gather

A hadith qudsi tells us: “I am with My servant when he remembers Me — if he remembers Me within himself, I remember him within Myself; and if he remembers Me in a gathering, I remember him in a better gathering.” This is the heart of why we come together each week, rather than only remembering God alone.

Sidi Hamza described the gathering of dhikr as a place where love between disciples and their companions on the path deepens — “Sufism is not a science of papers, but a science of taste.” A typical gathering includes the Wadhifa (a collection of set dhikrs), recitation of Qur'an, a talk, and often Samaa (sung Sufi poetry), before food is shared together. Guests of all backgrounds are always welcome.

WELCOME

New to the Path — What to Expect

First time coming to Dhikr? Here's what to expect:

  • The evening usually runs around two hours
  • It opens with the Wadhifa — a set collection of dhikrs recited together. You don't need to know it by heart; sit, listen, and join in as you're able
  • A short talk follows, then Maghrib prayer
  • The evening closes with refreshments and time to talk — this is as much a part of the gathering as the dhikr itself
  • Come as you are. No prior knowledge of Sufism or Arabic is expected

DID YOU KNOW?

The name “Boutchichi” traces back to an earlier shaykh, Sidi Ali al-Boutchichi, known for serving bou tchich — cracked wheat — to the poor who came to his zawiya. The name has stayed with the order for centuries since.

ASK THE TARIQA

Questions & Answers

What does “idhn” mean, and why does it matter so much?

Idhn is spiritual permission or authorisation — the licence given by a living Shaykh to guide disciples and prescribe dhikr. It's central to how the Boutchichiyya understands its own authenticity: the path isn't transmitted through books or blood alone, but through a chain of idhn passed from teacher to teacher, going back to the Prophet ﷺ. This is why the Wird is given individually rather than published — it comes with idhn appropriate to the person receiving it.

Why is the Wird different for everyone? Why can't I just be given a standard one?

Because the Shaykh prescribes dhikr suited to you — your circumstances, your stage on the path, and your time. Sidi Jamal's own teaching held that the format of the Wird is a means of education, not a fixed text handed out uniformly. As you progress, elements are added. Two disciples at different points on the path may have quite different Wirds, and that's by design.

Why does the tariqa welcome non-Muslims and people “at every level of knowledge”?

This is a deliberate and long-standing feature of the Boutchichiyya's approach, not a recent softening. The path is explicitly open to anyone who sincerely wants to purify and enlighten their heart, whatever their starting point. Sidi Hamza's renewal of the order specifically moved away from requiring the heart to be purified before entry — instead, the sweetness of dhikr comes first, and purification follows through practice and companionship.

What is Samaa, and why do we sing rather than just recite silently?

Samaa is the practice of sung Sufi poetry (qasaid) performed at gatherings. It isn't entertainment — it's understood as a means by which the heart is moved and opened, alongside the more formal dhikr. Moroccan musicologist Abdelfettah Benmoussa has described it as a practice through which “celestial music becomes audible,” combining sacred sound with the divine word.
